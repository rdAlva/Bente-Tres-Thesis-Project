ABSTRACT
Background  This study aimed to determine the effects 
of primary care interventions on healthcare utilisation and 
estimated out-­of-­pocket (OOP) expenses in selected urban, 
rural and remote settings in the Philippines.
Methodology  Context-­specific measures relating to 
expanding healthcare provider networks, augmenting the 
health human workforce and subsidising transportation 
costs were implemented to strengthen primary care 
systems. In this study, two key outcomes were monitored: 
(1) monthly healthcare utilisation measured by the total 
number of outpatient consultations per site and (2) change 
in OOP expenses from baseline to endline within a 1 year 
study period.
Results  All sites had a positive trend in monthly 
outpatient consultations in healthcare utilisation over 
1 year. The remote site had the steepest increase in 
outpatient consultations, with a 401% increase compared 
with the baseline during the peak of consultations at 
month 7. The urban site had a 62% increase in outpatient 
consultations from baseline to month 6, while the rural site 
had a 251% increase from baseline to month 11, which 
corresponded to the peak month in terms of the number 
of outpatient consultations. The rural site had the largest 
decrease in estimated OOP expenses (50.3% reduction, 
95% CI −88 to –13), followed by the remote site (33.2% 
reduction, 95% CI −67,+1) and the urban site (16.0% 
reduction, 95% CI −65,+33).
Conclusion  The rural site showed a significant reduction 
in estimated OOP expenses and an increase in healthcare 
utilisation. The remote site had the steepest increase 
in utilisation, but the reduction in estimated OOP 
expenses was not statistically significant. The urban site 
experienced the lowest increase in utilisation, and the 
smallest reduction in estimated OOP expenses, which was 
also not statistically significant. Implementing primary 
care benefits will necessitate contextualised approaches 
to avoid the inadvertent aggravation of inequities in 
healthcare.
INTRODUCTION
Inequitable access to health services remains 
a significant concern in the Philippines.1 
Social, economic and geographic barriers 
give rise to variations in access to quality 
healthcare services, resulting in meagre 
improvements in health outcomes across 
regions and economic strata.2 Despite the 
implementation of policies to expand the 
coverage of health services, such as the 
National Health Insurance Act of 2013, the 
persistence of barriers to accessing quality 
healthcare services and high financial costs 
discourages healthcare utilisation particularly 
in resource-­limited and GIDAs.3–8
In 2018, the government passed the 
Universal Healthcare (UHC) Law to address 
these gaps and strengthen primary care in the 
country.9 10 To support the implementation of 
the UHC Law, the Philippine Primary Care 
Studies (PPCS) programme piloted primary 
care strengthening interventions in urban,

The primary care interventions are intended to address

al state that reducing out-­of-­pocket (OOP) expenditure

Therefore, these variables should become vital in evalu-

A previous study12 conducted a survey to determine 
the baseline health benefit utilisation and OOP expenses 
in the three primary care settings of the PPCS prior to 
the implementation of the primary care strengthening 
interventions. This is a follow-­up study to describe the 
observed changes in healthcare utilisation and estimated 
OOP expenses pre- and post-­implementation of primary 
care interventions across selected settings in the Phil-
ippines. The specific objectives of the study are (1) to 
determine the effect of primary care interventions on 
healthcare utilisation across three study sites, an urban, 
rural and remote site and (2) to compare the change in 
estimated OOP expenses across the study sites pre- and 
post-­intervention. It borrows Levesque et al’s Conceptual 
Framework on Access to Health, which elucidates supply-­
side and demand-­side factors that impact healthcare 
utilisation.11
Levesque’s conceptualisation of healthcare access 
considers the systemic variations between geographic 
locations, which closely aligns with the healthcare land-
scape in the Philippines. It highlights the study’s interest 
in evaluating interventions to promote the equitable 
implementation of primary care services that consider 
the specific population’s characteristics, resources and 
geographic location.11 From a health equity perspective, 
structural barriers must be addressed so that interven-
tions that intend to improve primary care systems can 
equitably impact communities across urban, rural and 
remote areas.13–16 Assessing and comparing these experi-
ences among urban, rural and remote patients will inform 
how context-­specific barriers impact the full realisation of 
UHC and the promotion of health equity in the country.
METHODOLOGY
Study sites
The urban site was an academic institution in Metro 
Manila, which has a 25-­bed outpatient facility that served 
31 000 university-­based students, faculty, employees and 
residents. The facility was affiliated with diagnostic and 
pharmacy providers within the university system, where 
most referrals were directly fulfilled. Outside of the facility, 
patients also had immediate access to other primary care 
providers within the metropolitan area.
The rural site was a fourth-­class municipality (ie, 
municipalities with an average annual income of 
$60 000–1 00 000) with 35 298 residents across 14 baran-
gays (administrative districts).
The remote site is a geographically isolated and disad-
vantaged area (GIDA), which, in the Philippines, is 
defined as a community with a marginalised population 
that is physically and socio-­economically isolated from 
mainstream society.17 The remote site had 23 932 resi-
dents across 24 barangays. Transportation and internet 
connectivity were challenges for this site.
For both rural and remote sites, there is a rural health 
unit (RHU) and barangay health stations (BHS) for its 
constituents. These public facilities had limited diagnostic 
capacity and pharmaceutical offerings. A physician super-
vised the RHU, while the BHS was staffed by barangay 
health workers (BHWs). Staffing for both sites was 
limited, with only one municipal health officer supported 
by midwives, nurses and BHWs before the study. Patients 
had limited access to other healthcare providers outside 
these public healthcare facilities.
Primary care interventions
PPCS introduced interventions to strengthen the primary 
care system in these three sites. These were implemented 
in the urban site from 2016 to 2017 and, subsequently, in 
the rural and remote areas from 2019 to 2020.
To address health financing problems, the study intro-
duced a primary care benefit package worth PhP 350 per 
capita, with a Php 2000 spending cap per patient over 1 
year. The amount could be used for any condition, that 
is, it was ‘disease-­agnostic’. It was also comprehensive 
because it could be used for consultations, diagnostic 
tests or prescribed medications. Patients were regis-
tered opportunistically at the point of care during their 
first consult. This contrasts with PhilHealth’s existing 
process of prior registration, which is repetitive, resource-­
intensive and inefficient.16
An electronic health record (EHR) system was estab-
lished to unify patient records and centralise medical 
information. Healthcare personnel used the system 
for triaging, referrals, medicine and laboratory request 
printing, medical certificate printing, drug dispensing, 
laboratory results releasing, financial tracking, patient 
census and record management.
Technical training on the most common primary care 
conditions was conducted for the healthcare workers, 
including healthcare professionals (doctors, nurses and 
midwives) and community health workers. They were also 
given free subscriptions and training on how to use a clin-
ical decision support tool (UpToDate) to assist them in 
performing their duties.
Community engagement activities for patients were 
also conducted. Promotional materials such as brochures, 
tarpaulins and social media pages were cascaded to

Study design and overview
Descriptive trend analysis was used for healthcare utili-
sation. Healthcare utilisation was measured as the total 
number of outpatient consultations recorded in the EHR 
system at each site. These consultations included curative 
and preventive visits and were reported monthly.
Pre-­post evaluation for OOP expenses. OOP expenses 
were measured using a healthcare benefit utilisation and 
financial risk protection questionnaire that was pre-­tested 
and implemented in a prior study.17 The follow-­up period 
was October 2016 to September 2017 for the urban site, 
April 2019 to March 2020 for the rural site and May 2019 
to April 2020 for the remote site. The start of the study 
period coincided with initiating and implementing the 
primary care interventions. Due to the urgency of dissem-
inating the results on the impact of primary care inter-
ventions on these two critical outcomes to inform policy, 
we analysed and reported the results within a 1 year study 
period for each site.
Preparation of questionnaire for OOP expenses
A healthcare benefit utilisation and financial risk protec-
tion questionnaire was previously created to assess 
patients’ previous consultations, admissions and expenses 
incurred within a prescribed recall period (online supple-
mental appendix 1).
For this study, we only extracted data on outpatient 
expenses. Participants were asked to estimate how much 
they spent on outpatient care, specifically consultations, 
diagnostic tests and medicines. They were also requested 
to identify their respective financing sources, such as 
OOP, national health insurance (provided through the 
study) or other sources. To minimise recall bias, a recall 
period of 3 months was set.
Sampling and survey distribution for OOP expenses
In the baseline study published in 2024, the sample size 
was calculated based on the two outcomes, namely, utili-
sation and OOP expenses. Since the sample size require-
ments were larger for the utilisation parameter, this was 
the sample size requirement used in the baseline study.12 
Simple random sampling was done for the urban site, 
while stratified sampling was done for the rural and 
remote sites where heads of the households were sampled 
from each barangay (stratum).
In this follow-­up study, total enumeration was done for 
the utilisation outcome. The total number of outpatient 
consultations in each of the research sites was reported. 
For the OOP expense outcome, we invited the same 
respondents who answered the baseline survey to partic-
ipate in the follow-­up study. Respondents who refused to 
participate or could not be contacted were replaced with 
new respondents. The new respondents for replacement 
were matched by employment category and department 
in the urban site, and by sex, age and household site in 
the rural and remote sites.
All endline surveys were done at least 1 year after imple-
menting the interventions. Written and verbal informed 
consent was collected from each respondent before 
survey administration.

Conceptual framework of access to healthcare
In 2013, Levesque et al proposed a conceptual frame-
work for understanding healthcare access.11 The frame-
work highlights five supply-­side determinants namely: (1) 
approachability, which refers to the ability of individuals 
with health needs to recognise the existence of available 
services, access them and benefit from their impact on 
their health; (2) acceptability, which involves cultural and 
social factors that influence people’s willingness to accept 
certain aspects of a service, such as the provider’s sex or 
social group, and the beliefs related to medical systems; 
(3) Availability and Accommodation, which mean that 
health services, including the physical facilities and 
healthcare professionals, can be accessed both physically 
and promptly; (4) Affordability, which indicates people’s 
financial ability to allocate resources and time to access 
appropriate services; and (5) Appropriateness, which 
refers to how well services meet clients’ needs, including 
their timeliness, the thoroughness of health problem 
assessments, the accuracy of treatments and the technical 
and interpersonal quality of the services provided.
Meanwhile, five demand-­side determinants also shape 
healthcare utilisation and access, which complements the 
five supply-­side determinants, respectively. Levesque iden-
tifies the following: (1) the ability to perceive, influenced 
by factors such as health literacy, knowledge about health 
and beliefs related to health and illness; (2) the ability 
to seek, which pertains to personal autonomy and the 
capacity to choose to pursue care; (3) the ability to reach, 
which involves personal mobility, access to transportation, 
occupational flexibility and awareness of health services, 
all of which enable an individual to physically access 
service providers; (4) the ability to pay, which refers to 
the capacity to generate economic resources without 
incurring catastrophic expenses that would jeopardise 
the ability to meet basic necessities; and 5) the ability to 
engage, which involves client’s participation in decision-­
making and treatment choices.
For this study, Levesque’s framework was used to 
examine the influence of study interventions on health-
care utilisation. Supply-­side interventions of the study 
respond to identified site-­specific gaps among the deter-
minants of healthcare utilisation (figure 1). Self-­reported 
OOP expenses are considered a proxy for patients’ ability 
to pay (demand side) in response to the primary care 
benefit package introduced by the study (supply side).

DISCUSSION
The rural site had the highest number of cumulative 
outpatient consultations (37 818 consultations), the 
largest decrease in average estimated outpatient OOP 
expense from baseline to endline (−50.3%). The urban 
site had the smallest increase in outpatient consultations 
and smallest reduction in estimated outpatient OOP 
expense from baseline to endline.
Urban healthcare utilisation and estimated OOP expenses
The urban site had minimal increases in monthly outpa-
tient consultations throughout the study period. Two 
things might explain this lack of response. First, intro-
ducing relatively small subsidies for primary care may not 
significantly alter healthcare utilisation in a community 
with regular access. Second, the baseline utilisation may 
already represent the optimised healthcare use for the 
site.
The urban site has an open and pluralistic system, high-
lighting the multiplicity and availability of stakeholders 
engaged in healthcare services.17 Multiple health service 
providers exist outside the urban site, where patients may 
opt to avail of their health services. This can explain the 
minimal increase in the number of outpatient consulta-
tions for the site. Moreover, the urban site, relative to its 
rural and remote counterparts, may already have better 
health outcomes due to better access to health services.

Vlahov et al explore the urban health ‘advantage’ phenom-
enon and argue that health indicators are better in urban 
areas due to socioeconomic heterogeneity, higher levels 
of social support and higher access to necessities of life.18 
This likely reduces the need for patients to visit the urban 
site, compared with rural and remote sites. Grounded on 
the health access framework by Levesque et al, we see that 
the urban healthcare system, with present and substan-
tial resources under the Availability and Accommodation 
domain, led to stability in healthcare utilisation for the 
urban site.
Varied results were noted for the average estimated 
OOP expense of patients in the urban site. While the 
average estimated OOP expenses reduced by 16.0% from 
baseline to year 1, the percentage of estimated OOP 
expense at the end of year 1 remains high at 53.7%. We 
can attribute the reduction in estimated OOP expenses 
to the Php 2000 benefit package provided by the 
programme to increase patients’ ability to pay.11 However, 
it is still likely that some patients did not maximise their 
benefits at the urban site clinic and instead acquired care 
from other service providers not covered by the benefit 
package. Similarly, this can be explained by the open and 
pluralistic system of the urban site, where patients are not 
obliged to seek care from one provider alone.
Rural healthcare utilisation and estimated OOP expenses
The rural site had a steep increase in monthly outpatient 
consultations, demonstrating higher utilisation. This site 
also had a large reduction in estimated OOP expenses. 
Considering Levesque et al’s health access framework, 
we may relate this to the supply-­side interventions of the 
study targeting the areas of Approachability, Availability 
and Accommodation, and Affordability.
Approachability towards the healthcare site was 
improved using promotional activities, such as the 
cascade of flyers and town hall meetings. The structure 
of these activities included presenting the basic concepts 
surrounding primary care and emphasising its impor-
tance in improving patient health. These methods likely 
encouraged patients to visit the clinic more. Gupta et al 
describe the importance of health education materials, 
mainly through social marketing, to improve patient 
behaviour in seeking primary care.19 Moreover, addi-
tional RHU physicians were recruited in partnership 
with the local government unit. The support of the local 
government was crucial in ensuring that the supply of 
healthcare providers was sufficient to meet healthcare 
demands.16 An EHR system was also implemented, opti-
mising the time spent by healthcare staff serving patients 
instead of managing patient records.
Improved average estimated OOP expense was also 
observed in the rural site between baseline and year 1, 
where the average estimated OOP expense was reduced 
by 50.3%. The percentage of estimated OOP expense at 
the rural site was the lowest across all sites at 18.3%. Like 
the urban site, these improvements can be attributed 
to providing a Php 2000 benefit package. Patients likely 
maximised this benefit, thus decreasing their OOP 
expenses. Furthermore, the study expanded the health-
care provider network of the rural site by partnering with 
laboratory and diagnostic providers. With additional 
providers in the system, patients’ needs from these facil-
ities were likely covered by the benefit package, thus 
decreasing their OOP expenses. These interventions can 
explain the improvement in OOP expense for health 
services.11
Remote healthcare utilisation and estimated OOP expenses
The remote site had fluctuating utilisation, as shown 
in their monthly outpatient consultations. The varying 
levels of health utilisation can be related to similar study 
interventions identified at the rural site. These initiatives 
targeted the areas of Approachability, Availability and 
Accommodation, and Affordability. However, the impact 
on the remote site is not as large as observed on the rural 
site. The existing structural barriers can largely explain 
this difference for the remote site.
The EHR system was not fully optimised due to poor 
internet connection. An effective EHR system is expected 
to support service delivery networks by (1) centralising 
patient information, (2) making comprehensive data

the provincial government installed additional towers

pality under one network.16 However, this was not feasible 
in the remote site due to its dense and mountainous land-
scape.22 As a result, the remote site experienced consid-

A 33.2% decrease in the average estimated OOP 
expense was noted between baseline and endline. Similar 
to the rural site, these improvements can be attributed 
to the provision of the Php 2000 benefit package that 
decreased the OOP expenses of patients. The healthcare 
provider network of the remote site was also expanded 
through partnerships with laboratory and diagnostic 
providers, allowing patients’ needs from these facilities to 
be covered by the benefit package.
However, OOP expenses remained high at endline 
similar to the urban site. It is important to contextu-
alise that healthcare spending in the remote site was 
low at baseline compared with the rural and urban sites. 
Therefore, health services were likely underused at the 
beginning, and patients were more likely to have more 
severe or complicated conditions. Chen et al highlighted 
geographical disparities in the utilisation of preventive 
care services, which can explain this underutilisation.24 
When patients were encouraged to consult more, health 
expenditures from outpatient consultations, laboratory 
services and medicines likely increased. The expansion 
of healthcare provider networks could have been the cost 
driver, such that some expenses from laboratories and 
pharmacies probably exceeded patient benefits.
Implications for UHC implementation
Equitable implementation of UHC in the Philippines 
should consider the variations in health system readiness 
across site-­specific contexts. The contexts of each site are 
different, where the most disadvantaged remote site was 
already predisposed to equity concerns, specifically poor 
infrastructure.
The existing infrastructure impacts primary care utilisa-
tion across sites. Infrastructure elements, such as internet 
connectivity and geographical characteristics, affect the 
proliferation of primary care access points. These issues 
are most pressing in the remote site, where primary care 
options remained limited at baseline and endline because 
of the inefficient implementation of the EHR due to poor 
internet connectivity. Meanwhile, the rural site resolved 
this and activated more access points through community 
health stations. On the other hand, the urban site already 
had multiple primary care access points outside the study 
setting where patients could have otherwise received care.
Returning to Levesque et al’s framework for health-
care access,11 existing infrastructure problems at the 
remote site limited the impact of the study’s inter-
ventions under the Availability and Accommodation 
domain. With this domain not fully implemented, it is 
also likely that the effect of the Affordability domain, 
which follows it, remains unmet. Each domain of the 
framework must be addressed to achieve our vision for 
optimised healthcare utilisation and improved health-
care access. This calls for additional funding to ensure 
health system readiness across different geographic 
regions to adopt primary care and universal health 
coverage.
Limitations
The scope of this study is limited to outpatient consulta-
tions in RHUs for rural and remote sites and a primary 
care clinic in an urban site. Future studies may include 
data from community health stations, diagnostic labo-
ratories and pharmacies. Another limitation is that 
not all consultation data points can be considered 
independent, as several individuals may likely have 
consulted several times during the study period. Clus-
tering within households in the rural and remote may 
also have affected the number of consultations, since 
the observation units for this variable are individuals, 
while the sampling units are households. Moreover, 
the OOP expenses are estimated by patients through 
survey data. Recall bias might occur for some of these 
metrics. The potential impact of socio-­demographic 
factors (eg, ethnicity, income level, family size) on the 
resulting data set remains outside the study’s scope. 
Future studies may want to explore external factors 
that may ultimately contribute to patients’ care utili-
sation. Lastly, the COVID-­19 pandemic was declared 
in March 2020, which likely affected healthcare utili-
sation and OOP expenses towards the tail end of the 
first year of primary care implementation for the rural 
and remote sites.
CONCLUSION
This study measured healthcare utilisation and esti-
mated OOP expenses across urban, rural and remote 
settings in the Philippines after introducing primary 
care interventions. The rural site showed the most 
promising results at the end of the study period for 
healthcare utilisation and estimated OOP expenses. 
In contrast, the urban site had a minimal increase in 
utilisation. This may be due to an open and plural-
istic healthcare system, which provided several other 
options for healthcare. Due to existing infrastructure 
problems, the remote site could not fully optimise the 
interventions provided despite the provision of equal 
funds for primary care services. Such pre-­existing 
problems in access may require the allocation of 
greater funds for disadvantaged areas and may neces-
sitate contextualised approaches to avoid the inad-
vertent aggravation of inequities in healthcare access 
and utilisation.
