A B S T R A C T
Economic impact estimates of the initial lockdowns due to the COVID-19 pandemic showed a significant
reduction in economic activities globally. However, the succeeding impacts and their spatiotemporal distribution
within countries remain unknown. Studies showed that nighttime light data (NTL) has effectively revealed the
spatiotemporal dimensions of the economic effects of COVID-19. Thus, this study used NTL data to determine the
medium-term regional monthly economic impacts of the pandemic in the Philippines in terms of the Economic
Activity Reduction (EAR) index. We generated a spatial error model, regressing pre-pandemic NTL on mean
temperature, maximum rainfall, and built-up area. This model explained 81.6% of the pre-pandemic NTL and
was used to estimate the counterfactual NTL. We subtracted the actual from the counterfactual to compute the
EAR. Then, the EAR was regressed on regional factors to determine which ones influence the impacts. Results
showed uneven distribution of EAR across space and time. The EAR was generally higher in urban regions than in
rural ones. Overall, more regions in the south had higher EAR. Temporally, the EAR showed a dynamic pattern,
increasing in less urban regions and decreasing in highly urbanized regions. Regional analysis showed that ur-
banization level, population density, and poverty incidence had a significant positive relationship with the EAR.
Beyond the immediate impacts, NTL effectively revealed spatiotemporal dimensions of the economic effects of a
long-term global hazard.
1. Introduction
The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) spread rapidly across
countries and contracted the global economy by approximately −3.4 %
in 2020 (World Bank Group, 2022). Immediate macroeconomic losses
due to COVID-19 were estimated for various countries using conven-
tional methods (Kim et al. 2022; Betho et al. 2021; Santos et al. 2022).
However, these data and methods face limitations in determining the
subnational level dynamic impacts across years, delaying full recovery
in local communities.
Beyond the country-level impacts, researchers pointed out the
importance of subnational impact estimates for a proportionate resource
distribution (Lopez Ochoa et al. 2021) and high-frequency data to
monitor effects for timely policy implementation (Fezzi and Fanghella,
2020). In addressing this, a growing number of studies have been using
high-resolution and high-frequency datasets to estimate near-real-time
subnational
impacts.
For
instance,
researchers
used
electronic
payments (Carvalho et al. 2021), electricity consumption (Beyer et al.
2021; Fezzi and Fanghella, 2020), mobility data (Yang et al. 2022), and
night-time light (NTL) data (Alahmadi et al. 2021, 2023; Beyer et al.
2021; Xu et al. 2021) to analyze subnational high-frequency economic
impacts.
Among these datasets, NTL is frequently used in determining COVID-
19 impacts. NTL constitutes satellite data that records nocturnal light
brightness across the earth’s surface and have been used in a broad
range of cases, from socioeconomic to meteorological, and environ-
mental applications (Li et al. 2017; Miller et al. 2013). Among its ap-
plications, one critical niche for NTL is mapping economic variables,
such as the GDP (Chen and Nordhaus, 2019; Zhao et al. 2017). Earlier
studies (Doll et al. 2000; Henderson et al. 2012) statistically established
the relationship of NTL with economic variables, paving the way for its
wider acceptance as a tool for mapping economic activities. Subsequent
papers supported these earlier findings using NTL for mapping spatial
patterns of economic variables (Wu et al. 2013; Mellander et al. 2015).

Recent papers capitalized on its unique capacity for detecting human
activities and furthered its function to estimate losses from natural di-
sasters and monitor recovery (Jia et al. 2023; Qiang et al. 2020). The
advantages of NTL include fine spatial details, global coverage, exten-
sive temporal record (Jia et al. 2023), free access, minimum release
delay, objective physical measurement (Schippers and Botzen, 2023),
and frequent and regular data acquisition.
Continuous monitoring of regional economic impacts from COVID-
19 would be crucial to navigating recovery paths. COVID-19 case
surges resulted in dynamic responses and impacts lasting beyond initial
lockdowns. With the economic impacts predicted to last beyond this
current generation (Jorda et al. 2020), region-specific measures are
necessary for communities to recover. However, medium-term local
dynamic impacts and the regional factors shaping them have been rarely
studied as most studies focused on immediate consequences.
Hence, this study estimates the medium-term spatiotemporal distri-
bution of economic activity reduction (EAR) during the COVID-19
pandemic in the Philippines using regional monthly NTL. Specifically,
it addresses three research questions:
1) Is EAR due to COVID-19 evenly distributed across all regions?
2) If not, how had EAR temporally spread across regions?
3) What types of regional attributes influence EAR?
We propose an approach that allows subnational impact mapping
and monthly monitoring covering three years. This approach entails a
model estimating the pre-pandemic NTL and using this model to esti-
mate the counterfactual NTL. The difference between this counterfactual
and actual NTL gives the EAR. Then, we identified the regional factors
that significantly influence regional EARs. Through this method, we can
estimate the medium-term regional monthly distribution of the impacts
and understand the influencing factors.
2. Literature Review
2.1. COVID-19 in the Philippines
The Philippines implemented one of the strictest and longest COVID-
19 lockdowns worldwide (Hapal, 2021). This country registered the
highest economic losses among Southeast Asian countries in 2020
(Zhang et al. 2022; World Bank Group, 2022). Starting March 12, 2020,
Metro Manila was under a partial lockdown. By March 16, 2020, the
entire Luzon Island was under Enhanced Community Quarantine,
restricting gatherings and closing public transportation, schools, and
non-essential businesses (Amit et al. 2021). In April 2020, over half of
the population was subject to these measures (Del Castillo et al. 2024).
Around mid-May 2020, the government began easing restrictions, with
exceptions for high-risk areas. However, different quarantine levels
were implemented across local government units as cases surged in the
following months. The impacts of these case surges and interventions
were unknown as most studies focused on the initial impacts.
Studies that have assessed COVID-19 economic losses in the
Philippines used economic data from statistics and surveys. A linear
probability model was employed to ascertain the likelihood of short-
term COVID-19 business impacts based on survey data on MSMEs
(Shinozaki and Rao 2021) noting greater probability of economic
reduction in urban regions than in rural ones. Another study applied the
persistent inoperability input–output method and identified the worst-
hit sectors (Yu et al. 2020). Others derived a compartmentalized dy-
namic transmission model to estimate the economic and health tradeoffs
for different scenarios in selected regions (de Lara-Tuprio et al. 2022). A
recent study estimated a total loss amounting to 2.9 trillion PhP in 2020
and 3.2 trillion PhP in 2021 due to COVID-19 in the Philippines (Del
Castillo et al. 2024). However, the regional monthly economic impacts
of COVID-19 across time remain unknown.
2.2. COVID-19 economic impact estimation methods
Internationally, COVID-19 impacts have been estimated in various
countries using traditional methods such as the social accounting matrix
for Mozambique (Betho et al. 2021), computable general equilibrium in
South Korea (Kim et al. 2022), and input–output models in the
Philippines (Santos et al. 2022). With the need for near-real-time impact
estimation, other researchers used high-frequency datasets. Carvalho
et al. (2021) used electronic payment data to analyze the regional eco-
nomic impacts of COVID-19 in Portugal, finding central and densely
populated municipalities suffering a worse economic decline. Fezzi and
Fanghella (2020) used daily electricity consumption data to estimate the
immediate daily economic impacts of COVID-19 in Italy. Yang et al.
(2022) used daily mobility data for losses and recovery rate assessment
during the initial lockdown for cities in China.
Other studies revealed the spatial distribution of impacts using NTL,
as an established proxy for economic activities given the lack of local
high-frequency economic data. Most NTL-based studies employed a
before-and-after comparison method (Ghosh et al. 2020; Beyer et al.
2021; Alahmadi et al. 2021, 2023; Xu et al. 2021). Alahmadi et al.
(2021) observed pandemic-related NTL brightness reduction in parts of
Saudi Arabia after comparing 2019 and 2020 NTL images. Beyer et al.
(2021) mapped out the impacts of COVID-19 lockdowns per district in
India from April to August 2020 using VIIRS data. Xu et al. (2021)
compared spatial and temporal changes of NTL brightness in 20 selected
megacities using NASA’s Black Marble NTL, identifying Mumbai, Dhaka,
and Manila as the megacities with the highest nighttime brightness
reduction. Other studies developed regression models (Roberts, 2021;
Lin and Rybnikova, 2023) and used machine learning methods
(Dasgupta, 2022). Lin and Rybnikova (2023) provided evidence that
NTL can be used in estimating GDP under the impact of COVID-19 and
showed the different recovery rates across U.S. states. While numerous
studies have proven the utility of NTL data and other high-resolution/
high-frequency datasets, most of them focused on the immediate ef-
fects covering the first few months after the lockdown.
3. Materials and methods
3.1. Study Site
The Philippines is an archipelagic country in South East Asia (Fig. 1).
It has three main island groups: Luzon, Visayas, and Mindanao, and four
administrative division levels: regional, provincial, municipal or city,
and barangay. These administrative divisions demarcate resources,
services, and policies. National services are organized in the regional
units, while the barangays govern local communities.
During the COVID-19 pandemic, the National Government initially
enforced lockdowns. Later, each local administrative unit implemented
additional measures. These units vary in demographic, socioeconomic,
and geopolitical characteristics, which could affect how the economic
impacts of the pandemic spread. Understanding these differences is
essential for developing region-specific recovery strategies. One key
factor influencing economic impact is urbanization level, defined as the
proportion of the urban population relative to the total population
within an administrative unit (Philippine Statistics Authority, 2020).
In this study, we generated the model using provincial-level datasets
to meet the minimum data point requirements for the spatial regression
method. We aggregated the estimated provincial impacts at the regional
level for interpretability. We ranked and labeled the regions according
to their urbanization level, with R1 representing the most urbanized and
R17 the least urbanized. This ranking helps highlight differences in
urban-rural regional impacts and simplifies interpretation.

3.3. Methods
3.3.1. Framework
Our approach involves developing a model estimating the pre-
pandemic NTL. Using this model, we calculated a counterfactual
NTL—what the NTL would have been if the pandemic had not occurred.
This counterfactual becomes the baseline for calculating the economic
impact, represented by the EAR index. We then used regression analysis
to identify the regional factors influencing the EAR. The overall process
is illustrated in Fig. 5.

3.3.2. Data exploration and preparation
We began by analyzing the distribution of the dataset values. The
histograms (Fig. 5) revealed a skewed data distribution. To address this,
we applied the natural logarithm to the input datasets to normalize the
distribution. Subsequently, we separated the data into the pre-pandemic
and pandemic subsets, with March 2020 marking the start of the
pandemic.
3.3.3. NTL model development
For model development, we first tested the NTL data for spatial
autocorrelation using Moran’s I to avoid biased estimates. The
computed Moran’s I was 0.928 (p-value < 0.01), indicating significant
positive spatial autocorrelation. This means that spatial regression
models had to be used instead of OLS to obtain unbiased results. We then
performed Lagrange multiplier (LM) tests to determine which spatial
regression model to use. The results showed significant spatial depen-
dence in both the error and the dependent variable so we performed the
robust LM. Both had significant p-values but the spatial error model
(SEM) had a higher test statistic (Rao score = 25,888; p-value= <0.000)
than the spatial lag model (Rao score = 8.36; p-value < 0.004). This
means that the residual has greater spatial dependence than the
dependent variable.
Based on this, we developed the SEM model where the error term ε of
the OLS model was multiplied by the spatial weight matrix W and an
autoregressive coefficient λ, thus, isolating an identically and indepen-
dently distributed error term u. Then, the pre-pandemic NTL, lnNTL, was
modeled based on the average temperature lnmeantemp, maximum
rainfall lnmaxrainfall, and buil-up area lnbuiltarea. Additionally, we

We then regressed the EAR on the following factors to determine
which factor(s) influence the EAR as represented in Equation (7).

In this equation, lncaseperHr represents the natural logarithm of the
ratio of COVID-19 cases per hospital. This ratio was selected because
economic impacts are influenced not only by the proportion of the
infected population but also by the capacity of healthcare institutions to
accommodate patients requiring medical care. lnLIr refers to the natural
logarithm of the lockdown index (Equation (1)). Other regional factors,
such as urbanization level Urblev, population density Popden, population
growth rate Popgr, proportion of regional movers Regmov, poverty
incidence Povinc, and literacy rate Litrate, could also attenuate or
magnify the economic impacts. Previous studies have linked the eco-
nomic impacts to urbanization level (Carvalho et al. 2021, Hamman
et al. 2023) and population density (Khan et al. 2023). Furthermore,
regional movers could capture the changes in economic activity owing
to the urban exodus that occurred during the lockdown period, espe-
cially under the Balik Probinsya or Return to Province program (UPPI
and DRDF, 2020). Regions with more regional movers, wealth, and
higher education levels can also affect the EAR distribution (Carvalho
et al. 2021).

4.1. Pre-pandemic NTL model
Table 3 presents the results of spatial regression models generated to
estimate the pre-pandemic provincial monthly NTL. As shown, we
generated three models with varying sets of explanatory variables.
Model 1, based on mean temperature, maximum rainfall, built-up areas,
and holidays, all variables significant at 1 %, explains 72 % of the pre-
pandemic NTL. The year was added in Model 2, effectively increasing
the R2 value to 0.816. Adding the regional dummy variables in Model 3
did not improve the R2 but slightly lowered the Akaike Information
Criterion (AIC), which measures the model quality based on its fit and
complexity. Having the lowest AIC, model 3 estimated the counterfac-
tual NTL.
4.2. Estimated NTL
We estimated the NTL per province using Model 3. For ease of
interpretation, we aggregated the provincial actual and estimated NTL
to the regional level. Fig. 6 plots the estimated monthly regional NTL in
blue and the actual NTL in black. As shown, the estimated NTL exhibits
an ascending trend across the years. It also follows a fluctuating pattern,
reflecting seasons controlled by the temperature and rainfall datasets.
Focusing on the pre-pandemic period, the estimated NTL generally fol-
lowed the actual NTL, suggesting that the model can capture the NTL
values. However, the modeled values could not capture steep momen-
tary drops possibly due to disasters or power outages and temporary
peaks of the actual NTL which occurred in regions surrounding Marawi
city during the Marawi Siege.
Regarding the pandemic period, the gaps between the actual and
counterfactual NTL represent the economic impacts. During this period,
two patterns emerged. The first pattern showed that counterfactual
values of R1 to R10 were higher than the actual NTL, indicating eco-
nomic activity reduction. The second pattern can be observed from R11
to R17 where the actual NTL was higher than the counterfactual, rep-
resenting economic activity gain.

5. Discussions
The results revealed that EARs varied across space and time,
depending on certain regional factors. EARs were generally higher in
urban areas than in rural ones, aligning with the findings of other re-
searchers (Carvalho et al. 2021; Hamman et al. 2023). Our regional
analysis confirmed this, with lnurblev consistently significant across all
models (Table 4). The study also found that while Luzon experienced a
substantial initial economic impact following the Luzon lockdowns, the
overall medium-term EAR was higher in Mindanao. EARs shifted over
time, with peaks moving from the most urbanized regions in the first
year to less urbanized ones in the second and third years. EAR peaks also
migrated from Luzon to Mindanao. Additionally, the three-year analysis
revealed the recovery status of the regions. Key factors linked to EAR
included urbanization level, poverty incidence, and number of cases per
hospital.
This study demonstrated a counterfactual-based approach to esti-
mating economic impacts, enabling the calculation of medium-term
EAR. This method complements the widely-used change detection
technique of analyzing NTL datasets. The change detection method
measures the difference between pre-and post-event images (Ghosh
et al. 2020; Beyer et al. 2021; Alahmadi et al. 2023; Xu et al. 2021) and is
appropriate for immediate impact assessment. However, it may under-
estimate long-term effects, as it does not account for the trend. In
contrast, our counterfactual-based approach allows for long-term
impact estimation and recovery tracking. Some studies (Lin and Ryb-
nikova, 2023) have used pre-pandemic levels as the recovery point.
Alternatively, the counterfactual value can serve as the recovery point in
estimating the recovery of losses rather than temporal changes. In the
counterfactual approach, recovery is achieved when the actual EAR
reaches the counterfactual value (EAR = 0). Although the results show
that some regions are moving toward recovery, many still experience
positive EAR, and regions with negative EAR show a worsening trend.
Hence, continuous monitoring of EAR is crucial for authorities to iden-
tify regions in need of further support and to allocate resources effec-
tively for recovery.
Medium-term impacts revealed that the most economically stricken
regions due to the pandemic were not always the most urbanized re-
gions, which is not covered by studies focusing on the initial lockdown
periods (Carvalho et al., 2021; Shinozaki and Rao, 2021). As COVID-19
was a dynamic hazard lasting for several years, the severity of economic
impacts fluctuated. This finding implies the need for continuous impact
monitoring. Highly urbanized regions often incur more absolute eco-
nomic loss in the short run than urbanizing regions. Although the ab-
solute losses are lower for urbanizing regions, the impacts to them could
be more devastating in the long run as the losses constitute a significant
portion of their economy. Neglecting the longer-term impacts could
leave these urbanizing regions without support, leading to further
decline of their economies, making poorer regions poorer, as implied by
poverty incidence having significant positive relationship with EAR in
2022.
We acknowledge several limitations of this study. First, the method
requires more data and computation, making it more time-consuming
than the simpler change detection approach. Second, the results only
reflect economic impacts from NTL-emitting sectors, thus, agricultural
impacts are not captured. Third, smaller events, unrelated to the
pandemic, may influence the EAR. For example, sudden drops in NTL
could be caused by weather disturbances, reduced activity, or power
outages. In contrast, the NTL peaks in Mindanao in 2017 might be linked
to population displacement during the Marawi Siege. These smaller
events were not factored into the pre-pandemic model. Finally, the
method assumes no other hazards occurred during the pandemic,
though some natural disasters did take place. Nevertheless, these events
were short-lived and affected only a few regions, meaning this method is
not suited for estimating the impacts of multiple hazards. This implies
that EARs were not purely the effect of COVID-19 alone but could also
include effects of minor events. Future research could address these
limitations by accounting for multiple hazards and integrating multi-
spectral images to capture agricultural losses.
6. Conclusions
The COVID-19 pandemic resulted in global economic activity
changes observable from space through NTL data. This study estimated
the subnational economic activity reductions at monthly intervals for
three years and identified regional factors influencing the changes. We
generated a model estimating the pre-pandemic NTL based on explan-
atory variables including mean temperature, maximum rainfall, built-up
area, holidays, trend, and regional dummy variables. The model was
able to explain over 81.6 % of the pre-pandemic NTL. Based on the
regression model results, we estimated the counterfactual NTL. The pre-
pandemic regional estimated NTL closely followed the actual NTL. The
regional monthly EARs were then computed. Finally, the regional fac-
tors influencing the EARs were identified through regression.
This study’s contribution centers on estimating the regional medium-
term EAR due to the COVID-19 pandemic using NTL. Our results
revealed that EAR was higher in urban regions than in rural regions. EAR
was not static over time as shown by the increased impact in less urban
and southern regions in the second and third years. The regional analysis
showed that urbanization level, COVID-19 cases per hospital, and
poverty incidence had a significant positive relationship with EARs. This
study showed that impacts differ across space and fluctuate across time
depending on regional characteristics. It is important to understand
these spatiotemporal dimensions and continuously monitor their long-
term impacts. In this regard, NTL holds great potential in understand-
ing these dimensions beyond the immediate impacts.
