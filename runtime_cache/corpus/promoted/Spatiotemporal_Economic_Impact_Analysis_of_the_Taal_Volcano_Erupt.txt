ABSTRACT 
Analyzing the spatiotemporal dimension of the economic impacts 
of disasters is critical for providing timely and proportionate sup­
port. However, traditional economic impact measures often lack 
spatiotemporal details. Hence, we estimated the daily electricity 
consumption (EC) loss using high-frequency EC data and analyzed 
temporal dimensions of the immediate impacts of Taal Volcano’s 
eruption on January 12, 2020. Subsequently, we computed the 
nighttime light (NTL) change using high-resolution NTL data to 
analyze the spatial distribution of these impacts. The temporal 
analysis revealed two EC loss peaks. The first peak coincided with 
the power outage and decreased energy demand, followed by a 
brief recovery after power restoration. The second peak emerged 
upon the influx of the affected population, evacuees, earthquake 
occurrences, and road closures. Major assistance distribution 
spurred recovery towards the end of the analysis period. The spa­
tial analysis showed significant NTL reductions where ashfall inter­
sected built-up areas. The municipalities with the worst NTL 
reduction included Talisay, Laurel, Santa Teresita, Agoncillo, 
Lemery, San Nicolas, Taal, and Balete. This study demonstrates an 
alternative method that uses high-frequency EC to reveal daily 
temporal fluctuations of immediate non-agricultural economic 
impacts and map out the areas worst affected by a volcanic 
eruption.

1. Introduction
Spatiotemporal dimensions are critical for the proportionate and timely distribution 
of resources in disaster-affected areas. Assessments of volcanic eruption impacts, 
however prompt, often lack these spatiotemporal dimensions. Spatiotemporally blind

economic impact assessment may stem from a lack of data. Typical measures like 
GDP data are available at the national and quarterly scale, often aggregating the 
impacts across large areas. Further, immediate on-site data collection can be challeng­
ing due to safety reasons. Consequently, temporally blind assessments could delay a 
response and conceal recovery trends, while spatially blind estimates lead to possible 
mismatched and biased resource allocation (Brucal et al. 2020; Malik 2022).
A possible solution to this lack of data is to use alternative high-frequency electri­
city consumption (EC) and high-resolution nighttime light (NTL) datasets. Both data­
sets exhibit a strong relationship with GDP, as shown in the regression model results 
(Online Appendix A) and previous studies (Doll et al. 2000; Ghosh et al. 2010; 
Henderson et al. 2012; Al-mulali and Mohammed 2015; Faisal et al. 2017; Khan and 
Kong 2020; Chen et al. 2022).
Several studies have used EC data to assess economic impacts of the COVID-19 
pandemic (Fezzi and Fanghella 2020; Beyer et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2021; Ai et al. 
2022; Del Castillo et al. 2024). EC data also showed a decline following a volcanic 
eruption (Wilson et al. 2012). However, no research has yet applied this data to esti­
mate the impacts of local disasters.
NTL data have been used to map economic impacts of local disasters such as 
typhoons, earthquakes, and floods (Qiang et al. 2020; Tveit et al. 2022; Jia et al. 2023; 
Zhou et al. 2024). Although NTL data’s continuous acquisition is ideal for long-term 
impact analysis of volcanic eruptions, no studies have yet utilized NTL to map the 
impacts of volcanic eruptions.
This study uses EC and NTL data to analyze spatiotemporal distributions of the eco­
nomic impacts of the Taal Volcano eruption on January 12, 2020. It adopts the following 
research questions: (1) How were the economic impacts based on EC data distributed 
across time, and (2) which municipalities were worst affected, based on NTL data?
To answer the first research question, we estimated the daily economic impacts 
using daily EC data. First, we generated a regression model of pre-eruption daily EC 
to estimate a counterfactual EC, then subtracted the actual EC from it to compute 
the daily EC loss (ECL) as a measure of economic impact. Assuming that actual EC 
data were only affected by the eruption, the analysis covered 18 days, ending on 
January 30, 2020, when COVID-19 was first detected in the Philippines. Extending 
the period could introduce pandemic-related influences beyond the study’s scope. For 
the second research question, NTL was used to identify the most affected municipal­
ities by computing the NTL change (NTLC) as the difference between the average 
NTL of previous years and the eruption month’s NTL.
This study presents a novel approach to estimating the immediate impacts of vol­
canic eruptions. To our knowledge, no study has used EC data to assess the economic 
effects of natural disasters, making this the first to apply EC data for short-term 
impact analysis after a local disaster. Additionally, our analysis using NTL data identi­
fies the most affected municipalities and is the first to use NTL data to map the 
impacts of a volcanic eruption. While EC and NTL data were analyzed separately 
due to resolution differences, we propose a method for remotely assessing the daily 
and municipal distribution of economic impacts, which can be applied even while sig­
nificant hazards persist onsite.

2. The 2020 eruption of the Taal Volcano
The Taal Volcano, located in the Southern Luzon Region of the Philippines 
(Figure 1), erupted phreatomagmatically in 2020, affecting an estimated 8,600 km2 
with ashfall (Balangue-Tarriela et al. 2022) and impacting over 700,000 people 
(NDRRMC 2020). The eruption caused significant damage to agriculture, aquaculture, 
residences, and infrastructure, disrupted transportation, education, work, air travel, 
and affected the air quality (Del Castillo et al. 2020; Lim et al. 2022; Santos et al. 
2023). It resulted in damage costing over 8.4 billion PhP (NEDA IVA, 2020). Weaker 
explosions continued for 10 days (Balangue-Tarriela et al. 2022). The most affected 
areas included Agoncillo, Alitagtag, Balete, Cuenca, Laurel, Lemery, Lipa City, 
Malvar, Mataas na Kahoy, San Nicolas, Santa Teresita, Taal, Talisay, Tanauan, and 
Tagaytay (NEDA IVA, 2020).

3.2. Methods
We used high-frequency EC and high-resolution NTL datasets as proxies for eco­
nomic activity to analyze the spatiotemporal distribution of the eruption impacts. 
Initially, we examined the relationship of EC and NTL with a usual measure of eco­
nomic activity, being GDP. Due to the lack of high-frequency and high-resolution 
GDP data, we aggregated the EC and NTL data to the national quarterly scale and 
performed a regression analysis (Online Appendix A). The results indicated that EC 
and NTL effectively explain national quarterly GDP. Additionally, we found a strong 
relationship between EC and NTL, reinforcing their use as measures of economic 
activity.
Based on the established relationship, we analyzed the temporal and spatial distri­
bution of Taal Volcano’s economic impacts using daily EC and monthly NTL data­
sets. Due to differing data scales, we conducted separate analyses to maximize spatial 
and temporal details from each data. We analyzed the temporal distribution of 
impacts using daily EC data through electricity consumption loss (ECL) estimation 
involving three steps: pre-eruption EC modeling, counterfactual EC estimation, and 
ECL computation. Subsequently, we analyzed the spatial distribution of impacts using 
monthly NTL data through NTLC computation, aggregation, and mapping.
3.2.1. ECL estimation process
Figure 7 illustrates the ECL estimation process. We estimated the daily economic 
activity loss in terms of ECL by subtracting the actual EC from the counterfactual EC 
estimated using a model generated based on the pre-eruption EC data. This model 
established the relationship between the explanatory variables and pre-eruption EC 
under normal conditions. This relationship would persist had there been no eruption. 
Under this condition, we use the pre-eruption model to estimate the counterfactual 
EC. Then, ECL was computed by subtracting the actual from the counterfactual.

This approach resembles the method of Beyer et al. (2021), who assessed the

and time effects. Using this model, they predicted daily EC during the pandemic and 
compared it to actual EC to measure the impacts.

We used mean temperature instead of maximum temperature for cooling and 
heating due to the positive linear relationship between EC and temperature, unlike 
the U-shaped correlation observed in India (Online Appendix B). Key features of this 
study include validating the method from previous research and extending its applica­
tion to a different study site and to the context of a volcanic eruption.
3.2.1.1 Pre-eruption EC modeling. The first step in the ECL estimation process was 
to generate the pre-eruption EC model that captured the relationship between the 
dependent variable EC and the independent variables before the eruption. For this, 
we divided the daily Luzon datasets into pre- and post-eruption subsets.
We regressed the natural logarithm of the pre-eruption EC lnECL, d for Luzon L per 
day d on the natural logarithm of mean temperature lnmeantempL, d using pre-eruption 
data. Mean temperature was chosen as an explanatory variable due to its influence on 
energy use (Bayudan-Dacuycuy 2017; Dacuycuy 2019; Santos 2020; Sabado et al. 2022) 
and its stability during the eruption (Ramirez 2020), enabling a counterfactual or 
“business-as-usual” estimate. EC patterns varied by day (Figure 4), with higher consump­
tion on weekdays and lower consumption on weekends. To account for this effect, we 
included the day-of-the-week dummy variable DW. We also included a holiday dummy 
variable Holiday to capture the reduced EC on national holidays (Figure 3), coding holi­
days as 1 and non-holidays as 0. Certain holidays and seasons, such as the Holy Week 
and Christmas, have extended effects, which were accounted for by adding a week of the 
year fixed effects WYfe. Additionally, we included a trend variable to account for the 
annual increase in EC, as shown in Figure 3. The resulting pre-eruption model was then 
represented in Equation (3), where the parameters to be estimated were denoted by b0 
and b1; and E was the error term.

3.2.2. NTLC analysis
NTLC analysis determines the spatial distribution of economic impacts using monthly 
NTL data as an indicator of economic activity based on its relationship with GDP. 
NTLC measures the change in nocturnal brightness from a pre- to post-eruption time.
3.2.2.1 NTLC computation. The existing literature offers two main approaches in detect­
ing changes: difference and ratio. In the difference approach, NTL in the disaster month is 
subtracted from either the previous year’s NTL, the previous month’s NTL (Lin and 
Rybnikova 2023), or the average NTL of prior years (Beyer et al. 2021). The ratio approach 
calculates the post-disaster NTL as a portion of the pre-disaster NTL (Sarkar 2021).
We carried out all these methods mentioned above but found that the difference 
between the eruption month and the average of the previous years had the most 
meaningful results. Therefore, we computed NTLCp; which represented the change in 
emitted light brightness per pixel p by subtracting the average NTL per pixel for 
January 2017 NTLp, 2017; January 2018 NTL p, 2018; and January 2019 NTL p, 2019 from 
the NTL in January 2020 NTLp, 2020 (Equation 6).

4. Results
4.1. Pre-eruption EC model
Four pre-eruption EC regression models were generated to compare the explanatory 
power of the variables (Table 1). The lnEC1 model showed that the trend could 
explain 69.7% of the pre-eruption EC. Adding the DW dummy variables in lnEC2 
increased the R2 to 0.808. Including the Holiday dummy variable in lnEC3 further 
raised the R2 to 0.859. The addition of the lnmeantemp variable in lnEC4 boosted the 
explanatory power of the model, explaining 90.5% of the pre-eruption EC. Of the 
four models, lnEC4 had the highest R2 value and was used to estimate the counterfac­
tual EC.
4.2. Counterfactual EC
Post-eruption data were plugged into the lnEC4 model to estimate cEC. The com­
puted cEC is for the entire Luzon Island. Table 2 reports the daily cEC estimates.
The computed cEC was plotted with aEC for comparison. Figure 8 shows that cEC 
is higher than aEC in the first two weeks after the eruption, indicating loss. However, 
aEC increased in the third week exceeding the cEC, suggesting recovery.
4.3. Daily ECL
Subtracting aEC from cEC yielded the daily ECL. ECL was highest on the day after 
the eruption (Figure 9), briefly recovered on days 6 and 7, increased again on day 8 
reached its second peak on day 11, dropped on day 12, and further dropped to nega­
tive values on day 13, only to rise again the following day. Subsequently, ECL 
decreased until the end of the observation period.

The rest of the municipalities had an overall increase in NTLC.
5. Discussion
5.1. Temporal analysis
The daily ECL’s temporal pattern revealed two loss peaks and two recovery valleys. 
Two factors could have contributed to the first peak. One would be power disruption. 
Ashfall from the eruption resulted in a power shutdown, affecting 24 municipal units 
in Cavite, Laguna, and Batangas (NDRRMC 2020). Power outages after an eruption 
are common, as wet ashfall could result in substation insulation flashovers and subse­
quent power outages (Nagai and Nakada 2022). The second contributor to this first 
peak could be a decrease in demand. During the eruption, power distributors in 
Batangas, namely, the Batangas I and Batangas II Electric Cooperatives, reported an 
electricity demand reduction due to evacuation and business closures (Rivera 2020).
Power was restored in the affected municipalities after roughly six days (Santos 
et al. 2023) leading to a brief recovery, but ECL started rising again, reaching its

second peak, possibly due to the increase in the affected population and evacuees 
(NDRRMC 2020) (Online Appendix C). Although the eruption lasted for only 
10 days, earthquakes kept recurring, and up to 841 earthquakes per day were recorded 
on day 11 (PHIVOLCS 2020). Further, more roads were closed at the start of day 8 
due to earthquake damage.
ECL dropped to its lowest level on day 13, indicating a consumption rise. This 
drop correlated with the distribution of financial support worth 32 million PhP that 
day (NDRRMC 2020), possibly stimulating increased EC in the area. The following 
day, ECL rose again, as assistance dropped to less than one million. On day 15, ECL 
dropped again, suggesting recovery, while the assistance cost increased by 22 million 
PhP. From this point onward, ECL continued to decline for the remainder of the 
study period.
Over the study period, the average %ECL (1.6%) fell between estimates from other 
researchers. Santos et al. (2023) estimated a pessimistic scenario through the input- 
output method, calculating total losses of 3.5 billion PhP over 10 days, or 1.4% of the 
region’s annual gross regional product (GRP). A government report (NEDA IVA 
2020) estimated total losses at 8.4 billion PhP, or 3.3% of the Gross Regional Product. 
Note that our results exclude agricultural losses, as this sector has minimal energy 
requirements.
Our results complement the government report by accounting for household 
losses. Monthly EC data for the Luzon grid (DOE 2020) disaggregates consumption 
into residential (35.4%), commercial (34.7%), and industrial (28.0%) sectors. Based on 
this, 0.57% of the computed ECL can be attributed to household loss. Unlike previous 
estimates (Santos et al. 2023), which show a smooth decline in economic loss, our

results reveal dynamic fluctuations in impacts, driven by the progression of the haz­

impacts, the temporal analysis sheds light on the population’s reaction to the disaster 
and assistance, offering insights for responders and authorities to monitor and act 
swiftly. Additionally, this analysis confirmed that EC data could capture the immedi­
ate economic impacts of an eruption, extending previous research (Fezzi and 
Fanghella 2020; Beyer et al. 2021).
5.2. Spatial analysis
Spatial analysis revealed uneven NTL reduction, matching the 17-km danger zone, 
ashfall extent, and built-up areas. The most intensely dimmed spots (Figure 10) were 
within the 17-km danger zone. Within this zone, the dimming was concentrated in 
and around built-up areas (see land cover map in Del Castillo et al. 2020) within the 
ash-covered coasts north and west of Taal Lake (see ashfall extent map in Merin 
et al. 2021). Although agricultural losses have been reported (NEDA IVA 2020), 
NTLC was not able to detect these impacts.
Aggregating the pixels revealed the most affected municipalities, including Talisay, 
Laurel, Santa Teresita, Agoncillo, Lemery, San Nicolas, Taal, and Balete. This list 
largely aligns with reports, with two key discrepancies. First, the overestimated NTLC 
value in Santa Teresita was caused by an anomalous cluster of high NTL pixels in the 
January 2017 image, which inflated pre-eruption brightness and led to a significant 
NTL drop in 2020. Since the anomaly occurred in 2017, it was unrelated to the erup­
tion. Second, our list missed several municipalities identified in government reports 
(NEDA IVA 2020), including Alitagtag, Cuenca, Lipa City, Malvar, Mataasnakahoy, 
Tanauan, and Tagaytay. This could be due to increased NTL emissions from evacu­
ation sites in those areas. Despite these discrepancies, our NTLC map and the munic­
ipalities identified by our method demonstrate the potential of NTL data for mapping 
volcanic eruption impacts with fine spatial detail.
5.3. Policy and research recommendations
The temporal analysis implies the importance of prompt lifeline restoration and 
financial-support distribution for recovery. It further signifies that the impacts are 
not dimensionless values. Beyond the ballpark loss estimate, economic impacts have a 
temporal dimension shaped by dynamic factors like accompanying hazards, such as 
earthquakes, damaged infrastructures, and affected population flux, often neglected 
due to data limitations. This study demonstrated that EC data could fill this data gap. 
However, gaps still exist, particularly in spatial and sectoral disaggregation of EC 
data. Hence, it would be helpful for researchers to have more frequently updated EC 
data with finer spatial resolution and sectoral classification to enable near-real-time 
impact monitoring and to have further studies investigating how monitoring these 
impacts could reduce losses and recovery time.
The spatial analysis from the NTLC maps can guide reconstruction planning. 
Reconstruction should avoid high-risk zones identified as affected areas in the NTLC

maps, which coincided with those devastated by past eruptions, such as in 1911 
(Maso 1911). The NTLC map can also serve as a basis for assistance funds applica­
tion, to which victims can apply based on the state of calamity declaration, often cov­
ering entire administrative units. Such broad coverage means that support is not 
always proportionate to the damage suffered. This study highlights the uneven impact 
distribution within a municipality, suggesting that the NTLC map can help direct aid 
to the most affected citizens.
6. Conclusion
This study analyzed the spatiotemporal distribution of economic impacts using daily 
EC and monthly NTL data. We computed ECL and NTLC as indicators of economic 
activity reduction. Results showed dynamic fluctuations and spatial heterogeneity in 
economic impacts. Temporal analysis showed that ECL had two loss peaks and two 
recovery valleys, congruent with power outages and restoration, road closures, earth­
quake occurrences, the number of affected populations, evacuees, and assistance dis­
tribution. This finding highlights the importance of temporal dimension analysis and 
frequent data updating to capture the dynamic impact developments. The spatial ana­
lysis showed significant NTL reductions at the intersection of ashfall extent and built- 
up areas. This analysis detected the municipalities with the most NTL reduction and 
mapped the most affected areas within municipalities. This finding can guide recon­
struction planning and support prioritization.
We note that the results are limited to non-agricultural economic activities as these 
do not use substantial energy and emit significant light at night. Further, the results 
only cover the immediate impacts because the method cannot perform impact esti­
mates for multiple hazards. This study also featured two separate analyses using EC 
for temporal distribution and NTL for spatial distribution due to the differences in 
scales. Future research could lengthen the study period to monitor long-term impacts, 
use finer resolution data with sectoral classification, and explore other ways to use 
EC in conjunction with NTL data.
To conclude, EC and NTL datasets can be alternative methods for analyzing the 
spatiotemporal distributions of immediate economic impacts from a volcanic erup­
tion. This method features remote impact assessment for response and recovery plan­
ning, even with ongoing hazards. Using high-frequency EC and high-resolution NTL 
data, economic impact estimates of volcanic eruptions can be free from spatiotempo­
ral blindness.
