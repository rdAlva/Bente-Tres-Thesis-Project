Introduction 
In the 21st-century higher education landscape, increasing scholarly attention has been 
directed toward the role of spirituality and religious conviction in shaping students’ academic 
lives. A growing body of international research demonstrates that university students experience 
academic learning not solely as a cognitive pursuit, but as a multidimensional process 
intertwined with identity formation, moral development, and the search for meaning and purpose 
(Astin, Astin, & Lindholm, 2011; Chickering, Dalton, & Stamm, 2006; Smith & Denton, 2005). 
Alongside managing academic responsibilities, students concurrently grapple with questions of 
vocation, values, and ethical accountability (Parks, 2011). 
Empirical studies across global contexts have found that students who incorporate 
spiritual or religious frameworks into their academic experiences often exhibit heightened 
motivation, increased resilience, and a deeper capacity for meaning-making during periods of 
academic stress (Bryant & Astin, 2008; Koenig, King, & Carson, 2012; Hill et al., 2010). Rather 
than functioning as external influences, faith commitments serve as internal resources that help 
young people interpret success, failure, perseverance, and moral obligation within the context of 
university life (Smith & Denton, 2005; Walker & Dixon, 2002). For example, Astin et al. (2011) 
demonstrated that spirituality and religious engagement are positively associated with 
psychological well-being and sense of purpose among college students, reinforcing the 
importance of faith-learning integration in higher education. 
Campus-based religious organizations and ministries are also recognized as significant 
formative spaces where students learn to connect faith with academic life. Participation in 
campus ministry has been shown to foster reflective capacities, ethical reasoning, and a sense of 
academic purpose through opportunities for dialogue, mentoring, and communal reflection

(Bryant, Choi, & Yasuno, 2003; Cherry, DeBerg, & Porterfield, 2014; Bryant & Craft, 2010). 
Such ministries enable students to interpret academic work not merely as a requirement for

These formative experiences are echoed in the Philippine context, where local studies highlight 
that campus ministry and faith-based organizations support both academic engagement and the

Despite these advances, much of the existing literature—both internationally and in the 
Philippines—relies on quantitative methods that offer limited insight into students’ lived 
experiences (Astin et al., 2011; Bryant & Astin, 2008; De Leon, 2025). Philippine research, for 
example, often examines correlations between religiosity and academic outcomes but less 
frequently explores how students themselves construct meaning from their faith in the academic 
context. There is a growing call for qualitative research that centers students’ own narratives and 
experiences (Clandinin & Connelly, 2000; Tuason & Canoy, 2013). 
Within the Philippines, spirituality and religiosity remain deeply woven into cultural and 
educational life. Local research has established that religious practice and spiritual beliefs 
continue to play a central role in shaping Filipino students’ identity and university experience 
(Tuason & Canoy, 2013; Aguilar, 2014). Empirical studies in Catholic universities indicate that 
religious education and campus ministry are integral to holistic student formation, promoting 
moral values, community service, and reflective learning alongside academic achievement (De la 
Cruz, 2017; Ocampo & Serrano, 2020). For instance, Montiel and See (2008) found that 
participation in campus ministry fosters both academic resilience and ethical formation among 
college students in Mindanao.

Recent evidence from De Leon (2025) adds nuance to this landscape, revealing that while 
there is a modest but statistically significant link between Filipino students’ attitudes toward 
social media and their spirituality, their spiritual lives remain anchored in personal, cultural, and 
relational practices. This highlights the complexity of spiritual development in contemporary 
academic settings, shaped by traditional values and emerging influences. Filipino undergraduates 
also report using spiritual coping strategies to navigate academic stress and personal challenges, 
underscoring the enduring relevance of faith in higher education (Ocampo & Serrano, 2020). 
Nevertheless, similar to global trends, most Philippine studies employ quantitative or 
correlational designs, which provide useful statistical insights but do not fully capture how 
students experience religious commitment amid academic pressures and transitions (De la Cruz, 
2017; De Leon, 2025). There remains a need for qualitative studies that foreground the voices 
and lived realities of students, especially those involved in campus youth ministry (Tuason & 
Canoy, 2013). 
This research gap is particularly salient in faith-based institutions such as Holy Angel 
University, where religious education is central to institutional identity and mission. Holy Angel 
University, as a Catholic institution, promotes holistic student formation by integrating 
intellectual competence, ethical responsibility, and spiritual growth. Campus Youth Ministry 
serves as a key venue for faith formation, leadership development, and community life, 
positioning itself at the intersection of religious commitment and academic experience (De la 
Cruz, 2017). 
The present study is grounded in the professional and pedagogical commitments of the 
researchers, who are religious educators directly engaged in higher education formation.

Review of Related Literature 
Campus Youth Ministry (CYM) defined

Campus Youth Ministry (CYM) refers to structured religious organizations within higher 
education institutions that foster faith formation, leadership development, and community 
engagement among students. These ministries serve as both social and spiritual support systems, 
providing avenues for worship, service, peer mentorship, and reflective dialogue (Bryant, Choi, 
& Yasuno, 2003; Cherry, DeBerg, & Porterfield, 2014). In the Philippine context, CYM is often 
embedded within Catholic universities, aligning with institutional missions to promote holistic 
student formation and moral development (De la Cruz, 2017; De Leon, 2025). 
International studies show that campus ministry plays a critical role in shaping students’ 
religious identities and offers an essential context for the integration of faith and learning (Astin, 
Astin, & Lindholm, 2011; Cherry et al., 2014; Smith & Denton, 2005). Participation in CYM is 
associated with increased spiritual engagement, ethical reasoning, and a sense of belonging 
(Bryant & Astin, 2008; Small & Bowman, 2018). Ministries commonly structure their activities 
around liturgical celebrations, retreats, social outreach, and reflective sessions, facilitating the 
translation of religious values into daily academic life (Chickering, Dalton, & Stamm, 2006; 
Streib & Hood, 2016). 
Furthermore, research reveals that campus ministries function not merely as ancillary 
programs but as integral components of student formation, particularly in institutions where 
religious education is central to institutional identity (Astin et al., 2011; Cherry et al., 2014). 
Engagement in CYM has been linked to leadership development, enhanced interpersonal skills, 
and moral discernment (Bryant, 2006; Parks, 2011). For many students, these ministries provide 
a formative environment where questions of vocation, purpose, and ethical responsibility are 
explored in community (De Leon, 2025; De la Cruz, 2017). 
Students’ Spirituality

Spirituality among university students encompasses a search for meaning, purpose, and 
connectedness that transcends material or academic pursuits (Astin, Astin, & Lindholm, 2011; 
Kim & Esquivel, 2011). It is often described as distinct from religiosity because it may—or may 
not—be associated with formal religious affiliation or practice (Bryant & Astin, 2008; Love, 
2002). Students’ spirituality is frequently expressed through reflective practices, ethical inquiry, 
and engagement in service or social justice initiatives (Tisdell, 2003; Cherry, DeBerg, & 
Porterfield, 2014). 
Recent research underscores the enduring salience of spirituality for student well-being 
and holistic development, even in increasingly secular or pluralistic academic settings. For 
example, Astin et al. (2011) found that spiritual growth is strongly correlated with gains in self-
esteem, sense of purpose, and psychological well-being among college students in the United 
States. Similarly, Bryant and Astin (2008) identified spirituality as a protective factor against 
stress and depression during the college years. A large-scale study by Mayhew, Rockenbach, et 
al. (2016) also demonstrates that spiritual development is linked to greater life satisfaction and 
academic engagement across diverse student populations. 
In the Philippine context, qualitative studies indicate that spirituality remains deeply 
embedded in students’ personal identity and everyday routines, often serving as a source of 
comfort, motivation, and resilience (Tuason & Canoy, 2013). Filipino students commonly report 
that prayer, meditation, and participation in religious or spiritual communities help them cope 
with academic pressure and life transitions (Ocampo & Serrano, 2020). This aligns with 
international findings where campus-based spiritual engagement—such as involvement in youth 
ministry or faith-based organizations—offers opportunities for spiritual growth, peer support, 
and value clarification (Bryant, Choi, & Yasuno, 2003; Parks, 2011).

Moreover, numerous studies have shown that spiritual practices such as prayer, meditation, and 
communal worship are associated with increased self-regulation, emotional stability, and

Religious Commitment among Young Generation 
Religious commitment among young adults is typically characterized by adherence to 
faith-based beliefs, personal and communal practices, and active organizational participation 
(Smith & Denton, 2005; Streib & Hood, 2016). Although global trends indicate a general decline 
in formal religious affiliation among youth in some regions, recent scholarship emphasizes the 
persistent influence of religious values and the emergence of new forms of spiritual expression, 
with many young people maintaining individualized or non-institutional forms of faith (Pew 
Research Center, 2018; Smith & Denton, 2005; Mayhew et al., 2016). 
Within Catholic and other faith-based universities, religious commitment is often 
reinforced through institutional culture, campus ministry, and the integration of faith-based 
curricula (Astin, Astin, & Lindholm, 2011; De la Cruz, 2017). Studies in the Philippine context 
show that young people continue to report high levels of religious practice and faith-based 
identity, attributing significance to rituals, community worship, and service activities as central 
to their personal and academic lives (Tuason & Canoy, 2013; De Leon, 2025). Such institutional 
and community settings serve as important contexts for the socialization and maintenance of 
religious commitment among students.

Empirical research has consistently linked strong religious commitment in students to 
positive developmental outcomes, including greater life satisfaction, resilience to stress, and 
increased community engagement (Koenig, King, & Carson, 2012; Bryant, Choi, & Yasuno, 
2003; Smith & Denton, 2005). Commitment to faith traditions often provides a moral compass 
and interpretive framework for navigating academic, personal, and ethical challenges (Astin et 
al., 2011; Parks, 2011). However, studies also highlight the tensions faced by young adults as 
they negotiate religious expectations within pluralistic or secular academic environments, which 
can generate both opportunities for growth and sources of conflict (Cherry, DeBerg, & 
Porterfield, 2014; Smith & Denton, 2005). Notably, qualitative research underscores the 
importance of supportive religious communities, such as campus ministries, in sustaining young 
adults’ faith commitment and providing resources for meaning-making and belonging (Bryant & 
Craft, 2010; Tuason & Canoy, 2013). 
Academic Motivation and Engagement 
Academic motivation refers to the constellation of internal drives and external factors 
that propel students to achieve, persist, and find meaning in their educational pursuits (Pintrich & 
Schunk, 2002; Ryan & Deci, 2000). Recent meta-analytical and empirical studies confirm that 
both spirituality and religious commitment can enhance academic motivation by fostering a 
sense of purpose, hope, and psychological resilience (Astin, Astin, & Lindholm, 2011; Laird et 
al., 2014; King & Boyatzis, 2015). For example, research by Steger et al. (2013) found that 
college students with a strong sense of meaning and purpose—often rooted in spiritual values—
display higher levels of academic engagement and satisfaction. 
There is also robust evidence that students who view their academic work as congruent 
with their spiritual or religious beliefs report elevated motivation, better coping strategies, and

Sense of Purpose 
A sense of purpose is foundational to student well-being, motivation, and long-term 
academic and life success (Damon, Menon, & Bronk, 2003; Hill, Burrow, O’Dell, & Thornton, 
2010). For university students, spirituality and religious commitment are significant sources of 
purpose, providing frameworks for understanding one’s role in the world, future aspirations, and 
moral responsibilities (Astin, Astin, & Lindholm, 2011; Parks, 2011). Empirical research 
suggests that students who integrate their faith with academic pursuits are more likely to 
articulate clear vocational goals, maintain optimism, and pursue meaningful careers (Bryant, 
Choi, & Yasuno, 2003; Cherry, DeBerg, & Porterfield, 2014). 
Campus youth ministries also play a crucial role in cultivating this sense of purpose by 
fostering reflective discernment, providing mentorship, and encouraging service-oriented 
activities (Bryant & Craft, 2010; Parks, 2011). These ministries create structured opportunities 
for students to explore questions of calling, vocation, and life direction in the context of faith and 
community (Astin et al., 2011; Cherry et al., 2014). Students who participate in campus 
ministries often report a heightened sense of agency, optimism, and commitment to personal and 
societal goals (Hill et al., 2010; Parks, 2011). 
In the Philippine context, qualitative research underscores that involvement in campus 
ministry strengthens students’ sense of purpose by linking academic work to broader missions of 
service, justice, and personal growth (De la Cruz, 2017; Tuason & Canoy, 2013). International 
literature corroborates that a sense of purpose, nurtured through faith and spiritual engagement,

Ethical Decision Making 
Ethical decision making involves the ability to discern right from wrong and to act 
consistently with moral values, especially amid complex or ambiguous situations (Rest, 1986; 
Kohlberg, 1984). Spirituality and religious commitment serve as major influences on students’ 
ethical reasoning by providing enduring guiding principles and communal norms (Bryant, 2006; 
Cherry, DeBerg, & Porterfield, 2014; Koenig, King, & Carson, 2012). Studies across various 
educational contexts show that participation in campus youth ministry is associated with 
increased ethical awareness, critical reflection, and a greater willingness to engage in moral 
dialogue (Bryant & Astin, 2008; Parks, 2011). 
Research indicates that students with strong religious commitments are more likely to 
report ethical sensitivity, integrity, and a sense of responsibility toward others (Kim & Esquivel, 
2011; Astin, Astin, & Lindholm, 2011). Campus ministry activities—such as service learning, 
ethics workshops, and faith-sharing groups—create dedicated spaces for students to deliberate on 
moral issues and develop ethical leadership skills (Bryant, Choi, & Yasuno, 2003; Parks, 2011). 
These experiences foster not only personal integrity but also communal accountability and social 
responsibility (Astin et al., 2011; Cherry et al., 2014). 
In the Philippine context, ethical decision making is deeply rooted in spiritual, cultural, 
and community values. Qualitative research supports that Filipino students often draw on 
religious teachings, faith-based mentorship, and ministry guidance when navigating ethical 
dilemmas in both academic and social spheres (De Leon, 2025; Tuason & Canoy, 2013). 
Campus ministries thus play a pivotal role in nurturing ethical discernment and action among

Faith and Time Management among Students 
Time management remains a significant predictor of academic success, and faith 
traditions often offer structured routines and priorities that help students navigate competing 
demands (Britton & Tesser, 1991; Macan et al., 1990). Recent studies reinforce that students 
who are regularly involved in religious activities develop stronger planning skills and are more 
likely to allocate time effectively across academic, social, and personal obligations (Koenig, 
2008; Nursalam et al., 2022). Spiritual practices such as meditation, scheduled prayer, and ritual 
observance foster a rhythm of reflection and intentionality, enabling students to approach their 
commitments with greater clarity and discipline (Carter, Garrod, & Kang, 2013; Park, 
McDonough, & Binge, 2017). 
In environments where faith and education are both emphasized, students often describe 
faith-based time management as a source of peace and focus. Chickering, Dalton, and Stamm 
(2006) observed that students in faith-supportive environments use religious rituals and 
community events as anchors for their weekly schedules, which can reduce stress and support 
academic performance. Stebleton and Soria (2012) found that involvement in campus ministry or 
similar organizations is associated with enhanced self-efficacy and the ability to set and achieve 
goals. Similarly, Hagedorn and Purnamasari (2012) reported that spiritual engagement 
encourages students to balance ambition with self-care, contributing to both academic 
achievement and personal well-being. 
Recent Philippine research affirms that time management is closely linked to faith 
commitments and campus ministry participation among university students. Ching and Chua

scholarly attention in the past five years, as recent studies recognize that religious identity 
remains a critical foundation for students’ aspirations, well-being, and resilience in higher 
education (Mayhew et al., 2016; Clycq et al., 2022; De Leon, 2025). For instance, Saroglou and 
Muñoz-García (2021) found that students who view their academic work as an extension of 
spiritual values report higher motivation, stronger coping skills, and greater fulfillment in their 
educational journey.

Research Objectives 
Higher education institutions, particularly faith-based universities, aim to promote the 
holistic formation of students by integrating academic excellence with spiritual and moral 
development. Global and Philippine studies affirm that spirituality and religious commitment 
significantly influence students’ meaning-making, values formation, and personal well-being. 
However, much of the existing literature relies on quantitative approaches and treats religiosity 
as a general variable, offering limited insight into how students personally experience the 
integration of faith and academic life. 
Despite the strong presence of campus youth ministries in Catholic universities in the 
country, there is a noticeable lack of qualitative research that explores how student members of 
these ministries understand and live out their religious commitment within their academic 
experiences. In particular, the voices of students who actively participate in Campus Youth 
Ministry—and who are expected to embody faith–learning integration—remain 
underrepresented in scholarly discourse. 
At Holy Angel University, Campus Youth Ministry plays a central role in faith formation 
and student leadership. Yet, there is insufficient empirical evidence that examines how 
involvement in this ministry shapes students’ academic motivation, sense of purpose, ethical 
decision-making, and overall academic engagement. Addressing this gap is essential for 
strengthening religious education praxis, campus ministry programs, and institutional formation 
initiatives in line with the research agenda of the ICFSI Department, which emphasizes faith 
integration, student formation, and contextualized religious education.

Thus, this study sought to explore and describe the lived experiences of Campus Youth 
Ministry members of Holy Angel University regarding their religious commitment and its

Specifically, this study seeks to answer the following questions: 
1. How do Campus Youth Ministry members interpret and give meaning to their academic 
responsibilities in relation to their faith and spiritual identity? 
2. How does participation in Campus Youth Ministry influence their academic motivation?  
3. What challenges and tensions do they encounter in living out their religious commitment 
within the academic environment? 
4. Which Campus Youth Ministry programs or activities do students identify as having the 
most impact on their academic motivation, personal formation, or integration of faith and 
learning, and what suggestions do they offer to enhance these programs? 
METHODS 
Research Design 
This study adopted a narrative research design to deeply explore the lived experiences of 
Campus Youth Ministry members at Holy Angel University as they integrate religious 
commitment with their academic lives. Narrative inquiry is a qualitative approach well-suited for 
capturing personal stories and meaning-making processes, particularly in educational contexts 
where identity, motivation, and spirituality are central (Clandinin & Connelly, 2000; Creswell & 
Poth, 2018). Through in-depth, semi-structured interviews, participants were encouraged to share 
detailed accounts of how faith influences their academic motivation, time management, ethical 
decision-making, and sense of purpose.

Philosophical Underpinning 
This study is grounded in a constructivist-interpretivist philosophical paradigm, which 
emphasizes the ontological belief that reality is subjective and constructed through individuals’ 
lived experiences (Creswell & Poth, 2018; Lincoln, Lynham, & Guba, 2011). In examining the 
integration of faith and academic life among Campus Youth Ministry members, the research 
assumes that each participant’s reality is shaped by personal, cultural, spiritual, and institutional 
contexts. Rather than seeking a single objective truth, the study values the multiplicity of 
meanings and recognizes that each narrative offers a unique window into the intersection of faith 
and education (Clandinin & Connelly, 2000). 
Axiology also plays a salient role, as narrative research acknowledges and values the role 
of the researcher’s and participants’ beliefs, values, and ethical considerations throughout the 
research process (Denzin & Lincoln, 2018). The study is designed in a way that is sensitive to 
participants’ spiritual convictions and recognizes that both the act of storytelling and the 
interpretation of those stories are influenced by deeply held values and cultural norms. This 
value-laden approach affirms the importance of respect, empathy, and relationality in the 
research encounter, which is particularly relevant in faith-based contexts.

While epistemology is present in all qualitative work, this study specifically adopts a 
constructivist epistemology, positing that knowledge is co-constructed through dialogue and 
reflection between researcher and participants (Lincoln et al., 2011). The research process is thus 
collaborative, seeking to uncover not just what students know about integrating faith and 
academics, but how they come to know, understand, and narrate those experiences. Together, the 
ontological, axiological, and epistemological commitments of this study ensure a holistic, 
ethical, and context-sensitive exploration of the lived realities of Campus Youth Ministry 
members.  
Participants and Setting 
This study was conducted at Holy Angel University, a Catholic higher education 
institution in the Philippines known for its strong emphasis on holistic student formation through 
faith-based education and campus ministry. The participants of this study consisted of selected 
undergraduate members of the Campus Youth Ministry (CYM), which serves as a formative 
space for faith development, leadership training, and service engagement within the university.

Reflexivity 
Recognizing the inherent subjectivity in qualitative research, the researchers engaged in 
ongoing reflexivity throughout the study. As religious educators and individuals with current 
involvement in campus ministry, the researchers were aware that their personal backgrounds, 
beliefs, and professional commitments could influence various stages of the research process, 
including data collection, interpretation, and representation (Berger, 2015; Creswell & Poth, 
2018). To address potential biases, the researchers maintained reflexive journals, actively 
documenting their assumptions, reactions, and decision-making processes before, during, and 
after the interviews. 
Regular discussions and peer debriefing sessions were conducted between the principal 
researcher, a Theology professor, and the co-researcher, the Campus Ministry Office (CMO) 
Coordinator and Character Formation Officer, throughout the data collection and analysis 
phases. Following the completion of interviews and during the coding and thematic analysis 
process, both researchers independently reviewed the interview transcripts before engaging in 
structured discussions to compare interpretations, examine emerging categories and themes, and 
critically reflect on their assumptions, perspectives, and potential biases. Whenever differences 
in interpretation arose, the researchers revisited the participants' verbatim responses until a 
consensus was reached, ensuring that the findings remained grounded in the participants' 
accounts rather than the researchers' preconceptions. Additionally, the involvement of external

responsibilities, responded to challenges, made important decisions, and pursued personal 
growth. Rather than treating faith as a separate aspect of life, participants viewed it as an 
essential framework that influenced their motivation, values, academic aspirations, and sense of 
purpose. Synthesizing the findings across the seven interview questions, five interrelated themes

The findings indicate that Campus Youth Ministry members perceive faith as the 
fundamental lens through which they understand their academic responsibilities and educational 
experiences. Synthesizing the findings from Questions 1 and 2, participants consistently 
described their academic work not merely as a requirement for graduation or future employment 
but as an expression of their relationship with God, a means of practicing Christian values, and 
preparation for future service. Their narratives suggest that faith provides a framework for 
interpreting the meaning, purpose, and moral significance of education. Rather than separating

This interpretation is consistent with Astin, Astin, and Lindholm (2011), who argued that 
spirituality enables college students to develop a stronger sense of meaning, purpose, and 
authenticity throughout their educational journey. According to their study, students who 
actively integrate spirituality into their lives are more likely to view education as a process of 
personal formation rather than simply a pathway to achievement. Similarly, Parks (2011) 
explained that emerging adulthood involves an ongoing search for meaning in which young 
people attempt to integrate beliefs, values, identity, and life goals into a coherent worldview. The 
participants in the present study reflected this developmental process by describing academic 
responsibilities as opportunities to honor God, steward their God-given talents, and prepare 
themselves for future service through their chosen professions. 
The findings further reveal that faith shaped not only how participants understood 
academics but also how they performed their responsibilities as students. Participants 
consistently highlighted values such as honesty, integrity, diligence, perseverance, and 
responsibility as essential guiding principles in accomplishing academic tasks. This observation 
supports Chickering, Dalton, and Stamm's (2006) contention that higher education should foster

opportunities to live out Christian virtues and demonstrate faith through responsible action.

Theme 3. Faith-Based Discernment in Academic Decision-Making 
The participants' narratives suggest that academic decision-making is not merely an intellectual 
or practical process but a deeply reflective and faith-informed experience. When confronted with 
important academic choices—whether persevering through difficult courses, assuming 
leadership roles, or balancing ministry commitments with school responsibilities—participants 
frequently turned to prayer, spiritual reflection, and consultation with trusted members of the 
Campus Youth Ministry community. Academic decisions were therefore understood as 
opportunities to discern God's will while exercising personal responsibility and judgment. 
Such experiences point to the significant role of faith communities in shaping how young adults 
navigate uncertainty. Rather than making decisions independently, participants described relying 
on relationships within the ministry as sources of wisdom, encouragement, and guidance. This 
pattern echoes Parks' (2011) view that emerging adulthood is characterized by an ongoing search 
for coherence among beliefs, values, and life choices. Parks further emphasized that meaningful 
communities contribute to the development of wisdom and confidence by creating spaces where

A recurring reality in the participants' experiences was the challenge of honoring both academic

these situations as evidence of incompatibility between faith and academics. Instead, they 
viewed them as part of the ongoing task of learning how to steward their time, commitments, and 
energies responsibly. 
The capacity to remain engaged despite competing demands appears closely connected to 
the role of faith as a source of resilience. Participants frequently described drawing strength from 
prayer, trusting in God's guidance, and maintaining spiritual practices even when faced with

Conclusion 
This study explored how members of the Campus Youth Ministry (CYM) perceive the 
relationship between their religious commitment and academic responsibilities within a Catholic 
higher education institution. Through the participants' lived experiences, the study demonstrated 
that faith is deeply integrated into their academic life and serves as a significant influence on 
how they understand their educational responsibilities, respond to academic challenges, and 
prepare for future service. Rather than perceiving faith and academics as separate domains, 
participants consistently described these dimensions as complementary aspects of their personal 
and spiritual formation.

education institutions. 
For the Campus Ministry Office and Campus Youth Ministry 
The Campus Ministry Office (CMO), in collaboration with the Campus Youth Ministry 
(CYM), may strengthen existing formation programs by intentionally integrating academic 
accompaniment with spiritual formation. While participants affirmed that prayer gatherings, 
leadership opportunities, and ministry activities positively influenced their academic motivation 
and personal growth, they likewise expressed the need for initiatives that respond to the 
academic realities of student life. Programs such as peer mentoring, faith-sharing circles 
focused on academic resilience, workshops on time management and discernment, and 
accompaniment during examination periods may further support students in balancing their 
academic responsibilities with their commitment to Christian service. 
For Theology and Religious Education Programs

and co-curricular initiatives that reinforce classroom learning through experiential faith 
formation. Such collaborative efforts can help students recognize that academic excellence, 
Christian discipleship, and service to others are mutually enriching dimensions of holistic

schedules, may encourage greater student participation without compromising educational 
responsibilities. Such collaboration reflects the Catholic educational mission of forming the 
whole person through the harmonious integration of intellectual, spiritual, moral, and social 
development.
