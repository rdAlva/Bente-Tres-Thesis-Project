Abstract  
 
Succession planning (SP, thereafter) is essential for leadership continuity and institutional 
sustainability, particularly within higher education institutions (HEIs) (Ahmad et al., 2020; 
Rothwell, 2005). While the corporate sector has extensively institutionalized SP, its adoption in 
state universities and colleges (SUCs) in the Philippines remains underexplored, with leadership 
transitions often influenced by organizational culture, bureaucratic constraints, and political 
dynamics (Brillantes & Perante-Calina, 2018; Palencia, 2022). Using qualitative methods, this 
study examines the extent to which organizational culture shapes SP within SUCs, analyzing 
leadership selection processes, structural barriers, and informal succession mechanisms (Chia, 
2021; Ghazali et al., 2021). Findings reveal that while SUCs recognize the importance of 
leadership continuity, the absence of formal succession policies leads to inconsistent appointment 
practices and susceptibility to external political influences (Collins, 2013; McKee & Froelich, 
2016; Umans et al., 2021). Furthermore, results highlight the tension between hierarchical 
governance structures and the necessity for more transparent and merit-based leadership 
development strategies (Bano et al., 2022; Besel et al., 2021). By contextualizing SP within 
Philippine SUCs, this study contributes to existing leadership development literature in HEIs and 
provides actionable recommendations for establishing structured and sustainable SP frameworks. 
The research underscores the importance of aligning SP with institutional culture to foster 
leadership stability, advocating for the adoption of systematic policies that balance organizationa l 
values with strategic succession mechanisms (Ghazali et al., 2021; Rothwell, 2011).

Introduction 
 
Succession planning has long been recognized as a vital strategy employed by various 
organizations to ensure leadership continuity, stability, and the achievement of long-term goals 
(Ahmad et al., 2022; Atwood, 2007; Jayme et al., 2013; Rothwell, 2005; Siambi, 2022). It involves, 
among others, identifying potential leaders, developing and mentoring them, and establishing 
structures that facilitate smooth transitions (Ahmad et al., 2024; Barnet & Davis, 2008; Mehrabani 
& Mohamad, 2011; Okwakpam, 2019; Rothwell, 2011; Sambrook, 2005).  
 
Literature reveals that effective succession planning is widely recognized for its role in 
strengthening organizational resilience, maintaining knowledge continuity, and fostering sustained 
growth (Appelbaum et al., 2012; Hatcher, 2023). Albeit used mainly by business organizations 
(chiefly large corporations), recent literature has revealed the growing interest of scholars in the 
use of SP in academic settings, particularly in institutions of higher learning (Barton, 2019; Chia, 
2021; Ghazali et al., 2023; Hatcher, 2023; King, 2019; Lloyd & Wolstencroft, 2022; Morrow, 2023; 
Owusu et al., 2022; Richard, 2009; Romero, 2021; Shamsuddin, 2012; Swanson, 2018; Tahir et 
al., 2023).  
For academic institutions, however, SP poses unique challenges (Chia, 2021). Leadership 
transitions in these contexts necessitate striking a balance between the pursuit of academic 
excellence and the need for stable governance structures, while addressing the diverse needs of 
faculty, students, and other stakeholders (Ghazali et al., 2021; Omar & Hassan, 2015).  
Although literature affirms the benefits of well-planned leadership transitions (Ahmad et 
al., 2022; Atwood, 2007; Jayme et al., 2013; Rothwell, 2005; Siambi, 2022), many institutions still 
face obstacles in establishing effective succession planning, often due to underlying structural, 

4 
 
cultural, and external complexities that impact these processes (Bano et al., 2022; Besel et al., 
2021; Collins, 2013; McKee & Froelich, 2016; Mwemezi, 2011; Umans, et al., 2021).  
In the Philippines, SP in state universities and colleges (henceforth, SUCs) face additional 
layers of complexity shaped by the distinctive organizational culture, bureaucratic constraints, and 
influence of political dynamics inherent to public institutions (Brillantes & Perante-Calina, 2018; 
Jaymalin-Dulay, 2021; Palencia, 2022). While formal policies for leadership continuity exist, little 
research has examined how cultural and institutional factors specifically impact these processes 
within SUCs in the Philippines. Thus, this study aims to explore the nuanced role of organizational 
culture in shaping succession planning within SUCs. This research further examines the visible 
practices and the hidden mechanisms that influence SP, contributing to a deeper understanding of 
leadership transitions and addressing a gap in the literature on higher education governance in the 
Philippines.  
 
Review of Related Literature 
 
 
Concepts of Succession Planning 
 
The idea of succession planning was pioneered in the early 20th century by Henri Fayol, a 
management expert who believed that current management has an essential role in ensuring an 
organization's stability and continuity through the strategic development of leadership skills and 
characteristics (Siambi, 2022).  
Despite its early emergence, it was only in the latter part of the 1960s and early part of the 
1970s that businesses took cognizance of SP's importance largely as a response to the emergence 
of leadership challenges faced by various companies at that time (Bano et al., 2022). Early notable 

5 
 
studies (Coleman, 1970; Trow, 1961; Zaich, 1986) in SP were mainly focused on workforce 
planning and executive succession as it was traditionally practiced decades ago that SP 
concentrated only on the people and not on their competencies (Garman & Tyler, 2004). As 
Mehrabani and Mohamad (2011) put it: “the only purpose of succession planning was to tag and 
identify the needed successors to replace their ancestors for a specific job” (p. 371).  
Since then, the concept of SP has been adopted by various sectors (business, public 
institutions, and education) of society, where leadership succession has been notably imperative as 
a strategy for organizational continuity and stability (Ahmad et al., 2020; Rothwell, 2005). Over 
time, due to its pervasive use and application in various sectors and contexts, particularly in the 
business sector, succession planning’s definition has evolved, thereby providing a much broader 
and encompassing area of inquiry across various aspects of an organization, which includes among 
others HR, sustainability leadership, and educational leadership (Rothwell, 2005). These 
definitions have provided scholars with diverse research directions and study scopes, thus 
widening the debate and offering new perspectives regarding SP.  
Scholars have proposed various definitions of SP, and they can be loosely categorized into 
two distinct groups: the broad and general definition and the sector-specific definition. One of 
these general definitions is that of Jayme et al. (2013). They define SP as a process involving long-
term preparation to develop replacements for key positions to maintain continuous leadership 
within an organization. Atwood (2007), however, did not only include leadership positions but 
included all employees in SP. She defined SP as a tool institutions use to organize and replace 
administrators, supervisors, and other employees when they leave their posts, which is crucial for 
their long-term success.  

6 
 
SP, according to Okwakpam (2019), involves ensuring that organizations hire and 
continually enhance the skills of their staff to fill critical roles within the organization. In the same 
vein, Barnett and Davis (2008) provided another broad definition, which states that succession 
planning is a process that involves identifying and preparing internal candidates to take on 
important roles within the organization. This time, putting emphasis on the importance of internal 
talent pool within the organization, an idea which runs counter with Deshwal (2015) and Charan 
(2004) who put hiring external employees as a key element to SP. For Sambrook (2005), SP is a 
process that involves preparation for the correct quantity and caliber of managers and highly 
skilled employees to address retirement, death, severe illness, or advancement, as well as any 
forthcoming positions in the organizations plans. For Armstrong and Taylor (2014) SP is just part 
of the larger HR function known in the industry as talent management.  
However, some studies (e.g. Ali & Mehreen, 2019; Chan et al. 2020; Fancher, 2007; 
Seniwoliba, 2015) have argued that while SP falls under the HR’s function, incumbent leaders of 
the organization have an important role to play and their commitment matters in the successful 
institutionalization of an SP strategy. Lastly, and perhaps the widely used and accepted definition 
in SP scholarship is that of Rothwell (2001). He defines SP as "a deliberate and systematic effort 
by an organization to ensure leadership continuity in key positions, retain and develop intellectua l 
and knowledge capital for the future, and encourage individual advancement" (p. 29).  
As earlier noted, pioneering studies on SP had been focused largely on top management 
replacement. Due to this practice and limitation, SP, has, for a time being and to a certain extent, 
been synonymous and used interchangeably to replacement planning- a risk management strategy 
that is used to replace top managers before they leave their offices (Burdett, 1993). Hence, early 
scholars had used replacement planning instead of succession planning in their studies (e.g. 

7 
 
Carnazza, 1982; Kesner & Sebora, 1994). However, Rothwell and Poduch (2004), in the context 
of public management, made a distinction between the two for clearer understanding of their use 
and scope. They argue that replacement planning is just “planning for senior-level backups” and 
“just about finding replacement” while SP does not only involve replacement but also “developing 
talent and building sufficient bench strength, and preserving the organization’s institutional 
memory” (p. 405). Contrary to some criticisms in the literature, succession planning is not just 
about filling in gaps in leadership hierarchy. As Siambi (2022) puts it: “In essence, leadership 
succession planning is not mere vacancy planning but a proactive approach to systemic 
development of leadership capabilities for with a long-term perspective” (p. 18). 
Furthermore, Rothwell and Poduch (2004) even stretched SP’s definition to make a clear 
difference between technical and managerial SP. For them technical SP is about developing talents 
across the horizontal spectrum with “specialized knowledge” while managerial SP focused only 
on the vertical spectrum of the organizational chart or simply put, the leaders of an organization 
(p. 406). Here, Rothwell and Poduch challenged the traditionally held view that SP is exclusive 
only for future leadership roles, but instead widens and expands the scholarship to include other 
members of the organization by putting spotlight on the critical role they play in the continuity and 
stability of the organization.   
Elsewhere, in the context of family-owned enterprises, scholars took cognizance of the 
term written and oral succession planning referring to formal SP and informal SP (Kitayi, 2017). 
Given these definitions alongside the studies made some 50 years ago and thereafter, SP has been 
generally focused on the individuals and not on developing the competencies they ought to possess 
(Mehribini & Mohamad, 2011).

Conceptual Models 
 
From these definitions, various models designed by a handful of SP scholars have emerged to 
address the universality or the specificity of SP needs by different organizations. Most frameworks 
of succession planning have been largely designed for business sector and have been widely used 
at that. Absury (1947, in Mehrabani & Mohamad, 2011) is perhaps the first scholar to introduce 
critical elements to SP. He identified “organization analysis, selection, evaluation, development of 
executive level talent, and inventory control” (p. 372). 
Christensen (1953, in Mehrabani & Mohamad, 2011) also identified elements that must be 
included in SP and they are “identifying the pool of potential successors, the actual designation of 
the successor, and the notification of both the successor designated and other major power figures ” 
(p. 372). In the same vein, Chapman (1954, in Mehrabani & Mohamad, 2011) suggested five 
elements for executive development programs. These are elements are “defined organization plan, 
performance appraisal, established replacement tables, development of high potentials, job 
rotation, and training programs” (372).  
Notable of these models is the framework proposed by Rothwell (2001, 2005), a seven-
step framework which focuses not only on the possible replacement for leadership roles but puts 
emphasis on identifying critical roles in the organization that are most vital in sustaining stability 
and effectiveness. Employees with potential are early on identified and provided with necessary 
skills through training and development to prepare them to take on the role should vacancy due to 
various reasons (e.g. retirement, resignation, death) arise. Another key element present in this 
model is the notion of institutional knowledge transfer. Rothwell (2005) posits that not only skills 
are imperative for SP, but also the transfer of institutional knowledge. A concept akin to 
institutional memory, unique to individual leaders and employees, which is developed and 

9 
 
accumulated over their tenure within the organization. This, according to him, must be passed on 
to the next leader through a transition period, a moment when the successor is mentored and trained 
shortly before the incumbent finally leaves the office. This transition period will enable successors 
to smoothly navigate well in their new roles when they finally assume the office, thereby avoiding 
the need for costly and time-consuming onboarding trainings on the part of the organization 
(Rothwell, 2005).  
 
Finally, the model supports long-term target setting insofar as building and retaining new 
breed of leaders are concerned as it also puts premium on evaluating the effects of leadership 
transition, loss of institutional knowledge and the cost of hiring new leaders outside the 
organization.  
 
Theoretical Models 
 
In summarizing existing theoretical constructs for SP, Richards (2010) categorized theoretical 
models into three major groups namely: universalistic, contingency, and configurational. 
Universalistic approach believes in best practices and in the idea of one-size-fits-all (Hamid, 2013). 
Early studies on SP had largely advocated for the adoption of best practices across organizations. 
This suggestion of uniformity arises from the notion that some practices are way better than the 
others and hence must be embraced and adopted by other organizations (Armstrong, 2014). 
Many scholars, however, were quick to criticize the inapplicability of this approach in some 
respect. Cavanaugh (2017) argued that a universalistic approach may hinder institutions from 
creatively formulating novel, culturally and contextually relevant practices that fit their own needs. 
Kaufman (2010) also observed that while this approach has been relatively successful in the United 
State and the United Kingdom, studies outside these countries have not been corroborative in 

10 
 
proving the benefits it purports to claim. Hence the emergence of contingency and resource-based-
view theories. On the one hand, contingency theory is a human resources management construct 
that believes that enhanced organizational performance depends on the coordination of specific 
HR management methods within the organization. Simply put, SP as an approach to improve 
institutional performance, must come from within the organization and must be based on existing 
practices.  
On the other hand, resource-based-view theory has a similar perspective but emphasizes 
that in order to have a more competitive advantage, HR must use their resources to create practices 
that are unique to their organization and “cannot be easily imitated” (Siambi, 2022, p. 21). For 
SP’s context, this means that adopting the best practices approach as forwarded by the 
universalistic theory cannot hold true when we talk of the organization’s competitive advantage. 
In summary, these theories give credence to the formulation of organization specific SP plans that 
fit within their own needs, resources, and contexts. As Shamsuddin and colleagues, (2012) write: 
 
Authorities are urged to not make complete adoption of existing models, 
which do not fit well with subtleties of the local scene, resulting in 
hiccups and paralysis of the HEI’s governance and administrative organs. 
In other words, an all-encompassing mold must be cast to cater for the 
needs, context and factors that make up our HEIs, without which we will 
be encumbered by a perpetual game of merry-go-round, never finding 
the way forward (p 151). 
 
Succession Planning in Higher Education 
 
As more and more organizations face talent management and leadership crises and as empirical 
studies on SP have provided evidence supporting its benefits, organizations like medical 
institutions (Beyers, 2006; Blouin et al., 2006; Bolton & Roy, 2004; Cadmus, 2006; Crosby & 
Shields, 2010), libraries (Fallon et al,. 2013; Bridgland, 1999, Fitsomons, 2013; Huynh, 2016), 

11 
 
and higher education institutions (henceforth, HEI) have turned to SP as a strategy to mitigate 
them. 
Higher education perhaps is the last among these sectors to apply the construct of SP in 
their context. HEIs in the first world countries like the United States, United Kingdom, and Canada 
are first to introduce SP into their system largely attributed to the inevitable cascading effects of 
SP from companies and the shift of these universities from traditional educational management 
prototype to a more corporate model of administration (Thomas, 2013).  
Among the first scholars to explore SP in HEI was Richard (2009) in her study of six 
educational institutions (community and technical colleges, public research universities, and 
private universities) in the US using grounded theory as an approach, she observed that despite 
widespread leadership development activities, very few of these institutions had formal succession 
planning. She attributed this to instances of how academic governance and culture may have 
impacted the approaches to succession planning of these institutions. From these observations, she 
suggested five strategies HEI may consider in implementing deliberate and systematic succession 
plans. She called this 5Cs: Strategies for Succession Planning in the Academy:(Champions, 
Culture, Competency-based,  Communication, Continuous).  
Using these 5Cs of Richards (2009), Barton (2017) took SP into a new perspective doing 
an ethnographic case study of a US Chirstian higher education institution to come up with context- 
specific observations that could be considered in institutionalizing an intentional SP. He was 
concerned that despite Christian HEIs accounting to one fifth of the colleges and universities in 
the US, “relatively few studies have addressed succession planning… in Christ-centered higher 
education in particular.” (Barton, 2017, p. 37). His study identified nine themes related to 
succession planning culture and leadership development that he believes that the top management 

12 
 
need to consider as they have significant implications for the university’s future. These themes are 
as follow: (a) developing next leaders is the President’s to priority; (b) influential historic, cultural, 
and religious norms shape the context; (c) participants have a clear understanding of the purpose 
of the programs; (d) valuable exposure and interaction with other leaders are emphasized; (e) 
participants deeply value their engagement in programs; (f) importance of making intentional and 
ongoing plans for participants; (g) succession planning efforts are a priority for the board of 
trustees and; (h) it is critical to intentionally develop leaders who understand the changing 
landscape of higher education.  
Also using the case study lens, Swanson (2018) centered his investigation on the 
perceptions of HEI employees about SP in a community college setting. He opined that gaining 
insight into employees’ viewpoints on SP can help assess strategic importance of SP in 
organizations. Results show what most scholars similarly observed, employees at the institution 
under study revealed that there was no succession planning put in place.  
Still in the context of HEI in the US, King (2019) used mixed method in investigating how 
succession planning and leadership development are being implemented in 37 public HEIs in 
Texas. The study found that while more Texas universities were involving themselves into more 
formal leadership development programs, it was concerning, however, that there are only two 
institutions that were involved in formal succession planning, and only a meager 57% of the 37 
still lack a formal leadership development program. Here, he made a distinction between official 
succession planning and leadership programs. The former being “a deliberate plan and procedure 
for identifying successors for leadership positions, while the latter refers to “an organized program 
or initiative aimed at improving the available ‘bench-depth’ of organizational leadership” (p. 57). 
 
 Moreover, he suggested that a leadership development program of HEIs should be created 

13 
 
that is specific to each state, funded by the state, and based within the state in order to enhance the 
existing national programs.  
In an effort to comprehend how executive leadership and administrators in HEIs engaged 
in SP as part of their overall organizational strategy, Morrow (2023), employed interpretive 
phenomenological analysis to explore the experiences of HEI leaders with SP initiatives and the 
cultivation of internal leaders to fill vacant leadership positions. A significant theme that emerged 
from this analysis aligns with previous findings by other researchers, indicating that leaders have 
faced a deficit of SP strategies established in their organizations, which they attribute to a dominant 
organizational culture overshadowing a focus on developmental midnsets. 
While the general consensus among these studies suggests that a formal SP is invariably 
linked to HEI’s success, Hatcher (2023) in her scoping literature review of SP in HEIs had 
specifically identified that formal SP had significant contribution to (a) knowledge management 
and (b) employee retention in an HEI. The review also corroborated the earlier suggestions that 
top management plays a crucial role in the realization of an SP strategy in their institutions and 
human resources management should integrate and implement SP in their practice.   
Hatcher (2023) added, however, an insightful recommendation which could have been 
missed out by previous studies and that is in order for an SP to succeed HEIs must be keen in 
aligning their SP strategy with their own organizational culture specifically using the servant 
leadership as a theory in implementing SP - a relatively different take as most SP studies (e.g. 
Adadevoh et al., 2024; Ahmad et al., 2024) had used the transformative leadership paradigm as a 
lens in their inquiry. 
With most of these studies being done in the first world countries and chiefly centered on 
the western perspective, the need for new research contexts that would yield generalizability and 

14 
 
improve understanding of succession planning theories and practices has been highly opportune 
(Omar & Hassan, 2015). Studies from Africa (particularly Ghana) and Asia (chiefly Malaysia) 
have compelling findings and insights to share with the current scholarship. Studies in Malaysia 
have essentially equated succession planning to sustainable leadership as many have observed the 
impending leadership crisis across spectrum of leadership roles in Malaysian HEIs due mainly to 
changing demographics and the lack of potential leaders to replace the incumbents (Ghazali et al., 
2021). Moreover, the Malaysian government sees HEIs, in particular public HEIs, to have 
profound contribution in sustaining its high growth nation status associating succession planning 
as a cornerstone for sustainability, stability, and continuity of HEIs (Omar & Hassan, 2015). 
 They see the lack of SP in public HEI as a problem that can bring negative consequences 
including among others wasted time and resources and low employee morale due to poor decisions 
made by unsuitable candidates (Chia et al., 2021). Hence, it was no surprising when Malaysian 
authorities through its Ministry of Higher Education took cognizance of SP as an integral part of 
public HEIs management strategy and initiated a nationwide SP program for academicians in 2016 
(Chia et al., 2021).  
This is very interesting and intriguing as this is the first and only instance in the literature 
where a national government took charge of SP for HEIs as earlier studies revealed that SP has 
been primarily an institutional concern and not a bureaucratic one. This move of the federal 
government of Malaysia created the Higher Education Leadership Academy (known locally as 
AKEPT) whose primary goal is to “develop holistic, quality, respected, and competent academic 
and administrative leaders who could lead local higher learning institutions to international 
heights” (Higher Education Leadership Academy, in Chia et al., 2021, p. 51).  

15 
 
Another interesting factor that prevents other HEI for successfully implementing SP is the 
tendency of many public HEIs to reward “research excellence over leadership capabilities” (Chia 
et al., 2021, p. 54). This leads to massive exodus of talents from the universities to other institutions 
with much lucrative benefits and salary packages to offer. The idea of seniority in leadership 
succession was also found to be a limiting factor why HEIs lack legitimate SP in their context.    
In relation to leadership identification as one of the emerging themes in the literature, some 
scholars have focused on identifying competencies of individuals and proposing competency-
based model in implementing SP. Ghazhali et al., (2021) forwarded the Competency-Based 
Succession Planning framework which puts individual competency at the center of the SP 
framework and identifies leadership training, personality traits, and organizational culture as key 
elements influencing it.  
Drawing from this initial study, Ghazhali (2023) through a combination of exploratory and 
confirmatory factor analyses, statistically analyzed responses from HEI administrators and 
concluded that interpersonal, personal effectiveness, teamwork, and self-development as key 
measure of employee competency essential for SP.  
Elsewhere in the literature, the quantitative study of Bano et al., (2022) showed the positive 
correlation between succession planning strategies and employee retention signaling that a well-
established SP in an HEI can lessen employee turn-over and make employees stay longer in the 
institution. Similarly, Ishak et al., (2016) correlated key predictors for SP success in an HEI. They 
found out that knowledge management and career development significantly influence the 
successful institutionalization of SP in an HEI.

Organizational culture: Definition and Models  
 
Organizational culture (henceforth, OC) is a critical aspect of any institution, influencing its 
effectiveness, employee behavior, and overall success. Drawing from various perspectives 
(anthropology, psychology, sociology, education), the term organizational culture had been defined 
in many ways over the years by scholars to encapsulate various realities that from different 
organization context (Keerio et al., 2022). For the purpose of comparison, here are few of widely 
known definitions put forth by scholars.  
Pettigrew (1979) is perhaps one of the early scholars that investigated OC, he defined OC 
as “the system of publicly and collectively accepted meanings, operating for a given group, at a 
given time. This system of terms, forms, categories and images interpret a peoples’ own situation 
to themselves” (p. 576).  
In their extensive review of more than a hundred articles, Deshpande and Webster (1989) 
provided a similar perspective, defining OC as “the pattern of shared values and beliefs that help 
individuals understand organisational functioning and thus provide them with the norms for 
behaviour in the organisation” (p. 4).  
Similarly, Shadzad and colleagues (2012) described organizational culture as a socially 
constructed characteristic of an organization which acts a social adhesive that united employees 
and instills a sense of belonging within the organization.  
Moreover, for most researchers, organizational culture includes a shared array of values, 
behaviors, norms, attitudes, assumptions, and beliefs of the members of an organization (Dension 
et al., 2015; Belias & Koustellos, 2014; Paais & Pattiruhu, 2020; Scott & Allen, 2022). 
From these definitions, scholars have proposed various models to explain the construct of 
OC. Schein’s Model is one of the most widely recognized frameworks for understanding 

17 
 
organizational culture. Another widely recognized model is that of Hofstede’s Cultural 
Dimensions Theory. This model examines how cultural values influence organizational behavior 
(Jacob, 2023).  
Lastly, considered by some scholars (e.g. Demissie & Egziabher, 2022; Keerio et al., 2022) 
as one of the credible frameworks made is the Competing Values Framework (henceforth. CVF) 
developed by Quinn and Rohrbaugh (1983). This model proves to be highly beneficial for 
identifying a diverse array of organizational phenomena, encompassing organizational structure, 
organizational excellence, phases of life cycle progression, responsibilities of HR managers and 
leadership positions, effectiveness theories, and managerial competencies.  
From this model, Cameron and Quinn (2011) divides OC into four culture domains: “clan 
culture; adhocracy culture, market culture and hierarchical culture” (in Demissie & Egziabher, 
2022, p. 3). Organizations with a market culture focus on external success and profitability, often 
prioritizing customer satisfaction and market share. Lastly, hierarchy culture values order, 
consistency, and control. These organizations have well-defined structures, procedures, and polices 
to ensure efficiency and predictability. Stability and smooth operations are key priorities (Demissie 
& Egziabher, 2022; Keerio et al., 2022). 
In summary, OC serves as a vital determinant in shaping the effectiveness, behaviors, and 
success of institutions, scholars have defined OC from various perspectives, recognizing it as a 
complex interplay of values, beliefs, and behaviors .The models discussed above offer structured 
ways to understand and analyze OC. These frameworks emphasize how culture influences 
everything from employee engagement to organizational performance, providing a valuable lens 
through which to assess institutional dynamics.

Organizational culture in higher education  
 
While the plethora of OC studies have been focused on the business world, some scholars have 
already recognized the importance of OC in the context of HEIs. Demissie and Egziabher (2022) 
observed that HEIs are no different from business organizations. HEIs are structured organizations 
with hierarchy and guided by their mission and vision. They have employees with various beliefs 
and practices and more importantly they operate to deliver service and achieve excellence. 
 
Most studies of OC in HEIs have focused on case studies determining dominant culture(s) 
present in their institution (Demissie & Egziabher, 2022; Vasyakin et al, 2016), relating leadership 
styles, faculty performance, and OC (Jamali, et al., 2021), the role of OC in implementing changes 
in HEI (Mierzwa & Mierzwa, 2020), correlation between the type of organizational culture and 
behavior of leaders (Kaufman, 2013), comparative study between public and private HEIs’ OC 
(Debski et al. 2020), and elements of OC in HEIs (Coman, 2016).  
 
From these sample studies, it can be deduced that OC in HEIs is a significant determinant 
of institutional performance, shaping how staff, faculty, and students interact and achieve goals. 
HEIs possess a unique OC characterized by academic freedom, shared governance, and traditions 
that influence behaviors and decision making (Tierney, 1988). In higher education, OC impacts 
teaching, learning, innovation, and faculty performance. Variations in OC across institutions stem 
from history, leadership styles, and external pressures, which can create challenges in institutional 
adaptability and transformation (Kezar & Eckel, 2002). 
 
Moreover, Kezar and Eckel (2022) argue that a significant challenge facing HEI is 
maintaining alignment between organizational culture and evolving demands (i.e. globalization, 
changing student needs, technological advancements). Another issue raised in the literature is the 

19 
 
possible tension between academic and administrative culture. Bergquist and Palawak (2008) 
argue that these two cultures can create friction, as academic culture values autonomy and 
intellectual 
freedom, while 
administrative 
culture often focuses on efficiency, 
resource 
management, and accountability. 
 
Elsewhere in the literature, scholars have identified that institutional type, leadership, 
mission and vision, history and traditions, and external environment are key factors that contribute 
to the development and maintenance of organizational culture in higher education (Demissie & 
Egziabher, 2022; Jerab & Mabrouk, 2023; Coman & Bonciu, 2016; Jacob, 2013). These factors 
have substantial implications for the efficiency and overall performance of HEIs. More 
specifically, OC can influence employee and student engagement as positive and supportive 
organizational culture can result in increased motivation, job satisfaction, and academic 
achievement (Demissie & Egziabher, 2022). OC also affects institutional change and innovation 
as a culture that encourages creativity and risk-taking can facilitate innovation and adaptation to 
new challenges and opportunities (Bogale & Debela, 2024; Coman & Bonciu, 2016). Lastly, a 
strong OC that aligns with institution’s mission and goals can enhance its overall effectiveness and 
performance (Amtu et al., 2021; Demissie & Egziabher, 2022; Aryani & Widodo, 2020) 
 
In a nutshell, OC is HEI is complex and multidimensional concept, deeply influencing how 
institutions operate and respond to internal and external challenges. The ability of HEIs to adapt 
their culture to changing societal and educational expectations while preserving core values is 
crucial for their long-term success. By understanding the dynamics of OC, leaders can better 
navigate the complexities of governance, innovation, and transformation within higher education.

The Role of HEI Leaders in Succession Planning and Organizational Culture 
 
Also notable in the literature is the weight that many scholars have put in the role of leaders in SP. 
Chia et al. (2021) for example put emphasis on the role of leaders in the success of SP in HEIs 
arguing that leaders’ perspectives on SP holds great significance as their views directly impact the 
effectiveness of SP within an organization. Consequently, current leaders must seriously consider 
succession planning to ensure that universities adopt systematic and comprehensive SP practices 
and policies. Shamsuddin et al., (2012) echoed the same position explaining that today’s leaders 
are tasked with the duty of guaranteeing a seamless handover of leadership, backed by an effective 
system for developing leaders.  
For Ghazali et al., (2023), succession planning and talent management should fall within 
the ambit of the current leadership of HEIs emphasizing the role of top management in ensuring 
leadership pipeline is established. Ahmad et al., (2024) even went as far as opining that during 
recruitment top management should integrate the qualities of potential leaders required in the 
organization and this should be aligned with the organization’s objectives. They further added that 
current leadership has important role to play insofar as providing inspiration and mentorship to 
prospective leaders, hence forwarding transformational leadership as a viable leadership style to 
be applied in Malaysian public HEI context calling for current leaders to begin training “future 
leaders to succeed in leadership positions” (p. 181).  
With respect to OC, existing research has highlighted the significant importance of leaders 
in molding and influencing the OC of their respective HEIs. Leaders serve as creators of culture, 
establishing the atmosphere, conveying principles, and demonstrating behaviors that spread 

21 
 
throughout the organization. As a result, leaders are the main catalysts of change within HEIs. 
Their actions and choices can either maintain or revolutionize OC, aligning it with strategic goals 
or permitting cultural values to shift (Jerab & Mabrouk, 2023).  
 
Statement of the Problem 
 
Current literature has acknowledged the benefits of SP predominantly in the corporate world and 
constrainedly in the academe and how these constructs have largely been applied to western 
settings; however, the influence of OC in SP has not been well-established in HEIs, particularly in 
the Philippine public HEI context. The significance of this gap lies in the necessity to comprehend 
these cultural dynamics for the improvement of leadership development and the facilitation of 
smooth leadership transitions in SUCs. This, in turn, can contribute to enhancing institutional 
effectiveness and long-term sustainability. Therefore, this study attempted to answer this research 
question:  
How organizational culture influences succession planning in HEIs within the Philippine 
context, specifically in state universities and colleges.  
There is a necessity to answer this question as it will have a tremendous impact on the 
future of SUCs in the country. A sound SP will ensure that future leaders of SUCs are carefully 
and strategically identified and are exposed to leadership development programs to prepare them 
for their future roles thereby providing stability and continuity and ensuring institutional memory 
and essential organizational culture are transferred to the next batch of leaders in the pipeline.

Methods 
 
Given the complexities and intricacies of succession planning practices, as well as the influence 
of the prevailing organizational culture within SUCs and the role that their leaders play in 
leadership transitions, this study employed a qualitative approach to answer the research question. 
Specifically, thematic analysis was employed to analyze the responses of the target participants.  
 
Research Setting 
 
Currently, there are 113 state universities and colleges (henceforth, SUC) with 433 satellite 
campuses operating under the supervision of the Commission on Higher Education (CHED), 
distributed across 17 regions of the country, ranging from large institutions with multiple campuses 
to smaller regional colleges. These comprise roughly 23% of the entire HEIs in the country, both 
public and private (CHED, 2023).  
 
Each SUC operates under its respective charter enacted as a Republic Act by Congress and 
approved by the President of the Republic of the Philippines. Under these charters, SUCs are 
governed either by a Board of Regents (for state universities) or a Board of Trustees (for state 
colleges) 
composed of 11-12 members from across various 
stakeholders (i.e. alumni 
representatives, faculty union president, staff union presidents, student council president, 
University Presidents, a Senator, a House Representative, Regional Directors/Secretaries of 
National Economic Development Authority, Department of Agriculture, Department of Science 
and Technology, Generals of Philippine Air force and Philippine Navy, and private sector 
representatives). All Boards are chaired by the Chairperson of CHEd or a Commissioner-designate 

23 
 
chosen by the Chair from among the other Commissioners of CHEd.  Together, acting as a collegial 
body, they promulgate policies that will essentially set the direction of each SUC they govern.  
 
These institutions are significant players in the country’s education landscape, enrolling 
nearly 2 million students as of 2023, accounting for almost 39% of the total higher education 
population in the Philippines (CHED, 2023). For the last few years, SUCs have been the source of 
the local workforce producing roughly 300,000 graduates every year, underscoring their essential 
role in national development (CHED, 2023).  
In the Philippines, SUCs play a fundamental role in providing accessible and quality 
education across diverse regions. However, the leadership challenges these institutions face, 
including political interference, resource constraints, and a rapidly changing educational 
environment, necessitate a structured approach to leadership development, hence the importance 
of SP. The Second Congressional Commission on Education took cognizance of this when they 
put ‘building leadership capacity in SUCs and LUCs’ under ‘Efficiency of public and private 
higher education provision’ as one of the top five (5) priority areas and issues of inquiry in higher 
education in the country adding more credence to its importance.  
The dynamic and unique landscape of higher education in the Philippines necessitates a 
careful examination of SP practices in SUCs, whether informal or formal, and the role that OC 
plays in their institutionalization 
or otherwise. As SUCs face increasing demands for 
accountability, academic excellence, and global competitiveness, the institutionalization of SP, 
whether at the bureaucratic or institutional level, becomes inevitably essential and perhaps soon 
obligatory. Delving into the intricacies of SP and OC in SUCs will prove beneficial in ensuring 
that policy aligns with practices and the realities on the ground, making any attempt at policy 
reform much more responsive and contextualized.

To conclude, SUCs of today face unique challenges in leadership, particularly in 
developing and identifying future leaders, due to varying levels of autonomy, budgetary 
constraints, organizational culture, and even political interference (Palencia, 2015; Yee, 2022).

method for this study was purposive, specifically 
targeting

Instrument 
 
To capture a comprehensive view of the realities and challenges of succession planning within the 
unique organizational context of their respective SUCs, select SUC leaders were personally 
interviewed to gain their insights. This research employed semi-structured interviews with open-
ended questions, specifically designed to understand not only the direct experiences and practices 
of key institutional actors but also to reveal the underlying factors, such as cultural norms, 
bureaucratic structures, and political influences, that shape these experiences. Follow-up questions 
were asked to probe deeper into the participants’ responses, ensuring the collection of rich and 
comprehensive data. Each interview lasted approximately 45 to 60 minutes.  
Procedure 
 
 
After gaining approval from the University Research Ethics Office, the target participants 
were formally invited to participate in the study via email and official communication channels. 
The invitation included an information sheet that explained the study’s purpose, scope, potential 
risks, and benefits, as well as an informed consent form for participants to sign before the 
interview. After obtaining consent, interviews were scheduled at the participants’ convenience. The 
researcher ensured a quiet, comfortable environment for the interviews, whether conducted face-
to-face or via Zoom, depending on the interviewees’ preference.  
 
All interview transcripts and recordings were securely stored on password-protected 
devices and folders to maintain confidentiality and avoid data breaches. Each participant was 
assigned a pseudonym to ensure anonymity during data analysis and reporting.

Data Analysis

accuracy. Using critical realism as lens (Archer’s (2020) Structure, Agency, & Culture) in the

Ronkainen (2021) were employed in identifying the themes (dispositional, inferential, and 
experiential themes) from the transcript. For the accuracy of identifying themes, inter-coding was 
conducted between the researcher and a recognized expert in the field.

Formal Succession Planning Policy is not in place 
 
This theme highlights a common practice and observation in many academic institutions 
particularly in HEIs where succession planning is not governed by a well-defined, formal policy. 
(Barton, 2017; King, 2019; Morrow, 2023; Richards, 2016; Richards, 2009; Swanson, 2018). 
 Instead, leadership transitions and appointments are often guided by informal mechanism, 
cultural norms, or ad hoc practices.  This illustrates how structural gaps constrain individual and 
even collective agency to transform the organization and instead reinforce a culture of informality.

Simultaneously, this entrenched institutional norm that prioritizes tradition and informal

The absence of a clear-cut policies regarding leadership transition could potentially lead to

organizational culture that could eventually pull down its performance and derail its strategic 
direction.   More so, this could possibly encourage a culture of resistance to institutionalizing SP 
dismissing it as a mere immediate need rather than a strategic plan toward institutional stability 
and sustainability.

Since the institution lacks a formal SP policy, this leads to reliance on leaders, thereby 
revealing leader-centric practices as part of the institution's organizational culture. Given the 
predominantly hierarchical nature of leadership structure in SUCs as bureaucratic agencies, this 
lack of SP also unwittingly consolidates the power to one entity (usually the University Presidents) 
giving them wide latitude of discretionary power, enabling them to dominate succession planning 
process.

In this set-up, leaders’ agency is highly pronounced as they hold the power to shape and

greater flexibility and responsiveness, this often comes at the cost of inclusivity, transparency, and

pipeline and discourage broader involvement of community (e.g. faculty, staff, and student

Conclusion 
The findings of this study illustrate the complex and often contradictory realities surrounding 
succession planning (SP) in Philippine State Universities and Colleges (SUCs). Guided by 
Archer’s (2005) Structure, Agency, and Culture framework, the results expose how the absence of 
formal SP policies, leader-centric decision-making, political interference, and bureaucratic 
constraints collectively sustain a fragmented and informal system of leadership transition. This 
interplay of structural gaps, cultural norms, and individual agency reveals an organizational culture 
that is simultaneously resistant to formal change yet increasingly aware of its institutional 
vulnerabilities. 
The persistence of informal, personality-driven succession practices reinforces short-term, 
leader-dependent governance at the expense of long-term institutional sustainability. Moreover, 
the entanglement of both external and internal political forces in leadership selection undermines 

38 
 
efforts to create transparent, merit-based, and participatory SP processes. These realities place 
Philippine SUCs at a disadvantage compared to international higher education institutions that 
have embraced systematic, competency-driven SP models. 
Nonetheless, the study also surfaces emerging pockets of reform. The expressed 
commitment to institutional development, recognition of policy gaps, and growing efforts to 
involve stakeholders in leadership selection represent promising cultural shifts towards building a 
more resilient, transparent, and sustainable leadership pipeline. These developments signal the 
possibility—albeit gradual and uncertain—of institutionalizing SP as both a strategic and cultural 
priority. 
Ultimately, the results underscore the need for Philippine SUCs to move beyond 
compliance-driven, politically influenced structures and foster leadership transitions anchored on 
inclusivity, meritocracy, and strategic foresight. Doing so requires not only structural reforms but 
also a deliberate cultural transformation that reframes leadership as a shared, institutionally 
embedded responsibility rather than an individual prerogative. As this study suggests, while deep-
seated challenges persist, pathways toward a more accountable and future-proof leadership system 
are beginning to emerge—one that recognizes the inextricable relationship between structure, 
culture, and agency in shaping the future of higher education leadership in the Philippines. 
Implications 
 
The study’s findings reveal significant implications for succession planning in Philippine 
SUCs. The absence of formal SP policies, inconsistent leadership selection criteria, and political 
influences highlight the urgent need for policy reforms, leadership development programs on 

39 
 
institutional and bureaucratic levels, and mechanisms for stakeholder engagement. Structural 
reform and cultural shift to transparency, meritocracy and collaboration are essential for ensuring 
leadership sustainability.  
 
This research also extends the recent scholarship on SP and OC by providing a localized 
perspective where political dynamics, cultural norms, and structural gaps play significant roles. 
The findings likewise highlight the importance of contextualizing SP theories to accommodate the 
unique sociopolitical and cultural realities of Philippine SUCs. 
 
 By unearthing the pervasive role of politics in SP, this study bridges political and 
organizational theories by demonstrating that leadership transition in HEIs are not solely 
organizational decisions but are influenced by broader political and power structures. Studies on 
this interdisciplinary are may be conducted in the future to expand our understanding of SP as a 
multifaceted bureaucratic process in HEIs.   
 
In the broader context, the results show the importance of leadership trait as a critical factor 
to institutional adaptability and strategic foresight in HEIs viewing leadership not just as a 
technical competency but as a relational and culturally embedded practice.
