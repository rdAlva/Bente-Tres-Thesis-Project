ABSTRACT
Health information systems are a key building block of a well-functioning health system and are a vital component in the
implementation of universal health coverage. The Philippines’s current transition toward universal health coverage had attendant
health information system reforms, such as the establishment of the country’s National Health Data Repository and the Health
Sector Enterprise Architecture. However, many issues remain in this transition, such as insufficient investments in health
information systems, poor reliability of health information systems, technical support delays, data quality issues, and cybersecurity
issues. We recommend making health data portable throughout the health system while adhering to data privacy laws, investing
in internet connectivity, information technology, and capacity building for human resources, and improving implementation of
current policies and guidelines to fully harness the potential of health information systems in making universal health coverage a
reality in the Philippines.
1
Introduction
A well-functioning health information system (HIS) is a key
building block of a strong health system. It is essential in
keeping track of patient history, in disease surveillance, in
processing of claims and co-payments, in predicting and fore-
casting demand for services, and in the monitoring, eval-
uation, and continued improvements of health policies and
programs [1]. Without an integrated and responsive HIS, it is
difficult to monitor patient pathways for continuity of care,
conduct disease surveillance, process provider payments, forecast
demand for services and resources, and track health systems
performance [1].
Despite substantial advancements in HIS and the ubiquity of
electronic medical records (EMRs), data availability for universal
health coverage (UHC) indicators is only 70% in the Western
Pacific Region, which affects healthcare planning and service
delivery. For this reason, the Global Strategy on Digital Health
2020–2025 recognizes that a responsive HIS is a catalyst to meet-
ing UHC and global health targets, whereas acknowledging the
challenges faced by low- and middle-income countries (LMICs)
with significant resource, governance, health system, behavioral,
organizational, and technical constraints [2].
This paper aims to document recent HIS reforms in the Philip-
pines, describe challenges in health information management

2
UHC and Recent HIS Reforms in the
Philippines
In the Philippines, the passage of the UHC Law in 2019 ushered
in rapid developments in the country’s HIS environment. As
mandated in its implementing rules and regulations, all health
service providers and insurers are now required to maintain an
HIS covering enterprise resource, human resources for health,
EMRs, and electronic prescription log, which shall be electron-
ically uploaded on a regular basis through an interoperable
system called the National Health Data Repository (NHDR) [3].
The NHDR is envisioned as the authoritative repository of the
country’s health and health-related data. It will serve as the basis
of health policy and standards, decision-making, health program
planning and implementation, health systems strengthening,
and monitoring and evaluation. Furthermore, the Health Sector
Enterprise Architecture (HSEA) is being developed to standardize
and organize the information and communications technology
infrastructure of the country, ensure its alignment to the aim of
UHC, and support digital transformation of the healthcare sector.
These reforms are key to unify and harmonize HIS in the country,
with standards enforced to ensure quality and interoperability
across various public and private systems [4].
3
Health Information Management Challenges
in the Philippines
3.1
Insufficient Investments in HISs
In a rapid survey we conducted with 20 purposively-selected
local government units (LGUs) in the Philippines for project
planning purposes (Supporting Information File 1), only 10 (50%)
have budget allotment for HIS operations and maintenance,
and only one mentioned having dedicated space, IT personnel,
and a permanent trained encoder running the LGU’s HIS. Data
management and encoding of health information from many
government facilities are still being done by midwives, nurses,
and physicians, increasing their workload and giving them less
time to care for patients. In many less-urbanized areas in the
country, paper-based recording and reporting of health events are
still prevalent. The lack of HIS investments of LGUs highlights
the unrecognized importance of enhancing capacities and infras-
tructure for health data management. Because of the shortages
in human and information technology resources due to chronic
underinvestment, many LGUs will have difficulty implementing
these new HIS reforms.
3.2
Poor Reliability of HISs
Many of our survey respondents reported that the HIS they
are using commonly experience server downtimes, which is
due to poor internet connectivity or bugs in the system. Some
HIS platforms are still under development and require systems
updates, which would necessitate retraining for users. Lastly,
respondents also reported the HIS platform’s inability to operate
as a standalone system as a challenge, indicating the need to have
various platforms installed to generate different reports or process
claims.
3.3
Technical Support Delays
In the same survey, only three (15%) have dedicated local technical
support teams; others have to call a central office technical
support team, which could mean significant delays. Seven (35%)
LGUs receive response to their technical support queries within
the day, five (25%) receive response within 1 to 2 days, whereas
six (30%) receive technical support anywhere from 3 to 5 days.
One (5%) LGU, located in a mountainous area in the Philippines,
reported that they waited for more than a month before receiving
technical support. These delays create inefficiencies in the health-
care sector, with health providers reverting to paper-based data
collection and management.
3.4
Data Quality Issues
Of 20 respondent LGUs, 12 (60%) do not have an established
protocol for validating the data collected, raising concerns
about data quality. Among the eight (40%) LGUs that did,
data validation methods included reviewing data during input,
interviewing patients, or during program implementation and/or
system review. These quality issues affect estimates on disease
burden and UHC indicators such as coverage and quality of care,
which ultimately impact how services are designed, promoted,
delivered, and financed [5, 6].
3.5
Lack of Data Portability and Interoperability
of HIS
Ideally, when a patient is referred from one hospital to another,
his/her data should accompany him/her as well, providing
continuity of care from one health facility to another. However,
health data ownership in the Philippines typically lies with the
providers, and patients will have to get permission from the
provider to share their health data when they go to another facility
[7]. Further aggravating this issue is the lack of interoperability
of the different HIS currently existing in the country, despite the
guidelines explicitly stating that they have to be interoperable [8].
As a result, the patient may not have continuity of care as he/she
navigates through the health system.
3.6
Cybersecurity Issues
There have been many verified cybersecurity breaches of health
and insurance data in the Philippines [9]. Beyond these national
agencies, we have also identified cybersecurity vulnerabilities in
our respondent LGUs. In our survey, 12 (60%) LGUs store health
data in only one platform—either their desktop computer in
the health facility or a server linked to the EMR. Implementing

4
Recommendations
The promise of UHC in the Philippines through HIS reforms
is tremendous; however, many challenges remain that require
a whole-of-society approach to address [2, 10]. We provide
recommendations backed by successful experience from other
countries:
4.1
Making Health Data Portable Throughout
the Health System While Adhering to Data Privacy
Laws
Previous research studies have highlighted the vital role of
standardized data collection and transmission systems, universal
identifiers for individual patients, and modernized working
practices to facilitate health data portability as patients navi-
gate throughout the health system [11]. In the Philippines, the
closest universal identifier that could be used is the national
identification system, and it can be integrated into the country’s
various HISs to facilitate UHC implementation; however, data
privacy concerns persist, which preclude its use. The approach
used by some HISs in Europe, in compliance with their General
Data Protection Regulation, includes k-anonymization, masking
patient and admission identifiers, deleting all direct identifiers,
and removing images and free text except for laboratory variables
[12].
4.2
Investing in Internet Connectivity, IT, and
Capacity Building for the Health Workforce
A key facilitator during the early phase of transition to electronic
HIS in the United States was substantial financial investments,
which allocated federal budget for IT resources and training their
health workforce with the overall goal of improving quality of care
[13]. However, in the Philippine context, financial investments
should come not just from the national government but also
from the local government, which is empowered through the
Local Government Code to design and manage their own health
systems [14] and, as a result, their own local HIS. Through
guidance from the Department of Interior and Local Government,
there could be roadmaps for LGUs for transitioning from a paper-
based system toward electronic HIS. These roadmaps should
describe which IT infrastructure and training needs should be
addressed at each stage of the transition [2]. This will also
entail concomitant investments from private telecommunication
providers, to ensure reliable internet connectivity even in less
urbanized areas. To encourage the private sector to do this, tax
breaks or government subsidies could be given.
4.3
Improving Implementation of Current
Policies
Guidelines for interoperability, data quality, and reducing cyber-
security vulnerabilities are already present in various policy
documents in the Philippines [8, 4]. However, these are not
implemented properly because personal information controllers
and processors in the country may not be aware of such guidelines
or do not have the necessary skills or resources to implement it.
This necessitates national government agencies to streamline the
dissemination of pertinent guidelines for interoperability, data
quality, and cybersecurity toward local government health cen-
ters and continuously invest in capacity building to implement
the said guidelines.
5
Conclusions
These nascent HIS reforms and the challenges in implementing
them are part of the birthing pains in the transition toward an
integrated health system necessary in making UHC possible.
These issues and the gravity of the necessary reforms highlight
the need for different national government agencies, local govern-
ments, as well as partners in the private sector to come together
and invest in information technology and the infrastructure and
human resources necessary to improve HIS for UHC.
