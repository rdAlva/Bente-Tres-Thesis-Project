A B S T R A C T
This study investigates the processes driving interannual variability of rainfall over the western coast of the 
Philippines during the peak southwest monsoon (SWM) months (July–September) from 1961 to 2022. Specif­
ically, it describes how tropical cyclones (TC), SWM, and TC-enhanced SWM influence SWM rainfall variability. 
Rainfall measurements from 11 stations along the western coast of the Philippines were classified into three 
types: direct TC rainfall (PD), indirect TC rainfall (PI), and SWM rainfall (PSM). The yearly contributions of PD, PI, 
and PSM to SWM rainfall were estimated to be 15.4 %, 33.1 %, and 51.5 %, respectively. Furthermore, four 
rainfall variability types were found depending on the characteristics of each year’s rainfall anomalies: enhanced 
PD but suppressed PSM (D+S-), suppressed PD but enhanced PSM (D-S+), enhanced PI but suppressed PSM (I+S-), 
and suppressed PI but enhanced PSM (I-S+). Composite analysis using ERA5 data revealed the presence of 
anomalous winds and circulations unique to each type that causes variations in the TC tracks and the monsoon 
flow, which accounts for the rainfall variability. Notable ones are the anomalous anticyclone over Taiwan in the 
D+S- case, which pushes TCs closer to the Philippines, and the anomalous anticyclone east of the Philippines in 
the I+S- case, which enhances the trade winds.
1. Introduction
The Philippines receives around 965 to 4064 mm of rain every year 
(PAGASA, 2018; Olaguera and Manalo, 2024a). However, the spatial 
and temporal distribution of this rainfall is not uniform. Depending on 
the time of year, some regions of the Philippines experience more 
rainfall than others (Kripalani and Kulkarni, 1998). For example, the 
western coast of the country receives 40–80 % of its annual rainfall 
during the southwest monsoon (SWM) season, while the eastern coast 
receives 40–80 % of its annual rainfall during the northeast monsoon 
(NEM) season (Olaguera et al., 2022a, 2022b). Another source of rainfall 
in the Philippines are tropical cyclones (TCs), which mainly occur dur­
ing the months of July, August, and September (JAS), and provide 
around 35 % of the annual rainfall (Cinco et al., 2016). Despite possibly 
causing floods and landslides, rainwater from these weather systems is 
necessary for domestic and agricultural use, recharge of water reser­
voirs, and hydropower generation.
The SWM in the Philippines, locally known as “Habagat”, is part of 
the western North Pacific summer monsoon (WNPSM), a component of 
the Asian monsoon (Matsumoto, 1992; Murakami and Matsumoto, 
1994). The Asian monsoon includes the Indian summer monsoon (ISM), 
the East Asian monsoon (EAM), and the WNPSM. The monsoon is caused 
by the differential heating between the landmass of mainland Asia, and 
the surrounding oceans. Since the Asian landmass is warmer than the 
oceans around it during boreal summer, the winds in the region blow 
from the ocean to the land (Das, 1986). The onset of the SWM in the

western North Pacific (WNP) typically marks the start of the rainy sea­
son in the Philippines (PAGASA, 2018). This onset occurs around mid- 
May and is usually triggered by three events: the shift of the Western 
North Pacific Subtropical High (WNPSH) to the northeast, the evolution 
of the monsoon trough over South China Sea, and the great approach of 
the easterly wave (Akasaka et al., 2007; Akasaka, 2010; Olaguera et al., 
2021; Olaguera and Manalo, 2024a). During mid-June, the monsoon 
trough deepens and migrates northward to the central Philippines. By 
early July, the southwesterly winds become predominant, which in­
dicates that the SWM has been fully established. The monsoon flow 
continues to strengthen through late July until its peak in early August 
(Matsumoto and Olaguera, 2020). The SWM starts its retreat during 
mid-October and is characterized by a gradual decrease in rainfall in the 
western coast of the Philippines, an increase in rainfall in the eastern 
coast of the Philippines, the weakening of the westerlies, the enhance­
ment of the easterlies, and the southward migration of the monsoon 
trough, and the WNPSH (Kubota et al., 2017; Olaguera and Manalo, 
2024b).
On the other hand, TCs are large, non-frontal storm systems that are 
accompanied by spiral rain bands, which bring torrential rain and strong 
winds, and are usually characterized by a closed low-pressure center 
(Doocy et al., 2013). The Philippines is situated in the WNP basin, which 
is the most active TC basin where around one-third of the total number 
of TCs in the world form every year. On average, around 26 TCs occur in 
this basin annually (Lee et al., 2020). Of the ~19 TCs that enter the 
Philippine Area of Responsibility (PAR) and affect the Philippines, nine 
TCs make a direct landfall (Cinco et al., 2016). Typhoons in the WNP can 
form any time of year but the peak of the typhoon season is usually July 
to October (PAGASA, 2024). According to Corporal-Lodangco and Leslie 
(2017), 48.8 % of all Philippine TCs (i.e. TCs that enter PAR) in a year 
occur during the months of July, August, and September (JAS). Even 
though these months have the highest frequency of TCs, this season has 
the lowest landfall percentage with only 24 % of the TCs making land­
fall. During these months, weak easterlies, a deep monsoon trough, and 
a subtropical anticyclone to the far northeast of the Philippines cause the 
TCs to recurve, leading to less Philippine landfall (Corporal-Lodangco 
and Leslie, 2017).
The peak of the typhoon season coincides with the peak SWM 
months. This leads to the interaction between the two weather systems. 
In some cases, TCs are able to “enhance” the SWM. This usually happens 
when a TC is located to the northeast of the Philippines during the peak 
SWM months, bringing heavy rainfall especially over the western coast 
of the Philippines (Cayanan et al., 2011; Bagtasa, 2019). The most recent 
example is Typhoon Gaemi in 2024, which did not make landfall in the 
Philippines but still brought about extremely high amounts of rain over 
the country due to its enhancement of the SWM. According to the 
Department of Science and Technology-Philippine Atmospheric, 
Geophysical, and Astronomical Services Administration (DOST- 
PAGASA), the TC-enhanced monsoon brought 471 mm of rainfall in 
Quezon City, Philippines, over a period of 24 h on 23–24 July 2024. For 
comparison, the climatological mean rainfall for the entire month of 
July in Quezon City is 493.3 mm (PAGASA, 2015). This means that the 
TC-enhanced monsoon event brought nearly a month’s worth of rainfall 
in just a span of 24 h. The widespread flooding in numerous areas in 
Luzon led to the deaths of 48 people and $145 million worth of damages 
(NDRRMC, 2024). Two other examples of this phenomenon are the 
“enhanced Habagat” cases of 2012 and 2013, which occurred from 6–10 
August 2012 and 18–22 August 2013, respectively. The total rainfall 
measured by the Science Garden station of PAGASA during the entire 
duration of these two events were 1007.4 mm and 1067.4 mm, 
respectively (Lagmay et al., 2015). Bagtasa (2023) explained that the 
above-normal rainfall during these two events were caused by the 
“moisture conveyor belt” (MCB) traversing the Philippines. The MCB is a 
long region of high moisture flux that stretches from the Indochina 
Peninsula, over the South China Sea, and onto the Philippine Sea, due to 
the enhancement of the SWM by a TC. The MCB was formed from the 
deepening of the Asian monsoon trough created by the TCs to the 
northeast of the Philippines and the low-pressure systems off the coast of 
the Vietnam-China border.
While there have been studies that have analyzed the climatology of 
the SWM rainfall in the Philippines, few have specifically focused on the 
TC-enhanced SWM. For example, Cruz et al. (2013) examined the 
climatology of the Philippine SWM rainfall but did not address the in­
fluence of passing tropical cyclones in their analysis. Their study showed 
a decreasing trend in rainfall associated with the SWM from 1961 to 
2010. They also found that the number of “no rain” days has increased 
while the days with rainfall greater than the 95th percentile have 
decreased, suggesting a longer dry period for the past 50 years. Studies 
on the TC-enhanced SWM usually focus on specific enhanced SWM days 
or heavy precipitation events (HPE) (e.g., Cayanan et al. (2011), and 
Bagtasa (2019)). Cayanan et al. (2011) identified four heavy rainfall 
cases, three of which had a TC present inside the PAR. Their study 
concluded that winds generated by the TC contribute to the winds from 
the SWM, thus creating stronger southwesterlies. These enhanced 
southwesterlies, when forced upwards by the mountain ranges of Luzon 
Island, will have a higher vertical velocity, which leads to stronger 
convection and more intense rainfall over Luzon Island. Bagtasa (2019)
identified all the HPE days, which is defined as days with rainfall in the 
upper 85th percentile, from 1958 to 2017. The study found that the 
majority of the HPE days involved a TC to the northeast of the 
Philippines, which led to the formation of the MCB that brought heavy 
rainfall over the Philippines. Kudo et al. (2014) and Fujiwara et al. 
(2017) both conducted a case study on Typhoon Man-yi and similarly 
discussed the presence of the MCB over the Philippines.
This study aims to illustrate the processes driving the interannual 
variability of rainfall over the western coast of the Philippines during the 
SWM season using long-term rainfall and atmospheric data spanning 62 
years from 1961 to 2022. Specifically, it examines how TCs, SWM and 
their interaction influence rainfall variability. Unlike previous works, 
this study analyzes the climatology of the enhanced SWM, not just the 
“no TC” SWM, nor specific TC-enhanced cases. Here, annual rainfall 
during the SWM season over the western coast of the Philippines will be 
classified as direct TC rainfall (PD), indirect TC rainfall (PI), and SWM 
rainfall (PSM), in order to determine the dominant rainfall type for each 
year in the analysis period. The large-scale processes during the years 
with anomalous rainfall values will be investigated in order to determine 
the cause for the year-to-year variability in the three types of rainfall. 
This procedure was done by Chen et al. (2010a) for the island of Taiwan, 
but there are no studies as of yet for the Philippines. The rest of the paper 
is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the various datasets used in 
the analysis, as well as the methodology for this study. Section 3 presents 
the results of the analysis on the different rainfall types and the dis­
cussions regarding the composites created. The summary and conclu­
sions of the study are in Section 4.
2. Data and Methodology
2.1. Data
The analysis period spans from 1961 to 2022. Only the months of 
July, August, and September (JAS) were included in the analysis, which 
is the peak SWM season in the Philippines (Lyon and Camargo, 2009). 
The following three datasets are used: 
a. Daily rainfall measurements from 11 stations provided by the DOST- 
PAGASA. These stations (Ambulong, Baguio City, Coron Island, Cuyo 
Island, Dagupan City, Iba, Iloilo City, Laoag City, Port Area, Sangley 
Point, and Science Garden) were chosen (Fig. 1) since they have no 
more than 20 % missing data during the analysis period. They are 
also located in the western portion of the Philippines, which is most 
exposed to the SWM, with a climate that is classified as Type I in the

2.2. Methodology
The daily rainfall data from each station was separated into three 
types: PD, PI, and PSM. The PD was determined by identifying the “direct 
TC days”, which were defined as the days wherein a TC was located 
within 500 km centered at any of the 11 PAGASA stations (see Fig. 1). 
This specific distance was chosen as it is the extent of a TC’s direct 
rainbands (Yokoyama and Takayabu, 2008). Related studies such as Lau 
et al. (2008), Nogueira and Keim (2010), Prat and Nelson (2013), Dare 
et al. (2012), and Khouakhi et al. (2017) to name a few, also used this 
specific distance in order to identify the extent of a TC’s rainfall. While 
some studies such as Kubota and Wang (2009) showed that TC rainfall 
can reach up to 1000 km away from the TC’s center, this study used the 
500-km radius instead in order to make sure that the recorded rainfall is 
really from the TC itself. Although it is true that a TC may produce rain 
up to 1000 km away from its center, that radius may be too large and 
may mistakenly identify TC-enhanced SWM rain as direct TC rain. 
Additionally, JTWC defines a medium-size TC as a TC with a radius of 3◦
to 6◦. Assuming a radius of 500 km, which is around 4.5◦, puts it very 
close to the middle of JTWC’s TC size chart (JTWC, 2017). The PI rainfall 
was determined by identifying the “indirect TC days”, which were 
defined as the days wherein a TC was inside a box bounded by the lat­
itudes 6.34◦N and 30◦N, and the longitudes 110◦E and 135◦E (Fig. 1), 
but outside the 500 km radius of any of the 11 stations. The eastern 
border of this domain was chosen in order to line up with the eastern 
border of the PAR. The northern and western borders of this domain 
were based on the extent of the TC points during high precipitation 
event (HPE) days in Bagtasa (2019), since these HPE days were caused 
by a TC indirectly affecting the Philippines by enhancing the SWM. The 
southern border of the domain is exactly 500 km south of the south­
ernmost PAGASA station used. Lastly, PSM was rainfall observed by the 
stations while there were no TCs present inside the domain described 
above. In this study, only the TCs that were at least of Tropical Storm 
strength in the Saffir-Simpson scale (≥34 knots) were used to identify 
the direct and indirect TC days. Rainfall caused by Tropical Depressions 
and low pressure systems during the analysis period were classified as 
SWM rainfall.
In order to obtain one single rainfall value for each day in the 
analysis period, the rainfall values of the 11 stations were averaged per 
day. This singular value is representative of the rainfall experienced over 
the western coast of the Philippines. Each daily rainfall value was either 
classified as PD, PI, and PSM depending on whether the day was a direct, 
indirect, or no TC day. All the PD, PI, and PSM values during JAS were 
added in order to obtain a yearly value for each rainfall type for all the 
years from 1961 to 2022.
SWM rainfall over the Philippines has frequently shown interannual 
variability (Kripalani and Kulkarni, 1997). The yearly anomalies and 
standard deviations (SD) for each rainfall type were obtained. The 
rainfall type anomalies were calculated by subtracting the 1961 to 2022 
mean of that rainfall type from the yearly values of the same rainfall 
type. In order to determine which years had significant anomalies, a 0.6 
SD threshold was created around the mean of each rainfall type. Various 
SD thresholds have been used in previous studies to determine rainfall 
anomalies such as Chen et al. (2008, 2010a, 2010b), which used 0.66, 
0.8, and 0.7 SD, respectively. Garfinkel and Hartmann (2008) used a 
threshold of 0.6 SD in order to determine whether a month is classified 
as an El Ni˜no-Southern Oscillation (ENSO) month or Quasi-Biennial 
Oscillation (QBO) month. In this study, the 0.6 SD threshold was used 
in order to maximize the sample size for each rainfall variability type. 
The years with anomalies above and below that threshold were only 
considered in the composite analysis later.
Based on the yearly anomalies, we can define eight rainfall vari­
ability types, which are the D+S+, D+S-, D-S+, D–S–, I+S+, I+S-, I- 
S+, and I–S–. The “D”, “I”, and “S” stand for direct TC, indirect TC, and 
SWM rainfall, respectively. The “+” and “-” symbols signify whether the 
direct TC, indirect TC, or SWM rainfall for that specific year is anoma­
lously high or anomalously low, respectively. A summary of these 
rainfall variability types is shown in Table 1.
This procedure is similar to the one used by Chen et al. (2010a), in 
which the yearly rainfall of Taiwan during JAS months was categorized 
into T+, T-, S+ and S- types using the yearly anomalies and the standard 
deviations. We extended their procedure by adding the PI rainfall type to 
account for the indirect TC cases and classifying it further into I+ and I- 
years.
Composites of various atmospheric variables were created for each of 
the rainfall variability types that had a sufficient sample size. A boot­
strap t-test (Efron and Tibshirani, 1994) was used to test the significance 
of all composites relative to all JAS days during the analysis period (e.g., 
Olaguera et al. 2022b). The regular Student’s t-test was repeated 1000 
times, each iteration with a different, random sample. In this study, the 
rainfall variability types with at least three years were used. Composite 
anomalies of the 850 hPa stream function and 850 hPa horizontal winds 
were used to show the anomalous circulations and anomalous wind

3. Results and Discussion
3.1. Climatological conditions
The climatological conditions over the WNP during the JAS season 
from 1961 to 2022 are shown in Fig. 2 for various atmospheric variables. 
The SWM can be seen in Fig. 2a, as it flows from the Bay of Bengal, 
passing over the Indochina Peninsula, and moves towards the 
Philippines. East of 140◦E, these southwesterlies converge with the 
southeasterly winds driven by the anticyclonic rotation of the WNPSH, 
which is depicted by the 5870 gpm contour line. Regions of strong up­
ward motion can be found near the coast of Myanmar, the western coast 
of the Philippines, and over the Pacific (from 5◦N to 20◦N and 125◦E to 
160◦E) (Fig. 2a). The upward motion in Myanmar and the Philippines 
are due to the southwesterly monsoon winds interacting with the 
various mountain ranges present in the respective regions. These 
mountain ranges force the southwesterly winds upward due to the 
orographic lifting effect. This creates convection and precipitation over 
the region as the warm, moist air that is forced upward condenses as it 
cools. The area of upward motion in the Pacific Ocean is due to the 
convergence of the southwesterly monsoon winds and the easterly trade 
winds in a region called the monsoon trough. Due to the region’s 
frequent convection, this region is conducive for TC development.
Next, we examine the climatological 850 hPa stream function, 700 
hPa relative humidity, and moisture flux (Fig. 2b, c). In a stream func­
tion plot, positive values usually depict an area of anticyclonic circula­
tion while negative values usually depict an area of cyclonic circulation. 
The presence of the WNPSH can be seen as the large area of positive 
stream function southeast of Japan. Another area of positive stream

High moisture flux values are present over the Bay of Bengal as seen 
in the region of dark blue shadings south of 20◦N and west of 95◦E 
(Fig. 2c). High values of moisture flux mean that the air in this region is 
carrying significant amounts of water vapor, which may lead to pre­
cipitation once the water vapor condenses. This region of high moisture 
flux values are likely due to the ISM, which affects the northern Indian 
Ocean during the JAS months. A “tongue” of high moisture flux values 
can also be seen protruding from the Bay of Bengal and extending to­
wards the South China Sea and the western coast of the Philippines, 
traversing the Indochina Peninsula. This extension is usually where the 
SWM that affects the Philippines is present. It is also where the MCB 
passes through, based on previous studies (Kudo et al., 2014; Fujiwara 
et al., 2017; Bagtasa, 2019). The moist air brought about by the easterly 
trade winds can also be seen as the region with fairly high moisture flux 
values at around 20◦N and to the east of 150◦E. Finally, the EAM can be 
seen as the southwesterly water vapor flux traveling over the Korean 
Peninsula and Japan.
Along with moisture flux, another parameter that reflects rainfall 
over a certain region is vertically integrated moisture flux convergence 
(VIMFC). This parameter has been shown to be a good predictor of 
thunderstorms (Zomeren and van Delden, 2007); therefore, it was used 
in this study to determine regions with high chances of intense rainfall. 
High values of VIMFC can be seen in the western coasts of Myanmar and 
the Philippines (Fig. 2d). The heightened VIMFC values in these two 
regions are due to the moisture-laden air from the monsoons undergoing 
orographic lifting. The western coasts of Myanmar and Luzon Island are 
lined with various mountain ranges which force the moist winds up­
wards. This leads to convection and causes the moisture to build up and 
converge on the windward side of the mountain ranges. This creates 
precipitation once the moist air reaches a certain height, cools, and 
condenses.
High surface latent heat flux values indicate regions of high energy 
transfer from the ocean’s surface to the atmosphere due to evaporation. 
Similar to the moisture flux and VIMFC composites, surface latent heat 
flux composites are also able to show regions with a high moisture 
content, which may lead to possible precipitation. The overall shape of 
the region with high surface latent heat flux values (Fig. 2e) follows the 
overall shape of the region with high moisture flux values (Fig. 2c). The 
high surface latent heat flux values in the Indian Ocean are due to the 
ISM, while a similar “tongue” can be found extending towards the South 
China Sea and the western coast of the Philippines, which is due to the 
SWM.
Two important factors in the formation of TCs are SSTs and vertical 
wind shear (Wallace and Hobbs, 2006). Warm SSTs are needed for the 
TC to gather enough moisture and create enough convection for the TC 
to form and intensify, while low vertical wind shear is important in TC 
development since strong vertical wind shear is able to damage the TC 
structure and separate the low level circulation from its main convective 
cluster (Lam and Kong, 2022). Various studies have also stressed the role 
of vertical wind shear in TC formation and development. For example, 
Zehr (1992) stated that tropical cyclogenesis does not occur in a region 
with a vertical wind shear intensity of 12.5 m s−1. Gallina and Velden 
(2002) concluded that WNP TCs generally weaken when experiencing a 
vertical wind shear of at least 9 m s−1. Wong and Chan (2004) concluded 
that TCs significantly weaken when experiencing a vertical wind shear 
of at least 10 m s−1. Their study also stated the importance of TC size 
when analyzing the effects of vertical wind shear. TCs with smaller radii 
tend to have a lower vertical wind shear threshold where they start to 
weaken.
The climatological SST of the WNP is very high during JAS months, 
reaching around 29 ◦C in the ocean waters south of 20◦N (Fig. 2f). This 
allows TCs to form, especially in the tropical region. As the latitude 
increases, the SST decreases, with the SST at around 45◦N reaching as 
low as 10 ◦C. SST also plays a role in the onset of the SWM. Wu and Wang 
(2001) concluded that the northeastward advance of the SWM follows 
the northeastward migration of the warm SST tongue in the WNP. The 
shift in the location of the warm center causes a shift in the region of 
convective instability and low-level convergence. Meanwhile, the ver­
tical wind shear composites show two regions of high vertical wind 
shear: one is west of the Indochina Peninsula, while the other is to the 
north of Japan and the Korean Peninsula (Fig. 2g). The high vertical 
wind shear west of the Indochina Peninsula is caused by the tropical 
easterly jet stream over the region during boreal summer. The high 
vertical wind shear north of Japan and the Korean Peninsula is caused by 
the East Asian westerly jet stream. Despite these regions of high vertical 
wind shear, a northwest-southeast oriented region of very low vertical 
wind shear can be seen in the Pacific Ocean south of 25◦N and east of 
120◦E.

3.2. The three SWM rainfall types over the Philippines
The composites in the previous section were able to show the sources 
of the rainfall experienced by the Philippines during the JAS months. 
Rainfall from the SWM can be seen as the moisture-laden air traversing 
the South China Sea and reaching the western coast of the Philippines. 
Meanwhile, the numerous TCs that come in close proximity to the 
Philippines are also able to bring significant amounts of rainfall over the 
country. The different sources of rainfall is the reason why, in this study, 
the yearly rainfall during the SWM season is separated into three cate­
gories: PD, PI, and PSM. Yearly means of the PD, PI, and PSM for each 
station were calculated and are shown in Table 2. The table is arranged 
in descending order according to the latitude of the station. This is done 
in order to show the spatial trend of each rainfall type. The yearly 
average values for each rainfall type is also shown at the bottom of the 
table. It can be noticed that the PD and PI values of the stations closer to 
the equator are lower than the PD and PI values of the stations further up 
north. This may be directly attributed to the behavior of the TCs during 
this time of year. TC’s generally tend to pass closer to the northernmost 
parts of the Philippines, as seen in Fig. 2h. This means that the northern 
stations, on average, would receive more TC-related rainfall, whether 
direct or indirect, compared to the southern stations.
The yearly breakdown of the three rainfall types is shown in Fig. 3. It 
can be seen that PSM contributes the most, while PD contributes the least 
to the yearly SWM rainfall amount. PD, PI, and PSM account for 15.4 %, 
33.1 %, and 51.5 % of the total yearly SWM rainfall, respectively. It is

worth noting that four years in the analysis period had rainfall values

and 82.5 %, respectively, which clearly shows the drastic change in TC- 
related rainfall.
However, these percentages do not accurately depict the intensity of 
each rainfall type as the yearly totals of each rainfall type depend on the 
number of direct and indirect TC days per year. Out of the 92 days within 
the JAS months, there were, on average, 6.5 direct TC days and 24.8 
indirect TC days each year. The remaining days are classified as no TC 
days. Dividing the yearly PD, PI, and PSM values by the number of direct, 
indirect, and no TC days, respectively, gives the daily PD, PI, and PSM 
values, which are 35.6, 19.8, and 12.6 mm, respectively.
The low number of direct TC days is in line with the findings of 
Corporal-Lodangco and Leslie (2017), which showed that JAS TCs 
usually do not make landfall in the Philippines. The TC density map 
shown in Fig. 2h also suggests the same. TCs during JAS months usually 
pass through a region east of Taiwan. This region can be seen in Fig. 2h 
as the grid box bounded by the latitudes 22.5◦N and 25.0◦N, and the 
longitudes 125.0◦E and 127.5◦E. This region of high TC frequency is 
farther than 500 km from any of the stations, which explains the number 
of indirect TC days being almost four times more than the number of 
direct TC days. TCs in this region generally induce rainfall over the 
Philippines by enhancing the SWM (Cayanan et al., 2011; Bagtasa, 
2019). The effect of this enhanced rainfall can clearly be seen in the 
daily PI values, which is, on average, 57.1 % more than daily PSM values.
The standard deviations of the daily and yearly PD, PI, and PSM were 
calculated in order to determine the daily and yearly variability of the 
three rainfall types. The SD of the daily PD, PI, and PSM are 31.0, 22.3, 
and 13.6 mm, respectively. At first glance, it may seem that PD varies the 
most while PSM varies the least; however, the large differences between 
each rainfall type’s SD is due to the large differences in the means. To 
counter this, the coefficient of variation (CV) for each rainfall type was 
calculated by dividing the SD by the mean. The CV of the daily PD, PI, 
and PSM are 87.1 %, 112.3 %, and 107.8 %, respectively. From these 
values, it can be seen that PD experiences less variability with respect to 
its mean, while PI experiences the most variability. The reason for this is 
that TC days tend to have more consistent daily rainfall values since TCs 
generally produce rain during the entire span of multiple TC days. In­
direct and no TC days, on the other hand, have multiple days without 
rainfall at all, especially during indirect TC days wherein the TC present 
is close to the domain’s edge.
The yearly SD and CV for each rainfall type show the opposite trend 
compared to the daily values. The SD of the yearly PD, PI, and PSM are 
147.0, 223.0, and 216.4 mm, respectively, while the CV of the yearly PD, 
PI, and PSM are 63.9 %, 45.3 %, and 28.2 %, respectively. In the yearly 
values, PD experiences the most variability, while PSM experiences the 
least variability. This can be explained by the number of direct and in­
direct TC days per year. There are less direct TC days compared to in­
direct TC days and no TC days, which makes the yearly PD mean be very 
susceptible to fluctuations when the number of TC days change. No TC 
days, on the other hand, make up around two-thirds of all the days in 
JAS months. Variations on the number of no TC days only have a small 
effect on the yearly PSM mean due to the larger sample size.

3.3.1. Horizontal winds, vertical motion, and the WNPSH
Composite anomalies of the 850 hPa horizontal winds, 500 hPa 
vertical velocity, and the 500 hPa geopotential height were created and 
shown in Fig. 5. The 850 hPa horizontal wind anomalies were used to 
determine any anomalous characteristics in the wind flow over the re­
gion, especially regarding the SWM. The 500 hPa vertical velocity 
anomalies were used to help identify regions with anomalous convective 
activity, which may directly lead to more unstable conditions and 
enhanced rain. The 500 hPa geopotential height, specifically the 5870 
gpm contour line, was used to identify the extent of the WNPSH.
The composite anomaly for the D+S- case (Fig. 5a) shows a region of 
anomalous easterlies over Luzon Strait and Taiwan. North of these 
anomalies are large patches of positive vertical motion anomalies, which 
implies more stable weather in this region since convection is slightly 
suppressed. The same can be said over the western coast of Myanmar, at 
around 20◦N, which shows the same easterly and positive vertical mo­
tion anomalies. Over the Philippines, there seems to be no noticeable 
anomalies for both horizontal winds and vertical motion. However, a 
small region of negative vertical velocity anomalies is present over the 
southern half of the South China Sea, accompanied by minor westerly 
anomalies, which extend westward over the Malay Peninsula and the 
Indian Ocean. This may imply slight enhancement in the monsoon over 
the Malay Peninsula, but stops at around 115◦E, thus not reaching the 
Philippine archipelago. Finally, the position of the WNPSH, depicted by 
the 5870 gpm contour line, shows a very subtle westward extension.
The composite anomaly for the D-S+ case (Fig. 5b) shows significant 
anomalous westerlies over the Philippines and some regions of the 
Philippine Sea. In fact, the entire tropical region experiences some kind 
of westerly anomalies, which implies a weakening in the easterly trade 
winds. The large patches of positive vertical motion anomalies which 
extend from the eastern coast of the Philippines to the Philippine Sea 
may be due to the weaker easterly trade winds. Another possibility for 
the positive vertical motion anomalies over this region may be the fact 
that there are fewer TCs that pass over the country during the D-S+ case. 
The WNPSH, much like the previous case, shows a westward extension 
but nothing too drastic.
The composite anomaly for the I+S- case (Fig. 5c) shows large re­
gions of significant anomalous easterlies in the tropical region, 
stretching from 90◦E to 145◦E. These anomalies imply a weakening in 
the SWM winds, and a strengthening in the easterly trade winds. Positive 
vertical velocity anomalies are also present over the Philippines, as well 
as over large regions of the Philippine Sea. Similar to the D+S- case 
(Fig. 5a), the western coast of Myanmar, at around 15◦N to 20◦N, ex­
periences positive vertical motion anomalies and small easterly hori­
zontal wind anomalies. These anomalies might mean that the monsoon 
flow is weaker over this particular region in Myanmar and may be 
characteristic of S- years. Patches of positive vertical motion anomalies 
are once again present near Taiwan and the coast of eastern China, 
somewhat resembling the anomalies in the same region during the D+S- 
case. Finally, the position of the WNPSH in this case remains relatively 
the same.
Lastly, the composite anomaly for the I-S+ case (Fig. 5d) shows 
significant horizontal wind anomalies over Japan and the Korean 
Peninsula. These horizontal wind anomalies are accompanied by posi­
tive vertical motion anomalies implying stable weather over these 
countries during this case. The anomalous easterlies throughout the 
entire tropical region are not statistically significant but still imply a 
slight enhancement in the easterly trade winds. Perhaps the most 
striking feature in this case is the westward and southward extension of 
the WNPSH. The 5870 gpm contour line in this case extends towards 
mainland China and almost touches the northern tip of Luzon Island.
Composite anomalies of the 850 hPa stream function and 700 hPa

as well as any anomalies in relative humidity, which may be directly 
related to precipitation. The composite anomaly for the D+S- case 
(Fig. 6a) shows a large anomalous anticyclonic circulation over the 
eastern coast of China, centered at around 30◦N and 125◦E.

3.3.2. The moisture conveyor belt
The previous section showed that anomalous horizontal winds and 
anomalous circulations were able to enhance or suppress the monsoonal 
flow. In order to determine if these anomalies have an influence on the 
moisture transport in the SWM, moisture-related anomaly composites, 
such as moisture flux, surface latent heat flux, and VIMFC, were created. 
Bagtasa (2023) stated that not all TCs traversing the northeast of the 
Philippines lead to enhanced rainfall over Luzon Island. In order for a 
non-landfalling TC to produce enhanced rainfall, the MCB has to be 
established first. Additionally, Hegde et al. (2016) showed the effect of 
the SSTs in the Indian Ocean and the South China Sea with the MCB. 
Their study found that lower SSTs in these two bodies of water lead to a 
more structured MCB. Higher SSTs in these two bodies of water lead to a 
less structured MCB. SST anomalies will be discussed in the next section.

These moisture-related composites were able to depict the moisture

3.3.3. Tropical cyclone behavior
Firstly, the number of TCs that entered the domain for each rainfall 
variability type was calculated. On average, there were 10, 8.3, 12.6, 
and 7.3 TCs that entered the domain during the D+S-, D-S+, I+S-, and I- 
S+ cases, respectively. For comparison, the average number of JAS TCs 
that entered the domain during the years of 1961 to 2022 is 9.6. Years 
with high TC-related rainfall, which were the cases with D+ and I+, had 
TC numbers that were above the mean. Meanwhile, years with low TC- 
related rainfall, which were the cases with D- and I-, had TC numbers 
that were well below the mean.
Composite anomalies of the sea surface temperature were created for 
each variability type and are shown in Fig. 10. This was done in order to 
determine the regions in the ocean where TC formation was either 
favorable or unfavorable, as well as to possibly determine the effects of 
SSTs on the monsoon rainfall over Luzon. Wang et al. (2005) and Kubota 
et al. (2016) discovered that summer monsoon rainfall over Luzon and 
the SSTs in the ocean waters surrounding Luzon are negatively corre­
lated. This means that the atmosphere affects the SST more than the SST 
affects the atmosphere. This negative correlation may be due to the 
stronger surface wind, more cloud cover and rainfall associated with the 
monsoon being able to cool the ocean waters. SST variations have also 
been found to have an effect on the structure of the MCB (Hegde et al., 
2016).
The SST composites only show minimal anomalies in the SST of the 
waters around the Philippines, except for the I-S+ case (Fig. 10d). Ma­
jority of the SST anomalies are found to the north of 30◦N and seem to be 
related to the SWM rainfall anomalies over the Philippines. The “S-” 
cases, which are D+S- (Fig. 10a) and I+S- (Fig. 10c) feature very sig­
nificant negative SST anomalies to the north of 30◦N. Meanwhile, the 
“S+” cases, which are D-S+ (Fig. 10b) and I-S+ (Fig. 10d) feature very 
significant positive SST anomalies to the north of 30◦N. The only com­
posite which shows SST anomalies in the tropical region is the I-S+
composite. During this case, almost the entire WNP basin displays pos­
itive SST anomalies, which is something frequently observed during La 
Ni˜na conditions. It is also possible that the lack of indirect TC rainfall 
during the I-S+ case is the reason for the positive SST anomalies around 
the Philippines, similar to the findings of Wang et al. (2005) and Kubota 
et al. (2016).
Another parameter that affects the formation and behavior of TCs is 
vertical wind shear. It has been frequently observed in previous studies 
that high values of vertical wind shear is detrimental to TC formation 
and development. The composite anomalies of the vertical wind shear 
were created for each rainfall variability type and are shown in Fig. 11.

(Fig. 11a) only shows two regions of strong anomalies. The first region is 
the long region of positive anomalies north of 30◦N and stretches from 
110◦E until 160◦E. The anomalies in this region are most likely related to 
the East Asian westerly jet stream, which flows over the Korean Penin­
sula and Japan. The positive anomalies imply that the jet stream is 
stronger during this case. Between Taiwan and the Philippines is a re­
gion of negative vertical wind shear anomalies that stretch from 100◦E 
to 145◦E. The weak vertical wind shear may benefit the intensification of

In order to determine the overall behavior of the TCs during the 
different rainfall variability types, the tracks and genesis points of the 
TCs were also analyzed. Fig. 12 is a density anomaly map of all TC 
observation points for each rainfall variability type. The red grid boxes

positive TC anomalies around the northwestern tip of the Philippines 
and the southern tip of Taiwan. The positive TC anomaly in this region 
explains the high direct TC rainfall amount, since the anomalous grid 
boxes are inside the 500 km radius of the nearest station. Another TC 
hotspot can be found to the east of Hainan Island, at around 18◦N and 
112◦E. This may imply that TCs take a relatively west/northwestward 
track, which crosses the Luzon Strait in order to reach this hotspot. The 
positive anomaly grid boxes seen near Luzon also coincide with the re­

favorable conditions in this area may result in stronger TCs, which bring 
more rainfall to the country.
On the other hand, the density anomaly map for the D-S+ case 
(Fig. 12b) shows almost the complete opposite. The northern half of the 
Philippines is covered with negative TC anomalies, which means that 
there are less TCs affecting the country during this case. This explains 
the anomalously low direct TC rainfall during these years. The positive 
wind shear anomalies covering vast regions of the WNP, as seen in 
Fig. 10b, may be one of the reasons for the lower TC count. The positive 
TC anomalies in this case are found to the northeast of the Philippines 
but seem to be more than 500 km away from Luzon Island. This means 
that TCs during this case simply recurve away from Luzon Island.
The density anomaly map for the I+S- case (Fig. 12c) features the 
most grid boxes with positive TC anomalies compared to the other cases. 
A large area of positive TC anomalies are located to the east of Taiwan,

stations, and the high amount of TCs inside this region explains the high 
indirect TC rainfall value during this case. The same can be said with the 
other large area of positive TC anomalies to the east of the Philippines. 
These two TC hotspots may explain the high indirect TC rainfall during 
this case as TCs in this location are able to enhance the SWM and bring 
rainfall over the country.

3.3.4. TC tracks during specific years
To provide a deeper analysis, a specific year for each rainfall vari­
ability type was chosen and the TC tracks of the chosen years were 
studied. The most recent years in each rainfall variability type were 
chosen in order to keep the analysis as close to the present as possible. 
The years 2003, 2019, 1996, and 2017 were chosen from the D+S-, D- 
S+, I+S-, and I-S+ categories, respectively. Fig. 13 shows the tracks of 
the TCs during the JAS months of the four selected years.
The year 2003, representing the D+S- case, is shown in Fig. 13a and 
features nine JAS TCs. Out of the nine TCs, three made direct landfall 
over the Philippines, while two more traversed the Luzon Strait. This 
means that more than half of the TCs during this time came in close 
proximity to the Philippines, which led to the high direct TC rainfall 
measured over the country. Three of the four TCs that did not impact the 
Philippines directly followed very similar tracks. These TCs approached 
the Ryukyu Islands from the southwest before heading north and 
eventually northeast towards Japan. These TCs were not enough in 
number to produce high amounts of indirect TC rainfall over the entire 
three-month period. Their distance away from the Philippines also 
meant that any indirect TC rainfall observed was not as intense.
The year 2019, representing the D-S+ case, is shown in Fig. 13b and 
features nine JAS TCs as well. Out of the nine TCs, only one made direct 
landfall over the Philippines, while another one traversed the Luzon 
Strait. Some TCs that pass through the east of Luzon Island do enter the 
500 km radius of the nearest station but only for a very short time; 
therefore, the direct TC rainfall measured was very small. The lack of 
TCs directly affecting the Philippines during this year is the cause for the 
low direct TC rainfall amount. Out of the nine TCs, five passed through 
the gap between Taiwan and the island of Okinawa. These TCs even­
tually traverse the East China Sea and affect eastern China, the Korean 
Peninsula, and Japan. This is significantly different from the year 2003, 
wherein only two TCs passed through the East China Sea.
The year 1996, representing the I+S- case, is shown in Fig. 13c and 
features twelve JAS TCs. Out of the twelve TCs, two made direct landfall 
over the Philippines, while two more traversed the Luzon Strait. These 
TCs, along with the numerous TCs northeast of the Philippines, 
contributed to the high amount of indirect TC rainfall for this year. It is 
also worth noting that in this case, TCs form much deeper into the Pa­
cific Ocean. Five out of the twelve TCs originated east of 150◦E, which is 
not the case in the previous cases.
The year 2017, representing the I-S+ case, is shown in Fig. 13d and 
features nine JAS TCs. Out of the nine TCs, two made direct landfall over 
the Philippines, while two more traversed the Luzon Strait. The most 
obvious trait that can be seen in this case is the length of the TC tracks, 
which are significantly shorter than the tracks in the other cases. This is 
due to the westward shift in genesis positions during the I-S+ case. 
Despite having some TCs present northeast of the Philippines, as well as 
some TCs near Hainan Island, the shortness in the tracks of the TCs that 
pass through the northeast of the Philippines may imply that these TCs 
are not as strong as the TCs in the previous cases as they did not have 
enough time to intensify. This might explain the lower amount of indi­
rect TC rainfall for this year.

It seems that the positive anomalies in total rainfall for the TR+ type

cannot explicitly identify the cause of the positive or negative anoma­

The composite anomalies of the TR+ and TR- types were also 
examined. Fig. 14 shows the 850 hPa horizontal wind, 500 hPa vertical 
velocity, 850 hPa stream function, 700 hPa relative humidity anomalies, 
as well as the position of the WNPSH depicted by the 5870 gpm contour 
line of the 500 hPa geopotential height.
Fig. 14a and b show the horizontal wind and vertical velocity com­
posite anomalies, as well as the 5870 gpm contour line for the TR+ and 
TR- types, respectively. The TR+ type (Fig. 14a) is dominated by strong 
anomalous westerlies south of 10◦N, which enhances the monsoon flow 
and suppresses the easterly trade winds. From 20◦N to 30◦N and east of 
125◦E is a region of strong anomalous easterlies. The climatological 
horizontal wind composites (Fig. 2a) show that southeasterly winds 
dominate this region. Anomalous easterlies in this region suggest that 
the easterly components of these southeasterly winds intensified during 
these years. Positive vertical velocity anomalies can be found over some 
regions of the Indochina Peninsula, at around 10◦N to 15◦N and 100◦E to 
110◦E, and over the western coast of Luzon. These positive vertical ve­
locity anomalies are most likely due to the enhanced monsoon flow that 
passes through these regions. The positive vertical velocity anomalies 
east of 135◦N may be due to the monsoon trough, where the enhanced 
monsoon flow converges with the easterly trade winds. Near the equator 
are negative vertical velocity anomalies, which suggests suppressed 
convection over some areas of the Indonesian archipelago. Finally, the 
5870 gpm contour line suggests that the WNPSH is slightly shifted to the 
northeast during these years. This shift in the WNPSH might be the 
reason for the stronger monsoon flow.
The TR- type (Fig. 14b) is dominated by strong anomalous easterlies 
south of 15◦N, which implies enhanced easterlies and suppressed 
monsoon winds. Negative vertical velocity anomalies are present over 
the eastern coast of Myanmar, which slightly extends into the Bay of 
Bengal, some regions in the Indochina Peninsula, and some parts of the 
western coast of Luzon. These anomalies are due to the suppressed 
monsoon flow. Negative vertical velocity anomalies are also found near 
the equator, east of 140◦E. Near the equator, west of 130◦E are positive 
vertical velocity anomalies, which imply enhanced convection over the 
Indonesian archipelago. The 5870 gpm contour line also shows a 
noticeable westward shift in the location of the WNPSH. This shift in the 
WNPSH might be the reason for the stronger trade winds.
Fig. 14c and d show the stream function and relative humidity 
composite anomalies for the TR+ and TR- types, respectively. The TR+
type (Fig. 14c) shows a large anomalous cyclonic circulation centered at 
20◦N and 130◦E. The southern half of this anomalous cyclone contrib­
utes to the anomalous westerlies, while the northern half of this 
anomalous cyclone contributes to the anomalous easterlies seen in the 
TR+ horizontal wind composite anomaly (Fig. 14a). A strong anomalous 
anticyclone is also present east of Japan, which also contributes to the 
anomalous easterlies. Positive relative humidity anomalies can be seen 
stretching from 100◦E to 160◦E. This region of positive relative humidity 
traverses the majority of the Philippines, implying wetter weather over 
the area.
The TR- type (Fig. 14d) shows a broad anomalous anticyclonic cir­
culation centered at 20◦N and 120◦E, and a smaller anomalous anticy­
clonic circulation over the western coast of Myanmar. These anomalous 
circulations contribute to the anomalous easterlies seen in the TR- 
horizontal wind composite anomaly (Fig. 14b). Near the equator, from 
100◦E to 130◦E are positive relative humidity anomalies, which suggest 
wetter weather over the Indonesian archipelago. Negative relative hu­
midity anomalies are also found near the equator, east of 140◦E.
Composite anomalies of the vertically integrated water vapor flux 
were also created for both the TR+ and TR- cases, and are shown in 
Fig. 15. The moisture flux composite anomaly for the TR+ type is shown 
in Fig. 15a. A large region of positive moisture flux anomalies can be 
seen stretching from 85◦E to 140◦E. This large area of anomalies covers 
the entire Malay Peninsula, the southern tip of the Indochina Peninsula, 
and the majority of the Philippines, leading to enhanced rainfall over 
these regions. To the east of this region of positive moisture flux 
anomalies is a large region of negative moisture flux anomalies.
The moisture flux composite anomaly for the TR- type is shown in 
Fig. 15b. A large region of negative moisture flux anomalies are present 
from 75◦E to 135◦E. This region of negative moisture flux anomalies 
cover some regions of southern India, the Bay of Bengal, the Malay 
Peninsula, the southern tip of the Indochina Peninsula, and the majority 
of the Philippines, implying suppressed rainfall over these regions. To 
the east of this region of negative moisture flux anomalies is a large 
region of positive moisture flux anomalies, which implies stronger 
moisture transport by the easterly trade winds.
Although the total rainfall composites were able to show the strong 
horizontal wind and moisture flux anomalies that are present in the TR+
and TR- cases, the composites imply that the rainfall anomalies are due 
to the enhancement or suppression of the SWM flow over the 
Philippines. In reality, this is not usually the case, as seen in the year 
1986 (Table 4), which shows that the reason for the high rainfall 
anomaly is direct TC rainfall and not indirect TC or SWM rainfall. 
Breaking down rainfall into the three different types before analyzing 
allows more large-scale processes to show up in the composites, such as 
the anomalous anticyclone in the D+S- type (Fig. 6a), which may force 
TCs to travel a more west/northwestward track, thus producing more 
direct rainfall over the Philippines.

4. Summary and Conclusions
This study analyzed the interannual variability of direct TC, indirect 
TC (TC-enhanced SWM), and SWM (no TC) rainfall using rainfall data 
from 11 stations along the western coast of the Philippines. Previous 
studies that discussed TC-enhanced SWM rainfall, such as Cayanan et al. 
(2011) and Bagtasa (2019), focused on select enhanced SWM cases or 
HPEs instead of analyzing the year-to-year variability over several 
decades. This study was able to separate the SWM rainfall experienced 
by the western coast of the country into PD, PI, and PSM, and analyze the 
characteristics of years with anomalously high or anomalously low 
values in one or more of the rainfall categories. Climatologically, PD, PI, 
and PSM account for 15.4 %, 33.1 %, and 51.5 % of the total yearly SWM 
rainfall, respectively, and these yearly values tend to vary from year-to- 
year.
The categorization of the rainfall allowed for the creation of four 
rainfall variability types with a large enough sample size. In particular, 
the categories are as follows for years with: (1) an anomalously high PD 
but an anomalously low PSM (D+S-), (2) an anomalously low PD but an 
anomalously high PSM (D-S+), (3) an anomalously high PI but an 
anomalously low PSM (I+S-), and finally, (4) an anomalously low PD but 
an anomalously high PSM (I-S+). This procedure is similar to Chen et al. 
(2010a), which created variability types depending on the TC and SWM 
rainfall. In this study, due to the well-known enhancement of the SWM 
whenever a TC is in the vicinity, the indirect TC (I+ and I-) category was 
created. The processes and driving mechanisms behind the rainfall 
variability for each case is illustrated in the schematic diagram shown in 
Fig. 16.
The D+S- case features an above average number of TCs at around 10 
TCs per JAS season. These TCs track westwards and skim the northern 
portion of the Philippines due to the strong anomalous anticyclone to the 
north of Taiwan. The low vertical wind shear over the region where the 
TCs usually pass allows for better formation and intensification of the 
TCs. The close proximity to land leads to a high direct TC rainfall amount 
observed over stations.
The D-S+ case features a below average number of TCs at around 8.3 
TCs per JAS season. This may be due to the positive vertical wind shear 
anomalies encompassing large regions of the tropical WNP, which

The low number of TCs and the non-landfalling nature of these TCs 
explain the low direct TC rainfall amount observed by the stations

The I+S- case features a high number of TCs at around 12.6 TCs per 
JAS season. The TCs during this case seem to recurve away from the 
Philippines, which leads to a high number of indirect TC days and in­
direct TC rainfall. The moisture flux composites reveal that SWM is 
significantly suppressed during this case. Parts of the ISM are also 
significantly suppressed, hinting that the weakening of the monsoon 
during this case is not just localized to the WNP basin. The suppressed 
SWM is the cause for the anomalously low SWM rainfall during this case.
Finally, the I-S+ case features a below average number of TCs at 
around 7.3 TCs per JAS season. The WNPSH is shifted significantly to the 
west, which forces TCs to take on a more westward track. The trade 
winds are stronger during this case and the mean TC genesis point is 
shifted to the west by around 5◦. The low number of TCs, westward shift 
in genesis positions, and the westward track of these TCs are the reason 
for the low indirect TC rainfall measured. Furthermore, the low number 
of TCs allows the direct TC rainfall to not reach anomalously high values 
even if TCs during this case seem to directly affect the Philippines 
instead of recurving northward. The lower TC count means a higher no 
TC day count, which explains the higher SWM rainfall measured through 
the years in this case.
Based on the analysis conducted, clear differences in the atmospheric 
processes occurring over the WNP can be observed throughout the 
various rainfall variability types. Determining the processes occurring 
during a certain year might give insights on the possible type of rainfall 
and rainfall amount the Philippines might experience during that year, 
which may lead to improvements in the forecasting of possible heavy 
rainfall events caused by the TC-enhanced SWM. Additionally, some of 
these processes affect the behavior of TCs such as the anomalous cir­
culations, vertical wind shear anomalies, WNPSH position, and SST. A 
better understanding regarding these processes may help in the fore­
casting of TCs during that specific year, which would greatly help in 
disaster mitigation and evacuation efforts.
A potential area of discussion for future studies are the rainfall 
variability types that did not meet the three-year threshold (D+S+, 
D–S–, I+S+, I–S–). The rainfall variability types discussed in this 
study are composed of rainfall anomalies that are opposite to one 
another. Years with high direct or indirect rainfall also have low SWM 
rainfall, and vice versa. It would be interesting to analyze the composites 
of years wherein both rainfall types are anomalously high or anoma­
lously low since these will be the years that truly represent the extremes.
Another area for future discussion is the relationship between the 
rainfall variability types and ENSO. It is recognized that ENSO plays key 
roles in large-scale circulation patterns and the total rainfall variability 
over the western coast of the Philippines. For instance Kubota and Wang 
(2009) discussed the interannual rainfall variability in the WNP due to 
TCs and determined that TC rain is greatly affected by ENSO since ENSO 
modulates the TC activity over the WNP. A strong WNPSM typically 
occurs during El Ni˜no developing or La Ni˜na decaying years, and a weak 
WNPSM typically occurs during El Ni˜no decaying or La Ni˜na developing 
years (Chou et al., 2003). However, we only found fewer cases of the 
different variability types during El Ni˜no and La Ni˜na years (Table S1), 
which is not sufficient for composite analysis. Aside from this, there are
