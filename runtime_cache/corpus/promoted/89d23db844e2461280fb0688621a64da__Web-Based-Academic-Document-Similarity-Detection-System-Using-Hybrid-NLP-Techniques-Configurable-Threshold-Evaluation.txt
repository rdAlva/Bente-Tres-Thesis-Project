Introduction 
Academic institutions face a persistent and growing challenge in maintaining 
academic integrity, particularly as the volume of digital student submissions increases across 
courses and semesters. Detecting overlapping or copied text in academic work is technically 
demanding because documents vary significantly in length, vocabulary, and structure 
(Widianto et al., 2024a). The core challenge lies in converting unstructured textual content 
into a quantitative form that enables reliable pairwise comparison; each document must be 
transformed into a numerical representation so that similarity can be computed 
mathematically rather than subjectively (Jurafsky & Martin, 2023). The high dimensionality 
of text data, compounded by the diversity of academic writing styles, makes it difficult to 
design a detection mechanism that is both computationally efficient and accurate across a 
broad corpus of student submissions (Sajid et al., 2025). 
Existing document similarity platforms such as Turnitin and iThenticate have been 
widely deployed in higher education to assist instructors in detecting potential academic 
dishonesty. These systems compare uploaded documents against massive, internet-scale 
external databases and generate aggregate similarity scores that instructors must interpret 
(Omar et al., 2024). However, this architecture introduces a critical and often 
underappreciated failure mode: the Global Black Box Problem. Because commercial 
platforms lack institutional context, they routinely flag legitimate academic discourse, shared

as evidence of potential misconduct. Consequently, students who submit 
multiple drafts of the same work may be penalized for institutional self-plagiarism, not 
because their work is dishonest, but because the platform cannot distinguish between 
iterative scholarly refinement and unauthorized copying. Prior research has further 
established that no single automated detection method can reliably identify all forms of 
academic similarity, particularly paraphrasing and structural obfuscation, and that human 
review remains indispensable (Amirzhanov et al., 2025). More critically, commercial systems 
transmit student documents to external servers, rely on costly subscriptions, and lack the 
configurability that individual institutions may require (Ismail et al., 2024). Most deployed 
systems also prioritize automated flagging over transparent, instructor-guided evaluation, 
providing little explainability in their scoring methods and thereby limiting meaningful 
human oversight (Sajid et al., 2025). 
To address these limitations, this study proposes a Self-Augmenting Intra-Corpus 
Framework for Threshold-Driven Academic Document Similarity Detection   a locally 
deployable, privacy-preserving system that fundamentally reorients the detection architecture 
away from external database comparison and toward a context-aware, institution-specific 
evaluation model. The framework combines four core Natural Language Processing (NLP) 
algorithms: TF-IDF vector space modeling, N-gram analysis, Cosine similarity, and Jaccard 
similarity. TF-IDF transforms each document into a weighted numerical vector reflecting the 
importance of terms relative to the corpus (Jurafsky & Martin, 2023), while N-gram analysis 
captures contiguous word sequences   unigrams, bigrams, and trigrams   to detect structural

similarities even when words are substituted or reordered (Rahman et al., 2024). Cosine 
similarity produces a normalized global similarity score independent of document length 
(Sari, 2023), Jaccard similarity quantifies the proportion of shared N-gram sets between 
document pairs (Saeed & Taqa, 2023).  
The architectural innovation of this framework lies in its Live Batch Analysis and 
Threshold-Driven Promotion Logic. Unlike commercial platforms that compare submissions 
against a static, externally maintained database, this system performs real-time pairwise 
similarity computation across an entire instructor-uploaded submission batch. Upon 
completion of each analysis session, only those documents whose similarity scores remain 
below the instructor-configured threshold, that is, those identified as sufficiently original   are 
Promoted to the Local History Repository. This mechanism ensures that the repository is 
populated exclusively with high-confidence original work, creating what is functionally a 
Golden Standard institutional corpus that grows more authoritative with each evaluation 
cycle. Documents flagged as excessively similar to existing repository entries are withheld 
from promotion pending instructor review, thereby preventing the contamination of the 
historical baseline with potentially derivative submissions. This self-augmenting architecture 
eliminates the institutional self-plagiarism problem inherent in global platforms: because the 
system's historical baseline is built only from work that has already been validated as original 
within this institutional context, legitimate iterative scholarly work is not penalized. 
The framework operates exclusively within a local institutional infrastructure, 
preserving student data privacy, and flags document pairs exceeding a configurable threshold 
for instructor review rather than issuing automated verdicts. The developed framework

Review of Related Literature 
Algorithms and Similarity Metrics  
Studies consistently validate the combination of vector-based and set-based metrics as 
the most reliable approach to document similarity detection. Al-Tamimi et al. (2021) 
demonstrated that combining Jaccard and Cosine similarity metrics in a hybrid NLP system 
significantly enhanced detection accuracy, recall, and precision in scientific paper analysis.

Verma and Chauhan (2023) further confirmed that fusing TF-IDF, Cosine, and Jaccard 
similarity in a single plagiarism checker produces more reliable detection than any single 
algorithm alone, while Saeed and Taqa (2023) established that multi-stage TF-IDF and 
Jaccard-based filtering efficiently discards unlikely document matches before deeper 
analysis. Davoodifard (2022) validated Cosine similarity's effectiveness in computing vector 
angles between document representations to minimize false retrieval, and Sari (2023) 
confirmed its robustness for plagiarism detection on electronic documents in lightweight 
institutional settings. Sajid et al. (2023) established that Cosine similarity outperforms 
alternative distance metrics for varying-length documents, Cahyani et al. (2024) 
demonstrated that combining Cosine and Jaccard captures both global semantic similarity 
and local lexical overlap to reduce false positives, and Kaur and Singh (2023) confirmed that 
TF-IDF weighting with cosine distance consistently produces high-quality document 
representations. Januzaj and Luma (2022) demonstrated the interpretability of cosine-based 
scoring in institutional decision-making, while Rahman et al. (2024) established that 
N-gram-enhanced TF-IDF representations capture structural similarities that pure term-based 
models miss   together justifying the proposed framework's multi-algorithm design and 
Promotion Logic evaluation model.

Synthesis 
The foregoing literature collectively establishes a strong empirical and theoretical foundation 
for the proposed Self-Augmenting Intra-Corpus Framework. Existing systems such as 
Turnitin and iThenticate primarily rely on large-scale external databases and automated 
similarity scoring, often functioning as black-box platforms with limited transparency in how 
similarity scores are derived. These systems emphasize automated flagging, requiring 
instructors to interpret results without full visibility into underlying computations. In 
contrast, the proposed framework operates on a localized intra-corpus model, utilizing 
interpretable similarity metrics such as TF-IDF, Cosine similarity, Jaccard similarity, and 
N-gram analysis. It adopts an instructor-guided evaluation approach rather than automated 
classification. Furthermore, while existing systems depend on static or externally maintained 
repositories, the proposed framework introduces a self-augmenting Dynamic Benchmark 
Corpus, enabling continuous institutional adaptation while preserving data privacy through 
fully local deployment. Studies consistently validate TF-IDF, N-gram analysis, Cosine

overlap (Al-Tamimi et al., 2021; Verma & Chauhan, 2023; Saeed & Taqa, 2023; Sari, 2023; 
Cahyani et al., 2024; Rahman et al., 2024), and development literature confirms the 
feasibility of Python-based, locally deployable similarity systems in academic contexts

Promotion-gated, self-augmenting repository architecture while maintaining human-centered 
evaluation within a privacy-conscious, local institutional environment.

and selective historical archiving. This architecture departs from the conventional IPO model 
by incorporating a Threshold-Driven Promotion Stage as an explicit post-output process, 
ensuring that the system's Local History Repository is populated exclusively with documents 
verified as sufficiently original within the institutional context. 
The inputs consist of three primary components: student-submitted academic 
documents uploaded by the instructor; an instructor-configured similarity threshold that 
functions simultaneously as a detection cutoff and a Promotion Gate; and the selected 
N-gram size parameters   unigrams, bigrams, and/or trigrams   that govern phrase-level 
tokenization and comparison. 
The processing pipeline proceeds through six sequential stages: (1) text preprocessing 
through tokenization, stop-word removal, and normalization; (2) TF-IDF vectorization of the 
cleaned text; (3) N-gram set generation at the configured gram sizes; (4) Cosine similarity 
computation between all TF-IDF vector pairs to produce length-independent global similarity 
scores; (5) Jaccard similarity computation between corresponding N-gram sets to quantify 
local phrase-level overlap; and (6) Threshold Evaluation, in which document pairs exceeding 
the instructor-defined threshold are flagged and ranked. 
The output of the system is two-fold: numerical similarity scores for each document 
pair, and a ranked list of flagged document pairs for instructor review. Critically, the system 
does not label any pair as plagiarized; it presents similarity evidence to support, rather than 
replace, the instructor's professional judgment.

Objectives of the Study 
The primary aim of this study is to investigate the effectiveness of a Self-Augmenting 
Intra-Corpus Framework for Threshold-Driven Academic Document Similarity Detection. 
 
  
SCHOOL OF COMPUTING 
     

13 
 
HOLY ANGEL UNIVERSITY 
Specifically, it seeks to examine how combined Natural Language Processing (NLP) 
techniques and threshold-based evaluation mechanisms can improve the accuracy, reliability, 
and usability of academic similarity detection within a localized, privacy-preserving 
environment. 
Specifically, the study aims to: 
1.​ Analyze the effectiveness of combining multiple similarity algorithms including 
TF-IDF vectorization, N-gram analysis, Cosine similarity, and Jaccard similarity in 
detecting document similarity within an intra-corpus academic setting. 
2.​ Determine the optimal threshold ranges for similarity detection that balance 
sensitivity and specificity, minimizing false positives and false negatives in academic 
document comparison. 
3.​ Evaluate the detection accuracy of the framework using performance metrics such 
as precision, recall, and F1-score, with validation based on Subject Matter Expert 
(SME) judgment. 
4.​ Assess the reliability of Promotion Decisions in building a Dynamic Benchmark 
Corpus, based on expert evaluation of document originality and relevance. 
5.​ Evaluate system usability from the perspective of end-users (instructors) using 
the System Usability Scale (SUS), focusing on ease of use, efficiency, and overall 
user satisfaction 
6.​ Conduct a separate expert-based evaluation to assess the interpretability, 
transparency, and academic validity of the similarity reports generated by the system.

Scope and Delimitations 
This study covers the design, development, and evaluation of a web-based 
Self-Augmenting Academic Document Similarity Detection Framework for course-level and 
campus-level deployment. The system processes student-submitted academic documents   
essays, reports, and research papers   uploaded by instructors and compares them against both 
the current session batch and a growing Local History Repository using TF-IDF vector space 
modeling, N-gram analysis (unigrams, bigrams, trigrams), Cosine similarity, and Jaccard 
similarity as its core algorithms. The system generates numerical similarity scores and a 
ranked list of flagged document pairs that exceed the instructor-configured Promotion Gate 
threshold. Access is role-based: instructors may upload documents, configure thresholds, and 
view results, while administrators have additional privileges for user management and 
repository management. System performance will be evaluated using precision, recall, 
F1-score, and response time, with Promotion Decision accuracy assessed against SME 
manual judgment, on a Dynamic Benchmark Corpus of thesis documents derived from the 
publicly accessible Systems Plus College Foundation (SPCF) research repository. 
The proposed system does not perform internet-wide searches or external database 
comparisons and is not designed to automatically classify or legally determine plagiarism. 
The framework excludes deep learning models, transformer-based embeddings, and any form 
of semantic similarity analysis beyond the four specified algorithms. The Dynamic 
Benchmark Corpus is seeded with publicly available PDF thesis documents from SPCF, and

the study does not use actual student submissions from Holy Angel University during 
development or testing. The system is limited to textual document comparison and does not 
support image-based, audio, or multimedia similarity detection. Formal usability testing is 
limited to alpha and beta testing phases with purposively sampled participants, and 
generalizability beyond the School of Computing context is outside the scope of the current 
study. 
The use of a single institutional repository (SPCF) as the seeding corpus for the 
Dynamic Benchmark Corpus represents a deliberate scope delimitation rather than a 
methodological limitation of the detection algorithms themselves. The framework's 
algorithmic components TF-IDF vectorization, N-gram analysis, Cosine similarity, and 
Jaccard similarity are domain-agnostic and corpus-independent; their performance 
characteristics do not presuppose any particular source of documents. The SPCF corpus was 
selected specifically because it is publicly accessible, institutionally independent from the 
deployment target (HAU), and contextually representative of the academic writing style and 
document structure present in Philippine higher education computing programs. 
Generalizability of Promotion Decision accuracy findings to broader or more diverse corpora   
including multi-disciplinary, multilingual, or cross-institutional repositories  is acknowledged 
as outside the scope of the current study and is identified as a direction for future research. 
Future work may extend the Dynamic Benchmark Corpus seeding to include publicly 
available repositories from multiple institutions or disciplines to assess the framework's 
performance under greater linguistic and structural variability.

Method 
Research Design  
This study employs a descriptive quantitative research design, which is appropriate 
since the evaluation focuses on measuring the system’s performance in terms of precision, 
recall, F1-score, response time, and usability scores under varying threshold configurations. 
It also evaluates the accuracy of the system’s Promotion Decisions by comparing them with 
Subject Matter Expert judgment. The development of the Flask-based web application 
follows the V-Model, which structures the process into sequential phases of requirements 
analysis, system design, and implementation, with corresponding stages of unit testing, 
integration testing, system testing, and acceptance testing. This approach ensures that each 
component of the system, including the NLP algorithms and Promotion Logic, is 
systematically developed and thoroughly validated at every stage. 
At the same time, the study applies the Cross-Industry Standard Process for Data 
Mining or CRISP-DM model to guide the data-driven and Natural Language Processing 
components of the system. This model defines how documents are processed from raw input 
through preprocessing, feature extraction using TF-IDF and N-grams, similarity computation 
using Cosine and Jaccard measures, and threshold-based evaluation leading to Promotion or 
Withhold decisions. It also ensures that data preparation, modeling, and performance 
evaluation are conducted in a structured manner using established metrics such as precision, 
recall, F1-score, and accuracy. Together, the V-Model and CRISP-DM provide a

evaluation of the similarity detection process. 
Sources of Data  
The sources of data for this study are classified as secondary sources. The primary 
data pool is obtained from the publicly accessible research database of Systems Plus College 
Foundation (SPCF), available at https://www.spcf.edu.ph/academics/program/cob/research. 
This repository contains completed thesis documents that will serve as the basis for 
generating controlled test datasets. 
A total of 20 publicly available thesis documents will be collected from the SPCF 
repository. These documents are selected to provide variability in writing style and structure 
while remaining computationally manageable for controlled experimentation. 
Unlike conventional similarity detection systems that rely on pre-existing databases 
as the primary reference, the proposed system follows a batch-based similarity detection 
approach. The collected thesis documents will be used to create multiple variants, including 
verbatim copies, AI-paraphrased versions, and unrelated samples. These manipulated 
datasets will simulate plagiarism scenarios and establish ground-truth similarity conditions. 
All generated documents will then be uploaded in batches, where the system performs 
intra-batch comparison, evaluating each document against others within the same batch. 
Documents that exhibit significant similarity will be flagged, while those that meet the 
defined originality threshold will not be flagged and may be stored in the system’s Dynamic

Participants  
The system evaluation will involve a single group of ten participants who will engage 
in both Alpha and Beta testing. This group will consist of seven instructors and three experts 
in the field of computing. During the Alpha testing phase, participants, especially the 
computing experts, will assess the system's functionality, technical accuracy, and algorithmic 
correctness. Their focus will be on verifying the accuracy of the system's promotion 
decisions prior to its wider release. In the Beta testing phase, the same participants will 
evaluate the system's usability, interface design, and practical utility through structured 
testing tasks. The instructors will provide feedback based on their experiences in uploading 
and reviewing student academic documents, following a purposive sampling approach. 
All participants will be informed of the study's purpose and will provide informed 
consent before taking part in the evaluation.

document similarity detection system itself, used to generate all performance data. System

the following for each test run: (1) processing time per document batch in seconds; (2) total

each threshold setting; (4) computed precision, recall, and F1-score for each threshold 
configuration tested against the ground-truth corpus; and (5) presence of any system errors, 
processing failures, or unexpected outputs. All performance data will be logged 
automatically using Python's built-in time module and custom logging functions, and results 
will be exported to structured log files for tabulation and analysis.

processing pipeline. The checklist is organized into six sections corresponding to the

The threshold sensitivity analysis evaluates values ranging from 0.30 to 0.90 at 0.10 
intervals. This range was selected based on three converging rationales. First, empirical 
literature establishes that cosine similarity scores below 0.30 between academic document 
pairs are generally attributable to incidental lexical overlap rather than structural or 
intentional similarity, making sub-0.30 thresholds prone to excessive false negatives that 
would render the detection mechanism ineffective (Sajid et al., 2023; Sari, 2023). Second, 
thresholds above 0.90 approach near-identical document detection only, which fails to 
capture the paraphrased or partially rewritten submissions that constitute the most prevalent 
forms of academic similarity in practice (Rahman et al., 2024; Amirzhanov et al., 2025). 
Third, Sajid et al. (2025) and Noronha and Khan (2025) both identify the moderate range of 
0.50–0.70 as the zone within which configurable threshold systems demonstrate the most 
pronounced tradeoff between precision and recall across varied academic document types, 
making it the region of greatest methodological interest. By spanning from 0.30 to 0.90, the 
analysis captures the full behavioral spectrum of the framework   from permissive to highly

Research Procedure  
The development and evaluation of the proposed academic document similarity 
detection system follow two complementary process models: the V-Model for software 
development and the CRISP-DM (Cross-Industry Standard Process for Data Mining) model 
for the data-driven and algorithmic components. The V-Model ensures that each stage of 
system development is paired with a corresponding testing and validation phase, which is 
essential given the study’s focus on accuracy, reliability, and evaluation metrics. Meanwhile, 
CRISP-DM structures the lifecycle of the Natural Language Processing (NLP) pipeline, 
guiding how documents are processed, analyzed, and evaluated from raw input to final 
output. Together, these models ensure that both the system functionality and the similarity 
detection process are systematically developed and thoroughly validated.

1. Business Understanding​
 The study defines the problem of academic document similarity detection within a 
local institutional context, addressing the limitations of existing systems. The 
objective is not only to detect similarity but also to evaluate how a threshold-based

2. Data Understanding​
 The SPCF thesis corpus is analyzed to examine document characteristics such as 
length, vocabulary, and structure. A labeled test corpus is also created, consisting of 
verbatim copies, paraphrased documents, and unrelated pairs. These categories serve 
as the basis for evaluating both similarity detection and decision accuracy. 
3. Data Preparation​
 Documents undergo preprocessing steps such as tokenization, stop-word removal, 
normalization, and optional stemming. After cleaning, the text is transformed into 
TF-IDF vectors, and N-gram sets are generated. This allows both overall similarity 
and phrase-level patterns to be captured. 
4. Modeling​
 The system applies a hybrid similarity approach. Cosine similarity is used on TF-IDF 
vectors to compute overall similarity scores, while Jaccard similarity is applied to 
N-grams to measure overlap. These computations are performed across all document 
pairs and against the existing repository. The selection of TF-IDF with Cosine 
Similarity and N-gram analysis with Jaccard Similarity is based on their 
effectiveness, interpretability, and computational efficiency for document similarity 
tasks. Unlike deep learning approaches such as transformer-based models, which 
require large-scale datasets and significant computational resources, these methods

are more suitable for localized institutional deployment where resources and data may 
be limited. Furthermore, the chosen techniques provide transparent and explainable 
similarity scores, allowing instructors to better understand and trust the system’s 
output. This makes them appropriate for supporting threshold-based decision-making 
within the proposed framework. 
5. Evaluation​
 The system is evaluated in two ways. First, algorithm performance is measured using 
precision, recall, F1-score, and accuracy across different threshold values. Second, 
decision accuracy is evaluated by comparing system-generated Promotion or 
Withhold decisions with expert evaluation. The results are used to adjust threshold 
values, forming an iterative tuning process. 
6. Deployment​
The final system is deployed as a Flask-based web application in a local environment. 
Instructors can upload documents, set similarity thresholds, and review results. 
Documents below the threshold are automatically promoted to the Local History 
Repository, forming a Dynamic Benchmark Corpus, while documents above the 
threshold are withheld for review. This creates a feedback-driven system where the 
repository improves over time while maintaining data privacy.
