Abstract 
The goal of the study is to find out the relationship of learning choice tactics and positive 
reinforcement on M.A.C brand. The study utilized the Raosoft calculator to compute the total population 
and arrived at a sample size of 306 respondents. A set of researcher-made questionnaires was 
administered based on the factors of learning choice tactics. Price tactic and Variety Seeking tactic 
received the highest overall mean rating showing that their customers buying behavior is based on price 
and they want to try something new. While the factor Habit tactic received the lowest mean rating, the 
study also shows that there is a Weak Positive Correlation between Choice tactics and Positive 
Reinforcement. This indicates that the brand M.A.C is not as well-known like other brands. Pearson's 
Correlation result revealed that Brand Loyalty tactic gained the highest with the analysis of 'Weak 
Positive Correlation' impact to Positive Reinforcement on M.A.C brand. However, the brand itself is not 
very exposed to other people. Therefore, the researchers suggest focusing on customers type based on 
their skin type and this characteristic of a person which is more a sensitive concern. To achieve reliability 
and stability, they should focus on the real content of each product, or the ingredients of product and find 
the similarities based on the quality customer service, loyalty card and efforts that will lead to more brand 
exposure and generate more revenue as a result. 
 
Key Terms: Learning Choice, Positive Reinforcement, Price Tactic, Variety Seeking Tactic, Habit Tactic, 
Brand Loyalty Tactic.

According to Hoyer & MacInnis (2008), Learning Choice Tactics is a simple rule of thumb used 
to make low-effort decisions. The choice tactics were often used depending on the product category 
which the customers preferred. Shoppers can develop a decision strategy for each repeat purchase, 
low-elaboration choice, easy decision making in the product or service category. 
Choice tactics used in decision making can be either cognitively based (performance, habit, brand 
loyalty, price, normative) or based on feelings (affect, variety seeking, impulse). These tactics are learned 
over repeat purchase events through a process called operant conditioning. 
When customers were asked on how they made their decisions, several important classes of 
strategies emerged, including price strategies (it's the cheapest or it's on sale), affect strategies (I like it), 
performance strategies (it cleans clothes better), and normative strategies (my mom bought it), habit 
strategies (I purchase a similar brand I bought last time), brand-loyalty strategies (I purchase a similar 
brand for which I have a strong preference), and variety seeking strategies (I need to try something other 
than what's expected). 

According to Hoyer & MacInnis (2008), consumers are most likely to use price related strategies, 
such as buying the cheapest brand, buying the brand on sale, or using a coupon when they see few 
differences among brands and when they have low involvement with the brands in the consideration set. 
Despite the fact that price is a basic factor in many decisions, customers generally do not remember price 
information, even for a brand they have just selected. This response happens because price information is 
consistently available in the store, so consumers have little motivation to remember it. Note that 
consumers who worry about losing money are more concerned about price, while the people who are 
sensitive to gains see brand features as well as price as value. Simplifying choice heuristics that depend 
on price.

According to Hoyer & MacInnis (2008), affect-related tactics utilize a type of category based 
processing. In other words, we associate brands with overall emotional evaluations we recall from 
memory when making a decision, a process called affect referral or the 'What do I think about it?' 
heuristic. For example, when we hear the name Starbucks, we may link it with general feelings of 
happiness, and we may choose to get coffee there based on these feelings rather than on a detailed 
evaluation of Starbucks. In one study, consumers choosing between a healthy treat and a less healthy 
chocolate cake chose the dessert linked with the best effect (the cake) when they had little time to 
consider the decision. When they had more time to think, they chose the healthier treat, a response that 
suggests that affect referral is more of a factor under low processing effort. Another study found that 
positive feelings toward promotions can also transfer not only to the promoted product but to unrelated 
products too. 
According to Vizcaino (2018), affect tactics store managers generally use window signs to 
decorate their stores and, more importantly, to communicate store-related information to their customers. 

This research examines the role window signs play in influencing shoppers' attitudes and store patronage 
intentions. Observational evidence from two studies (data from a real-life store environment and from a 
laboratory setting) demonstrates that window signs generate positive inferences to consumers about the 
store image and the store promotion value, which ultimately influence shoppers' patronage intentions. 
Additionally, this study proposes and tests a model suggesting that window signs represent a diagnostic 
cue from which consumers derive a sense of place identity. The results show that place identity partially 
mediates the relationship between consumers' attitude towards window signs and buyers' store patronage 
intentions.

Satisfied customers are likely to develop a positive evaluation of the brand or service and repurchase it

According to Hoyer & MacInnis (2008), for normative tactics sometimes others can influence 
buyers' low-elaboration decision making. For example, a college freshman may purchase the brand of 
laundry detergent that his mom uses at home; a sophomore may purchase clothes that her friends like. Our 
use of such normative decision strategies can result from (1) direct influence; in which others try to 
control us; (2) vicarious observation, in which we watch others to guide our behavior; and (3) indirect 
influence, in which we are concerned about the evaluations of others. Normative strategies are especially 
common among inexperienced buyers who have little knowledge. Online communication can increase the 
importance of normative influence in decision making since buyers can contact one another so easily. If 
normative strategies are especially evident in a product or service category, companies can emphasize 
these motivations in advertising. Advertisers can also try to stimulate word-of-mouth communication, 
such as low-elaboration decision making that depends on others' evaluations.

According to Hoyer & MacInnis (2008), people are creatures of habit. When we find a useful way 
of doing things, we tend to repeat it without really thinking following the same routine each morning, 
driving the same route to work or school, shopping at the same stores. We do these things because they 
make life less complicated and more manageable. 
Sometimes consumers' acquisition, consumption, and disposition decisions rely on habit, too. 
Habit is one of the simplest, most effortless types of consumer decision making, described by (1) little to 
no data seeking and (2) little to no evaluation of alternatives. However, habit does not require a strong 
preference for a product; rather, it simply involves repetitive behavior and routine purchase. Decision 
making based on habit also reduces risk. Consumers know the brand will meet their needs since they have 
purchased it many times before. Research supports the effect of habit, on low-priced, frequently 
purchased products. However, the longer consumers wait to make their next purchase in a product 
category, the less likely they are to purchase the brand that they always buy. Doing the same thing time 
after time. 
According to Lindsay (2014), habit is a daily routine linked to the brands we purchase, people 
want the simplified decision making that routine provides. The lack of recognition of habit as a driving 
force in all of marketing could explain why so much marketing fails where ROI is concerned. If your 
brand's growth involves changing people from a competitor's product to yours, what you are really asking 
them to do is change their habit. 
According to Hoyer & MacInnis (2008), brand loyalty occurs when consumers make a conscious 
evaluation that a brand or service satisfies their needs to a greater degree than others do and choose to buy 
a similar brand more than once therefore. Basically, brand loyalty results from positive reinforcement of a 
performance related choice strategy. Brand loyalty can also develop when a consumer becomes proficient 
in using a particular product, such as a specific brand of financial management software. Faced with the 
learning curve required to switch to a different brand of software, the consumer tends to stay brand loyal 
due to cognitive lock-in. Buying the same brand repeatedly because of a strong preference for it. 
According to Kopp (2019), brand loyalty is the positive association consumers attach to a 
particular product or brand. Customers that demonstrate brand loyalty are committed to a product or 
service, which is shown by their repeat purchases despite competitor's efforts to attract them away. 

According to Rouse, and Shadley (2019), brand loyalty is an emotional attachment to a brand that 
drives consumers to continue and expand their purchases for products from the same brand. Organizations 
will use brand loyalty to drive additional purchases both with existing products and across brand 
extensions. Brand loyalty creates a behavior for a customer not only to perform repeat purchases for a 
product but also purchase additional products from the same company. Brand loyalty also creates a bond 
that prevents a customer from buying products from another brand. 
According to Hoyer & MacInnis (2008), decision making based on variety-seeking needs another 
common consumer decision strategy in low-effort situations is to try something different, a phenomenon 
called assortment seeking. A consumer may regularly purchase Starbucks coffee but one day have a 
desire to try Dunkin' Donuts coffee—then return to Starbucks for later coffee purchases. Consumers look 
for variety for two major reasons: satiation and boredom. If you had the same food for dinner every day or 
watched only one film over and over, satiation would occur, driving you to do something different. 
Consumer decisions that happen more than once can become repetitive. This finding explains why some 
consumers switch for change, despite the fact that they would have obtained more immediate satisfaction 
from repeating their typical decision. Another reason consumers look for variety in public situations is 
because they expect that others will evaluate their choice more positively. Consumers may engage in 
variety seeking because they see the costs of switching to another product to be lower than do consumers 
who are not variety seekers. 
According to Adomaviclus, Bockstedt, and Curley (2015), variety seeking prior research with 
consumable goods has consistently found that consumers have a preference for greater variety when 
selecting items at the same time as a bundle, rather than as a sequential series of individual decisions. 
According to Hoyer & MacInnis (2008), positive reinforcement as a rule originates from a feeling 
of satisfaction that occurs when we as consumers see that our needs have been adequately met. The 
reinforcement increases the probability that we will purchase a similar brand once more. After consumers 
apply one of the seven fundamental kinds of strategies to make a decision, they take the brand home and 
use it. During consumption, they can evaluate the brand, an action that results in one of three basic 
outcomes: Reinforcement (satisfaction leading to a positive attitude and repurchase). 
Reinforcement refers to a stimulus which follows and is dependent on a behavior and increases 
the probability of a behavior being repeated (Smith, 2017). The simplest way of understanding positive 
reinforcement is that something pleasant is 'added' when a specific action is performed (Cherry, 2018). 
Positive reinforcement is a part of the construct of Operant Conditioning developed by B. F. 
Skinner (Cherry, 2018). Skinner studied rats, and he found that if the rats consistently pressed a bar which 

delivered food to the rat, the rat would press the bar more and more in order to get the food reward. Like 
those rats, if people find a particular behavior satisfying, it is likely that they will repeat this behavior. 
 
Statement of the Problem 
The research aimed to identify the relationship of learning choice tactics and positive 
reinforcement on M.A.C brand through the following questions: 
1. How may the factors of learning choice tactics be described in terms of: 
   a. Price tactic 
   b. Affect tactic 
   c. Performance-related tactic 
   d. Normative tactic 
   e. Habit tactic 
   f. Brand loyalty tactic 
   g. Variety seeking tactic 
2. How may the respondent's positive reinforcement be described in general? 
3. Is there a significant relationship between learning choice tactics and positive reinforcement? 
 
Hypotheses of the Study 
H1: There is no relationship between price tactics and positive reinforcement. 
H2: There is no relationship between affect tactics and positive reinforcement. 
H3: There is no relationship between performance-related tactics and positive reinforcement. 
H4: There is no relationship between normative tactics and positive reinforcement. 
H5: There is no relationship between habit and positive reinforcement. 
H6: There is no relationship between brand loyalty and positive reinforcement. 
H7: There is no relationship between variety seeking and positive reinforcement.

The results obtained from this study are limited only to the whole customers of M.A.C cosmetics.

Method 
Research Design 

The research employed a descriptive method in order to understand the role of learning choice 
tactics as factors in the reinforcement of M.A.C cosmetics brand. Quantitative research aims to determine 
the connection between the independent and dependent variables. It is either descriptive or experimental 
and it establishes only associations between variables. An experimental research establishes results 
causally. 
 
Participants of the Study 
The survey questionnaire was distributed to the 306 customers in M.A.C brand in SM City Clark. 
Through this process, the researchers determined how the customer will respond regarding price tactic, 
affect tactic, performance-related tactic, normative tactics, habit tactics, brand loyalty tactic and variety 
seeking factors toward positive reinforcement of M.A.C brand company. The researchers targeted 306 
respondents, calculated respondents using Raosoft Calculator with a confidence level of 95%, a margin of 
error of 0.5%, a Population Proportion of 50% and an estimated of 1500 population. 
 
Research Instruments 
The research instrument is a researcher-made survey questionnaire to gather the needed data from 
the M.A.C cosmetics customers. The researchers gave the questionnaire to the customers of M.A.C 
cosmetics brand for data gathering. The researchers aimed to identify the factors on the perception of the 
customers' learning choice tactics through the survey, the questions about their perception of the company 
by putting a check in the survey form. 
 
Data Gathering Procedure 
The researcher's self-made questionnaire was given to possible respondents. To test the reliability 
of the survey, researchers will be conducting a pilot testing for M.A.C cosmetics at SM City Clark using 
40 questionnaires. Before the survey, the researchers first obtained a permit from the store manager. After 
given the permission, the questionnaire was distributed, and the data were collected for checking and 
tallying. 
 
Data Processing and Analysis Procedure 
A Likert Scale is used to measure the data. The Likert Scale is described thus: 1 strongly disagree, 
very un-important, highly dissatisfied, 2 disagree, un-important, dissatisfied, 3 agree, important, satisfied 
and 4 strongly agree, very important, highly satisfied.

Conclusion 
The study revealed that Price and Variety Seeking as factors of Learning Choice Tactics obtained 
the highest grand mean of 3.42 among other factors which were verbally interpreted as 'Strongly Agree'. 
In addition, respondents strongly agreed with statements that 'MAC is economical than the other 
brands' with mean rating of 3.45 and 'I buy MAC products because the textures and smell of foundation is 
different from other brands' with mean rating of 3.48 for factors – price and variety seeking tactics 
respectively. 
Meanwhile, Price factor reflecting statements 'I buy MAC cosmetics because of freebies, gift 
certificates and buy one take one promo' and Variety Seeking statement 'I buy MAC cosmetics to 
experience its different shades of lipsticks' were verbally interpreted by respondents as 'strongly agree' but 
obtained the lowest mean rating among other factors of 3.38 and 3.36 respectively. 
Furthermore, Habit tactics as factors of learning choice tactics obtained the lowest grand mean of 
3.26 among other factors which were verbally interpreted as 'strongly agree'. Respondents strongly agreed 
on statement that 'I often buy M.A.C to satisfy my needs' with mean rating of 3.29, while the statement 'I 
often buy M.A.C products weekly or monthly' obtained the mean rating of 3.24 with descriptive rating of 
agree. 
However, it is revealed that brand loyalty obtained the strongest correlated factors of Learning 
choice tactics to positive reinforcement with a Pearson's r of 0.475 and p-value of 0.001 which were 
interpreted as Weak positive correlation. While the Habit tactic gained the weakest correlated factors to 
positive reinforcement with a Pearson's r of 0.244 and p-value of 0.001 which were interpreted as Weak 
positive correlation. 

Lastly, the overall correlation of Learning choice tactics and positive reinforcement obtained a 
Weak positive correlation.

Definition of Terms 
Choice Tactics – Simple rules of thumbs used to make low-effort decisions. 
Price Tactic – Simplifying decision heuristics that are based on price. 
Affect Tactic – Tactics based on feelings. 
Performance-related Tactic – Tactics based on benefits, features, or evaluation of the brand. 
Normative Tactic – Low-elaboration decision making that is based on others' opinions. 
Habit Tactic – Doing the same thing time after time. 
Brand Loyalty Tactic – Buying the same brand repeatedly because of a strong preference for it. 
Variety Seeking Tactic – Trying something different. 
Positive Reinforcement – Generally originates from a feeling of satisfaction that happens when 
we as consumers see that our needs have been adequately met.
