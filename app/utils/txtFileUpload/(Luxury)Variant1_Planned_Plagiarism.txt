VARIANT 1: PLANNED PLAGIARISM 
(High-Effort Evasion Simulation) 
Self-Image Alignment and Aspirational Identity as Drivers of Luxury Brand 
Attitudes Among Filipino Millennial Consumers 
Prof. Marilou Q. Tolentino, DBA — Dean, College of Business, Systems Plus College Foundation, Angeles City, 
Philippines 
Abstract 
What drives Filipino millennial consumers to purchase and engage with luxury brands constitutes the 
central investigative concern of this study. A substantial corpus of scholarship from diverse national and 
cultural settings has enriched the academic understanding of consumer orientations toward luxury goods, 
thereby furnishing a productive theoretical foundation for the present inquiry. The investigation is 
principally concentrated on the role of actual and ideal self-congruity in shaping brand attitudes toward 
luxury products among Filipino millennials — specifically, the identification of factors that either 
motivate or condition this demographic's acquisition and use of luxury commodities. Beyond these 
self-congruity constructs, materialistic value orientations and status-related values were further 
incorporated into the analysis to assess their respective contributions to consumer motivation. A 
quantitative design, operationalized through a structured survey instrument, was administered to 
university students, recent graduates, and young professionals categorized as millennials across multiple 
organizational contexts. Findings revealed that ideal self-congruity exerts a comparatively stronger 
motivational influence on Filipino millennial consumers' attitudes toward luxury brands than does actual 
self-congruity. The effects of materialistic and prestige value orientations were found to be meaningfully 
articulated across respondents. The study's contributions extend across both commercial and scholarly 
domains, offering a scientifically grounded understanding of Filipino millennial brand attitudes — a 
population that represents approximately one-third of the national populace and nearly half of the active 
labor force. The variables examined had received scant attention within the Philippine context prior to this 
study. Practical implications are also directed at luxury brand managers seeking evidence-based guidance 
for calibrating marketing strategies and mix decisions when addressing the millennial consumer segment. 
Keywords: Consumer Attitude, Luxury Brand, Filipino Millennials, Actual and Ideal Self-Congruity, 
Purchase and Consumption, Motivational Factors. 
 
Introduction 
Tracked by Bain, the global luxury industry encompasses both high-end commodities and premium 
experiential offerings, organized across nine distinct market segments. At the forefront are luxury 
automobiles, premium hospitality, and personal luxury goods, which collectively command over 80% of 
the total market. Registering aggregate growth of 5% in 2018, the global luxury sector reached an 
estimated valuation of €1.2 trillion, with affirmative performance recorded across the majority of 
segments. The automotive luxury segment retained its dominant position, with constant exchange rate 
growth of 5% propelling revenues to €495 billion. Consumer appetite for experiential luxury remained 
robust, as evidenced by expansion in premium hospitality (5%), gourmet dining (6%), and luxury cruising 
(7%). Of further note, digitally mediated transactions claimed an 8% market share, while discounted 
luxury sales constituted 37% of total market volume (D'Arpizio et al., 2016). A marked upward shift in 
luxury acquisition among generation-Y consumers — individuals born between the 1980s and 1990s — 

has been observed, with this demographic demonstrating an earlier embrace of luxury products relative to 
traditionally established luxury buyer profiles (The Economist, 2014). 
Relative to conventional luxury consumers, millennials are distinguished by markedly divergent 
purchasing conduct: they exhibit greater spontaneity, a propensity for experimentation, and openness to 
emerging brand identities. This positions them in stark contrast to legacy luxury consumers, whose 
behavior is characterized by consistency of expenditure and stronger brand allegiance (Jay, 2012). 
Projections suggest that by 2026, millennials will constitute the primary consumer base of the luxury 
sector, necessitating that brand managers recalibrate their marketing and brand stewardship strategies to 
effectively capture this expansive and rapidly expanding segment (The Economist, 2014). 
Central to consumer decision architecture, the brand nonetheless remains a subject around which luxury 
consumption determinants and luxury brand governance are insufficiently theorized. The relationship 
between consumers' conceptualizations of luxury and the mechanisms precipitating luxury brand 
purchases remains both inadequately understood and broadly under-investigated (Hennigs et al., 2013). 
This theoretical gap is especially acute in the Philippine context. Luxury brands are positioned to satisfy 
both psychological and functional consumer needs through a composite of four value dimensions — 
financial, operational, individual, and social (Wiedmann et al., 2007). These dimensions permit consumer 
segmentation along a personally versus socially oriented axis: the question of whether luxury is acquired 
for intrinsic gratification or as an expression of achievement (Kapferer & Bastien, 2009). Accordingly, the 
delivery of value must align with consumers' subjective expectations and individual perceptual 
frameworks (Hennigs et al., 2013). 
Millennials have emerged as the focus of extensive scholarly inquiry, a reflection of their capacity to 
perplex prior generational paradigms. As a cohort, they are profoundly networked socially and 
demonstrate near-ubiquitous engagement with social media and information technology ecosystems. 
Within the Philippines, millennials — defined as individuals between 15 and 35 years of age — represent 
one-third of the national population. Internally diverse in values and self-presentation, they are understood 
to inhabit multiple identity layers, exhibiting different versions of themselves contingent upon what they 
wish to project to the world. In this context, brands are compelled to engage this demographic more 
substantively and convert such engagement into commercial alignment and affiliation. Central to 
millennial identity is an adherence to integrity grounded in shared experience rather than hierarchical 
authority. 
 
Literature Review 
No universally accepted definition of luxury exists, given that its meaning is inherently subjective — 
what constitutes luxury for one individual may not for another (Kapferer & Bastien, 2014). Nonetheless, a 
set of defining attributes has been proposed in the scholarly literature: exceptional quality, elevated price 
points, scarcity and distinctiveness, aesthetic and multi-sensory appeal, ancestral heritage and personal 
history, and an element of superfluousness (Dubois et al., 2001). 
Although luxury, fashion, and premium are conceptually linked and mutually borrowing in terms of 
associations, they are neither equivalent nor interchangeable (Kapferer & Bastien, 2014: 35). The mere 
presence of one attribute — elevated price, for instance — is insufficient to confer luxury status on a 
product. This ambiguity accounts for the frequent conflation of upper-range premium goods with genuine 
luxury items. Whereas premium product pricing is anchored in functionality, performance, and quality, 
luxury pricing is bound to scarcity, brand mythology, and narrative (Turunen, 2015). Paradoxically, many 
luxury brands have adopted fashion-oriented behaviors, while fashion simultaneously aspires to the 
aspirational cachet of luxury (Kapferer & Bastien, 2014). 

Luxury products are further distinguished from essential goods and branded products by their aspirational 
character: necessities satisfy needs, branded products fulfill desires of finite duration, and luxury goods 
respond to dreams — transcendent aspirations that elevate them above both requirements and wants 
(Kapferer & Bastien, 2014). The act of purchasing luxury is thus tantamount to buying into a dream, and 
such acquisitions tend to be impulsive, emotionally charged, or extravagant in character (Dubois & 
Paternault, 1995). As a commercial category, luxury commands substantial profitability through elevated 
price margins, while leading brand equity and global brand renown generate supplemental value for its 
premium, original-quality products (Kapferer, 1997). 
By degree of accessibility, luxury brands may be categorized as accessible, intermediate, or inaccessible 
(De Barnier et al., 2011). Further typologies, grounded in prior scholarship (Vigneron & Johnson, 1999; 
Kapferer, 2008; Corbellini & Saviolo, 2009), have been systematized in existing literature (Turunen, 
2015: 122). Definitional ambiguity has been compounded by brand extensions into new product 
categories at lower price tiers — as illustrated by the introduction of accessible entry-level items such as 
fragrances, which offer consumers a taste of established luxury brands (Patrick & Prokopec, 2015). 
Comprehending what underlies consumer motivation for luxury acquisition requires investigating the 
in-use value that luxury products deliver to their owners (Ballantyne & Varey, 2006). Consumption 
practices vary across individuals, as do the interpersonal and personal enactments of luxury use (Holt, 
1995). Through their consumption choices, individuals seek to construct meaningful lives with respect to 
personal identity and social relations, such that owned and consumed products and experiences contribute 
to their subjective quality of life (Pollay, 1983; Solomon et al., 2016). Consumer perceptions of luxury 
brands therefore mediate the relevance and meaning of consumption itself, necessitating investigation into 
the values that undergird consumers' individualized luxury experiences (Seo & Buchanan-Oliver, 2014). 
A binary segmentation of luxury consumers — heavy users and occasional users — was advanced by 
Kapferer and Bastien (2014). Prior to approximately the turn of the millennium, luxury market expansion 
was fueled predominantly by a large base of middle-class consumers making occasional purchases. The 
current landscape, however, is shaped more significantly by high-frequency purchasers. Western 
middle-class consumers, increasingly anxious about economic prospects, have gravitated toward less 
affluent consumption categories, while their wealthier counterparts have sustained optimism and 
continued to drive the bulk of luxury market activity. This bifurcation has enabled luxury brands to grow 
entry-level masstige product sales to aspirational middle-class consumers exercising greater purchase 
deliberation. Conversely, young Chinese middle-class consumers present a contrasting profile: despite 
comparatively lower absolute incomes, their rapidly rising earnings generate a sense of economic 
optimism and affluence, rendering the Chinese luxury market particularly dynamic. An analogous 
dynamic may partially account for the growing volume of generation-Y luxury purchases — especially 
following the 2008 economic recession — as millennials increasingly direct their expenditure toward 
self-indulgent luxury experiences (Jay, 2012). 
Research conducted by Kapferer (1998) with an internationally affluent youth sample yielded four distinct 
luxury consumer typologies. The first segment prizes beauty, excellence, and product uniqueness. The 
second is drawn to creativity and sensuality. The third values timelessness and international reputational 
standing, treating established luxury as a reliable and safe selection. The fourth and most 
exclusivity-oriented group focuses on rarity and the most inaccessible offerings — the purchasers of the 
most costly items. Findings also revealed that this fourth group skewed predominantly male, while the 
second consisted largely of female consumers, suggesting gender-differentiated consumption value 
profiles: males tend to consume overt signals of success, while females prioritize creativity, sensuality, 
and aesthetic beauty. Young males, in particular, lean toward conspicuous success signaling, while the 
third group is motivated by active visibility and prestigious brand recognition. The second group, by 
contrast, is more invested in projecting individuality, and the first regards itself as connoisseurs with a 
refined capacity to appreciate exceptional craftsmanship. 

An alternative consumer segmentation framework proposed by Han et al. (2010) examines the role of 
brand prominence in status signaling — specifically, the conspicuousness of brand logos as markers of 
social position. The greater a consumer's status need, the more they gravitate toward overtly branded 
goods. Conversely, consumers with minimal status motivation engage in quieter signaling practices, using 
subtly branded products recognizable only within their own peer segment. Affluent consumers with 
diminished status needs signal horizontally to one another through discreet luxury; affluent consumers 
with pronounced status needs consume loudly to differentiate themselves from less affluent groups. The 
less affluent with strong status aspirations, unable to access genuine luxury, frequently resort to 
counterfeit goods to mimic the appearance of higher social standing (Han et al., 2010). Consumers are 
thus influenced simultaneously by the groups they aspire to emulate and those from whom they seek to 
distinguish themselves. 
As theorized by Veblen (1965), it is not the mere accumulation of wealth, but its visible evidence, that 
confers social status — a dynamic that explains the conspicuous consumption of luxury as a symbol of 
personal achievement. Objects consumed by individuals frequently serve as identity signifiers, with 
personal possessions functioning as extensions of the self. Luxury items may be deployed to define, 
project, or differentiate one's identity — whether to assert individuality or to signal group membership.

Perceived luxury status varies not only across brands but also across product categories and situational 
contexts for the same brand. Cartier, for example, may command high perceived luxury in the jewelry 
domain but comparatively lower perceived luxury in apparel or fragrance. Similarly, Rolls-Royce 
occupies unambiguous luxury status in automobiles yet is not perceived as such in the domain of aircraft 
engines (Vigneron & Johnson, 2004; Kapferer & Bastien, 2014). 
To operationalize the quantification of luxury brand standing, Vigneron and Johnson (2004) developed the 
Brand Luxury Index (BLI), comprising five perceived dimensions: perceived conspicuousness, perceived 
uniqueness, and perceived quality (non-personal perceptions), along with perceived hedonism and 
perceived extended self (personal perceptions). These five dimensions are held to be the foundational 
requirements for establishing and sustaining a durable luxury brand identity, with effective management 
of both personal and interpersonal perceptions being essential to the long-term financial viability of a 
luxury brand (Kapferer & Bastien, 2014). Interdependent and mutually reinforcing, these dimensions are 
embedded in consumers' decision-making processes when evaluating luxury offerings. Consumers assign 
differential importance to individual dimensions based on personal value orientations. The BLI also 
facilitates inter-brand comparison and competitive advantage identification within target markets 
(Vigneron & Johnson, 2004). 
Three consumer-oriented value dimensions of luxury brands were articulated by Berthon et al. (2009): 
functional, experiential, and symbolic. Vickers and Renald (2003) further argue that these dimensions 
carry greater weight for luxury brands than for non-luxury brands, given heightened consumer 
expectations. Being contextually contingent, these dimensions evolve over time in response to individual 
and socio-cultural shifts, reflecting a co-creative dynamic between luxury brands and their consumers 
(Tynan et al., 2010). The functional dimension encompasses tangible, material attributes — quality, 
durability, reliability, functionality, and craftsmanship — with quality serving as the primary performance 
signal. The experiential dimension captures subjective, individual value — hedonic appreciation grounded 
in personal taste and brand-related stimuli such as design, packaging, communication, and environmental 
cues that engage consumer sensations, feelings, cognitions, and behavioral responses (Berthon et al., 
2009). This dimension is further enriched by uniqueness-seeking motivations, as luxury perceived as rare 
and precious guides individual emotional and cognitive engagement with the brand (Hung et al., 2011). 
The symbolic dimension concerns the brand's meaning within the social collective — its conspicuousness, 

expensiveness, and wealth connotations (Hung et al., 2011). It encompasses both other-directed signaling 
(communicating wealth, prestige, and style to external observers) and self-directed signaling (the personal 
meaning the brand's social statement holds for the consumer themselves) (Berthon et al., 2009; Belk, 
1988). Critically, symbolic value functions only within the communities in which the brand carries shared 
recognition. 
A conceptual framework developed by Wiedmann et al. (2007) maps four luxury value dimensions — 
financial, functional, individual, and social — that conjointly determine consumers' overall luxury value 
perception and acquisition behavior. Sub-dimensions include price value (financial), usability, quality, and 
uniqueness (functional), self-identity, hedonic, and materialistic values (individual), and conspicuousness 
and prestige values (social). Even where two consumers assign equivalent overall luxury scores to a 
brand, the underlying dimensional configurations may diverge markedly. This framework facilitates 
understanding of the conditions and drivers of luxury perception and thus also of consumer behavior, and 
provides practical guidance for creating, marketing, and monitoring luxury brands. 
While the Wiedmann et al. (2007) model represents the most comprehensive framework in the extant 
literature, subsequent scholars have identified opportunities for supplementation. Ciornea et al. (2011), for 
instance, proposed the addition of investment value to the financial dimension and heritage value to the 
functional dimension, along with superfluousness as a sub-dimension closely associated with materialism. 
The relative salience of individual versus social value dimensions — and their interaction with overall 
perceived luxury value and purchase intention — warrants continued analytical attention. 
Filipino millennials exhibit a strong orientation toward technology-enabled consumption, utilizing digital 
platforms not only for social engagement but for extracting economic value through discounts on airfare, 
food packages, and travel deals. Research conducted by Visa found that approximately 80% of Filipino 
millennials prefer cash-based transactions over credit card use, attributing this preference to concerns over 
financial security and confidentiality. In parallel, the purchasing capacity of Filipino millennials — as 
documented by Kantar Worldpanel and noted by Miraflor — is demonstrably expanding, with rising 
expenditure on personal care, household goods, and food items across retail formats. 
 
Background of the Study 
The paucity of empirical investigation into Filipino millennial attitudes toward the acquisition and use of 
luxury brands has provided the primary impetus for the present study. The generation of scientifically 
rigorous findings capable of advancing understanding in both academic and commercial arenas serves as 
the overarching rationale. The Philippine context, wherein the study variables had received minimal prior 
examination at the time of the research, represented a meaningful gap warranting systematic scholarly 
engagement. 
 
Significance of the Study 
The findings of this research are intended to furnish a scientific grounding for understanding the luxury 
brand attitudes of Filipino millennials — a population segment constituting approximately one-third of 
the national citizenry and nearly half of the labor force. 
 
Objectives of the Study 

The principal objective of this investigation is to examine the influence of actual and ideal self-congruity 
on the luxury brand attitudes of millennial consumers, along with the extent to which materialistic and 
status value dimensions condition such attitudes. The study further aspires to expand the body of 
scholarly knowledge pertaining to young consumer brand attitudes and subsequent purchasing conduct in 
the luxury sector, thereby maximizing the commercial yield from this target demographic and aligning 
product strategies with the distinctive needs of generation-Y consumers within the Filipino millennial 
population. The study is specifically oriented toward: 
•​
Determining the motivational forces driving Filipino millennial purchase and consumption of 
luxury brands in terms of actual versus ideal self-congruity. 
•​
Identifying which demographic variables — education level, income group, gender, and age — 
significantly predict luxury brand acquisition and consumption among Filipino millennials. 
•​
Assessing the effects of materialistic value, status value, and social desirability on luxury brand 
purchase and use among Filipino millennials. 
 
Statement of the Problem 
Central to this investigation is the examination of how actual and ideal self-congruity, and specifically the 
correspondence between a consumer's self-image and their perception of a luxury brand's image, bear 
upon brand attitude. The ideal self-image, as conceptualized by Ross (1971) and Schenk & Holman 
(1980), is the image of the person one aspires to become or the self one desires others to perceive — 
encompassing attitudes, perceptions, and affect. Engagement with luxury brands is theorized to advance 
the realization of ideal self-congruity by transmitting the symbolic meaning of luxury onto the individual's 
aspirational self-image. Directed toward the enhancement of one's own self-concept, consumption of 
luxury commodities and brands functions as a symbolic medium for communicating identity to both the 
self and to others (Grubb & Grathwohl, 1967). Brand attitude, as an essential indicator of brand equity, 
reflects a consumer's comprehensive evaluative orientation toward a brand and is principally conditioned 
by brand perceptions (Aaker, 1997). Self-congruity has further been established as a predictor of both 
brand attitude and purchase intention (Sirgy et al., 1997; Helgeson & Supphellen, 2004), with self-identity 
value, materialistic value, and status value shown to be closely interrelated constructs (Richins & 
Dawson, 1992; Dittmar, 1994; Webster & Beatty, 1997). 
The guiding research questions are as follows: 
•​
To what extent do actual and ideal self-congruity motivate Filipino millennials' luxury brand 
acquisition and consumption? 
•​
What are the respective effects of materialistic value and status value on Filipino millennials' 
luxury brand purchase and consumption? 
•​
Which demographic variables — education, income, gender, and age — significantly differentiate 
luxury brand acquisition and consumption among Filipino millennials? 
•​
Which specific survey items exhibit significant correlations with the purchase and consumption 
of luxury brands among Filipino millennials? 
 
Methodology 

Relationships among variables in the Philippine context were investigated through a descriptive research 
design. Survey methodology was adopted as the primary data collection instrument, with a structured, 
closed-ended questionnaire used to standardize responses and facilitate systematic comparative analysis. 
Both primary and secondary data sources were utilized. The questionnaire consisted exclusively of 
closed-format items offering pre-set response options, minimizing respondent-generated content and 
ensuring a high degree of structural uniformity. To anchor respondents in a shared referential framework 
for luxury versus non-luxury, a selection of well-known Philippine brand examples was presented at the 
questionnaire's outset. A five-point Likert scale (ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree) was 
employed. A shortened adaptation of the Crowne-Marlowe social desirability scale (Crowne & Marlowe, 
1960) was also incorporated to assess respondents' social approval concerns — a recognized and 
prevalent source of survey bias (King & Bruner, 2000). Lower scores on this scale indicate reduced social 
approval sensitivity and greater response authenticity (Crowne & Marlowe, 1960). 
The target population comprised Filipino millennials from higher education institutions, alongside young 
entrepreneurs, employees, employers, and graduates who engage in luxury brand consumption. A 
convenience-based, non-probability sampling approach, supplemented by self-selection, guided 
participant recruitment. Survey distribution was facilitated through OJT students and alumni acting as 
enumerators. The resulting sample of 134 respondents satisfies the criteria of efficiency, 
representativeness, reliability, and flexibility as articulated by Kothari (2004). 
 
Data Analysis 
Survey data were processed using SPSS statistical software. Descriptive statistics served as the principal 
analytical tool for numerically characterizing and comparing variables across demographic categories. 
 
Results 
Descriptive statistical analyses were initially performed to establish the demographic characteristics of 
participants. The relationship of actual and ideal self-congruity to materialistic and status value 
dimensions — and their reflection in luxury brand attitudes — was subjected to hypothesis testing. The 
social desirability scale results were similarly examined. A total of 134 respondents participated in the 
study. 
 
The sample composition was 47.8% male (n=64) and 52.2% female (n=70). Education level distribution 
included 51.5% bachelor's degree holders, 7.5% master's degree holders, 3.7% doctoral degree holders, 
14.2% secondary education, and 23.1% in other categories. Respondent ages spanned from 18 to 37 years, 
with a median of 25 and a mean of 27.5. The gender distribution was well-balanced, and the age spread 
adequately represented millennial consumers across different life stages. Most respondents were full-time 
students, resulting in a sample income profile that was not atypically elevated; accordingly, income data 
were excluded from the core analysis. 
 
The Marlowe-Crowne social desirability scale yielded a substantial proportion of socially desirable 
responses (698, or 74.42%) relative to socially undesirable ones (240, or 25.58%), indicating that 
respondents exhibited an orientation toward social approval in their survey answers, lending the data a 
degree of predictable reliability.

Across all study variables, the 18–25 age bracket recorded the highest mean values, reflecting elevated 
motivation in both luxury and non-luxury brand purchase and consumption — based on both actual and

Male respondents consistently outperformed females across all variables. Males demonstrated stronger 
motivation in luxury brand acquisition based on both actual and ideal self-congruity, and assigned greater 
importance to materialistic (functionality), social (prestige, image, reputation), and social desirability

Respondents holding master's degrees recorded the highest means across ASCL, ASCLN, ISCL, and 
ISCLN, indicating the strongest motivation to acquire and consume luxury brands based on self-congruity 
orientations. Doctoral degree holders, by contrast, exhibited the highest means for materialistic value, 
social value, and the social desirability scale, reflecting elevated motivation grounded in functionality, 
prestige, and social acceptance. 
 
Respondents in the 15,000–30,000 and 30,001–50,000 income brackets expressed the highest levels of 
motivation for both luxury and non-luxury brand purchase and consumption on both actual and ideal 
self-congruity dimensions. For materialistic value, social value, and social desirability scale, the 
30,001–50,000 and 50,001–75,000 income brackets yielded the higher mean values. Taken together, these 
patterns suggest that middle-income Filipino millennials are the most motivated luxury consumers, 
placing particular emphasis on functionality, social image or prestige, and social desirability or 
acceptance.

ASCL was found to be significantly correlated with ASCLN, ISCL, and ISCLN, indicating that 
motivation to acquire luxury brands based on actual self-congruity is relationally intertwined with ideal 
self-congruity orientations across both luxury and non-luxury brand categories. Filipino millennial 
consumption patterns, regardless of brand tier or self-congruity type, are thus mutually constitutive. 
Furthermore, the significant correlation between Materialistic Value, Social Value, and Social Desirability 
confirms that respondents' acquisition motivation is coherently organized around the tripartite axes of 
functionality, prestige and reputational image, and social approval. 
 
Conclusion 
Filipino millennials' brand attitudes toward luxury goods are more powerfully shaped by ideal 
self-congruity than by actual self-congruity, suggesting that aspirational identity alignment — rather than 

current self-perception — serves as the dominant motivational force in luxury consumption. Marketers 
should accordingly recognize that this demographic purchases luxury products in order to: 
•​
Project themselves onto the luxury brand identity. 
•​
Align themselves with aspirational social referents who consume the brand. 
•​
Forge identity affiliation with the luxury-consuming social community. 
 
Recommendations 
Luxury brand strategies targeting Filipino millennials should be anchored in ideal self-congruity 
alignment. Specifically: 
•​
Luxury brand positioning must be relatable to, consistent with, and expressive of the consumer's 
ideal self-image. 
•​
Marketing communications — including endorser selection, brand ambassadors, advertising 
themes, and promotional slogans — should reflect the ideal self of Filipino millennials, 
emphasizing functionality, image, reputation, and social desirability, and should be executed 
through premium distribution and packaging channels. 
•​
Ideally, consumers should see themselves in the brand, wish to be associated with those who 
consume it, and aspire to the identity it conveys. 
The absence of open-ended survey items represents a recognized methodological constraint of this study, 
as qualitative responses would have enabled more nuanced exploration of respondents' underlying luxury 
consumption rationales. Future scholarship might benefit from a mixed-methods approach — combining 
qualitative inquiry to generate rich insights with a subsequent structured questionnaire focused on the 
most salient luxury value dimensions identified qualitatively. Given the scale and growth trajectory of 
luxury brand consumption globally, the phenomenon remains substantially underresearched and presents 
fertile ground for future scholarly development.
