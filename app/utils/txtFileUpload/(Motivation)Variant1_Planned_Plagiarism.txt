Abstract 
An empirical investigation into the motivational determinants driving entrepreneurial 
inclination among students enrolled at the College of Business, Systems Plus College 
Foundation, in Angeles City, constitutes the central focus of this research undertaking. 
The primary objective was to isolate and scrutinize those statistically significant factors 
exerting measurable influence over students' disposition toward entrepreneurship. 
Central to this analytical inquiry were seven constructs: role models, the perceived 
image of entrepreneurship, curriculum design and course content, institutional 
promotion of entrepreneurial education, structured entrepreneurial programs, individual 
learning orientations, and respondents' overarching attitudinal stance toward 
entrepreneurial pursuits.

Introduction 
For many individuals who have completed formal education or grown disillusioned with 
conventional employment structures, the prospect of establishing a small commercial 

enterprise has long represented a viable post-graduation alternative. Across all sectors 
of the Philippine economy, public and institutional discourse on entrepreneurship has 
intensified considerably; governmental bodies have taken the lead in stimulating 
entrepreneurial 
interest 
through dedicated programs, while policy architects, 
private-sector organizations, chambers of commerce, women's advocacy groups, 
academic institutions, and undergraduate students have similarly demonstrated growing 
engagement with the field. Within the Philippine context, self-employment increasingly 
functions as a meaningful alternative to salaried employment — particularly given the 
persistent structural unemployment problem — and is regarded as a rewarding and 
attractive professional trajectory, especially for university-level students and recent 
graduates. 
The intellectual underpinnings of entrepreneurship, as conceptualized by Goel, Vohra, 
Zhang, and Arora (2007), encompass the construction and stewardship of 
profit-oriented, growth-driven ventures. This conceptualization finds further elaboration 
in the position advanced by McMullan, Long, and Graham (as cited in Lena & Wong, 
2004), who framed entrepreneurship as a catalyst catalyzing the emergence and 
genesis of novel commercial initiatives. Complementing this, Badulescu and Dodescu 
(2010) identified the aspiration for self-actualization, combined with the desire for 
elevated income and enhanced social and professional standing, as primary triggers 
propelling individuals — particularly adult women — toward commercial enterprise 
formation. The characterization of entrepreneurs offered by Goodbody Economic 
Consultants (2002) emphasizes achievement orientation, resilience in the face of 
adversity, 
industriousness, 
adaptability, 
and 
an 
eagerness 
for 
continuous 
self-improvement. Allen (2006), similarly, situated entrepreneurs as autonomous agents: 
decisive problem-solvers who operate independently of external reliance. 
The macroeconomic significance of entrepreneurial activity is considerable. As a 
generator of employment and driver of economic activity, entrepreneurship presents 
itself as a legitimate and strategically important career pathway for graduates and young 
people alike. Data indicate that approximately 25% of the national population falls within 
the youth demographic cohort, of which 39% are gainfully employed, yet 40% remain 
trapped below the economic poverty threshold. This socioeconomic reality renders 
youth-directed entrepreneurship promotion not merely desirable but strategically 
imperative — a perspective increasingly embraced at the global level. The dividends of 
youth entrepreneurship extend simultaneously to the individual and to the broader 
economic system. 
It was within this broader context that the present research sought to enumerate and 
analyze the factors underpinning students' entrepreneurial inclinations. The anticipated 
outcome was to furnish researchers and educators with substantive grounds upon 
which to actively cultivate and reinforce entrepreneurial dispositions and life orientations 
among academically enrolled individuals considering commercial ventures. 
Objectives of the Study 

The overarching aim of this investigation was to evaluate the entrepreneurial 
motivations of students within the College of Business at Systems Plus College 
Foundation, Angeles City. In pursuit of this aim, the study sought to: construct a 
descriptive profile of the respondent cohort; systematically examine the entrepreneurial 
motivations operating among respondents; identify which determinants were most 
influential in motivating students toward entrepreneurial engagement and willingness to 
undertake entrepreneurial endeavors; and appraise the institutional contribution of the 
College in advancing an entrepreneurial culture. 
The findings derived from this investigation were intended to yield actionable insights 
capable of reinforcing the entrepreneurial orientation of students. Ultimately, 
entrepreneurship was positioned as a substantive and viable professional alternative for 
graduating students of the College of Business at Systems Plus College Foundation. 
Literature Review 
Among the strategic approaches adopted by academic institutions to cultivate 
entrepreneurial interest, entrepreneurship education occupies a preeminent position 
(Linan, 2004). Embedded within this curricular strategy is the fundamental assumption 
that entrepreneurial competencies can be both systematically imparted and 
meaningfully absorbed by learners. 
Resurrection (2011) observed that governmental bodies across multiple jurisdictions 
have not been passive in their pursuit of entrepreneurial promotion initiatives, 
particularly targeting younger populations as a mechanism for poverty alleviation. 
Notwithstanding these institutional efforts, prevailing student mindsets continue to 
exhibit a pronounced orientation toward corporate employment rather than independent 
venture creation. 
Pihie (2009) documented that student respondents recorded moderate scores across all 
constructs associated with entrepreneurial intention and self-efficacy — spanning 
managerial, financial, and marketing dimensions. Notably, positive entrepreneurial 
aspirations yielded stronger associations with both entrepreneurial intention and 
self-efficacy relative to negatively framed aspirations. A particularly salient finding 
established that university-acquired entrepreneurship learning exerts a measurable 
influence on students' attitudinal posture toward entrepreneurial careers and their 
perception of behavioral control over such choices. Saravanakumar et al. (2012) further 
noted that students scoring low across all measured indices correspondingly exhibit 
diminished entrepreneurial inclination — an observation that reinforces the imperative 
for educational institutions to sustain and amplify entrepreneurship promotion. This 
finding simultaneously underscores the challenge confronting management education in 
the endeavor to produce entrepreneurially oriented graduates. 
Wang et al. (2011) extended this discourse by identifying a discernible entrepreneurial 
intention among international student populations, with a substantial proportion of such 
students demonstrating notably strong entrepreneurial motivation. This insight carries 
implications for educators seeking to nurture entrepreneurial spirit within this specialized 
demographic. A further contribution of the study was its determination that perceived 

feasibility functions as a more robust predictor of entrepreneurial intention than personal 
desirability — a finding that directs university educators toward feasibility enhancement 
as the more effective lever for cultivating entrepreneurial intent. Grassl et al. (2005) 
corroborated this by establishing that personality variables and perceived feasibility are 
dominant determinants of entrepreneurial inclination among business students. Kuehn 
(2008) emphasized that influencing students toward entrepreneurial engagement 
necessitates experiential and practical interventions on the part of educational 
institutions. This position was reinforced by Akram et al. (2010), who advocated for 
enhanced awareness campaigns that illuminate the benefits of entrepreneurship, 
thereby catalyzing student participation in ways that advance both national economic 
growth and global competitiveness. 
A sustained body of empirical scholarship has documented the contribution of 
entrepreneurship education to students' entrepreneurial inclination. Mueller and Volery 
(2006) established the pivotal role of such education in shaping students' career 
decisions regarding entrepreneurship. Wilson (2007) demonstrated that prior exposure 
to entrepreneurship education functions as a developmental precursor to elevated 
self-efficacy. Noel (1998) observed that entrepreneurship education correlates with an 
increased propensity to initiate independent business ventures, a propensity further 
amplified — according to Wilson (2007) — by the innovation orientation embedded 
within entrepreneurship curricula. Nurmi and Paasiao (2007) charged universities and 
higher education institutions with a cardinal responsibility: equipping students with the 
entrepreneurial knowledge and competencies necessary to ignite career-defining 
endeavors. 
The pedagogical dimension of entrepreneurship education merits particular attention. 
Garavan and O'Cinneide (1994) identified the alignment of curricular content and 
instructional methodologies with the goal of developing entrepreneurial competencies 
as a persistent and substantial challenge for academic institutions. Brown (1999) 
argued in favor of experiential, hands-on pedagogical models as the appropriate 
emphasis for entrepreneurship course content. In contrast, Vesper (2004) advanced a 
knowledge-based 
framework specifying four curricular domains essential for 
entrepreneurship education: business-general knowledge, venture-general knowledge, 
opportunity-specific knowledge, and venture-specific knowledge. The breadth of 
instructional approaches documented across the literature is considerable — 
encompassing textbook-based instruction (Fiet, 2002), formal examinations (McMullan 
and Cahoon, 1979), business plan development (Audet, 2000), biographical accounts of 
practicing entrepreneurs (McKenzie, 2004), guest lecturer series (Brown, 1999; Klandt 
and Volkmann, 2006), and organizational site visits (Cooper, Bottomley et al., 2004). 
Mahlberg (1996) underscored the formative influence of academic institutions in 
sculpting the entrepreneurial culture and professional aspirations of enrolled students, 
particularly in equipping them to navigate volatile commercial environments (Autio, 
Keeley et al., 1997; Landstrom, 2005). The characterization of universities as incubators 
of entrepreneurship (Bygrave, 2004) carries with it a strategic imperative: institutions of 
higher learning must actively position themselves as entrepreneurial hubs by cultivating 
ecosystems that integrate the foundational components of entrepreneurial development 
(Gnyawali and Fogel, 1994). 

The entrepreneurial career pathway offers students a route to financial autonomy while 
simultaneously generating broader socioeconomic benefits through employment 
creation, innovation, and economic advancement. Entrepreneurs are widely recognized 
as enjoying elevated degrees of financial independence, creative latitude, professional 
pride, and personal fulfillment. 
Research Framework 
The analytical structure of this study was organized around the identification and 
examination of seven motivational determinants of entrepreneurial inclination among 
College of Business students at Systems Plus College Foundation. These determinants 
comprised: attitudinal orientation, the perceived image of entrepreneurship, the 
influence of role models, institutional promotion of entrepreneurship, curriculum design 
and course content, structured entrepreneurial programs, and individual personal 
learning approaches. 
Research Design 
A descriptive research design, implemented through structured survey questionnaire 
administration, provided the methodological foundation for characterizing the profiles 
and entrepreneurial inclinations of students at the College of Business, Systems Plus 
College Foundation. The study was designed to describe the relational dynamics 
between two core variable categories: respondents' demographic profiles and their 
entrepreneurial intentions. The selection of this research design was premised on its 
capacity to generate substantive knowledge regarding the phenomena under 
investigation and the interrelationships among the study variables. 
Simple random sampling was adopted as the probability-based sampling strategy for 
identifying the target respondent pool, drawn from the student body of the College of 
Business, Systems Plus College Foundation. A structured questionnaire was 
administered to the identified participants, and data were analyzed using SPSS to 
derive both descriptive statistics and correlation coefficients. Non-numeric data were 
subjected to percentage-based tabulation and thereafter summarized, explained, and 
interpreted within the findings. 
Participants 
The study population consisted of students enrolled at the College of Business, 
Systems Plus College Foundation, for the 2015–2016 academic year. A sample frame 
calculation employing a 99% confidence level and a confidence interval of 8 yielded a 
requisite sample size of 380. All 380 distributed questionnaires were recovered; 
however, following validation, only 376 were retained for analysis, as a number of 
respondents had omitted responses to critical items or submitted incomplete 
instruments. 

Instrument 
The primary data collection instrument was a modified version of the Entrepreneurial 
Intention Questionnaire (EIQ), originally developed by Linan and Chen (2009). The 
questionnaire was structured to capture respondents' demographic profiles alongside 
the factors determining their entrepreneurial motivations. Prior to full-scale deployment, 
the instrument underwent pre-testing, and its internal reliability was assessed and 
confirmed using Cronbach's Alpha. Data collection was conducted during scheduled 
lecture hours, with questionnaires administered directly to enrolled students. 
Data Analysis 
The collected data were aggregated, tabulated, and analyzed using a suite of statistical 
techniques, including frequency counts and percentage distributions, weighted mean 
calculations, and the Pearson correlation test. The selection of each analytical tool was 
guided by the specific objectives of the study. All closed-ended questionnaire items 
were scored using a 5-point Likert scale. A preliminary pilot test was conducted prior to 
full data collection to refine the instrument's reliability and validity. Participation 
remained entirely voluntary throughout, with questionnaire administration supervised by 
course lecturers and instruments retrieved immediately upon completion. Statistical 
analysis was performed using SPSS version 18.0.
