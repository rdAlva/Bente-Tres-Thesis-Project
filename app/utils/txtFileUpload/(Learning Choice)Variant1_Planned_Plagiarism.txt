CONSUMER DECISION-MAKING APPROACHES AND BEHAVIORAL 
REWARD MECHANISMS: 
AN EMPIRICAL INVESTIGATION OF M.A.C COSMETICS AT SM CITY 
CLARK BRANCH 
Alcantara, M.M.R. / David, J.T. / Estabillo, R.R.K. / Kim, M.R. / Santos, K.P. 
 
Abstract 
Establishing the association between consumer decision-making approaches and behavioral 
reward mechanisms as observed among M.A.C cosmetics patrons constituted the central objective of this 
inquiry. A sample of 306 participants was systematically derived from an estimated population of 1,500 
through the Raosoft online calculator at a 95% confidence interval and 5% margin of error. 
Researcher-constructed survey instruments were employed to measure seven documented consumer 
decision-making approaches. Among these approaches, cost-based and assortment-seeking orientations 
registered the most elevated composite scores, thereby suggesting that purchasing patterns among M.A.C 
consumers are predominantly shaped by price sensitivity and the pursuit of novel product experiences. 
In contrast, the habitual orientation emerged as the dimension attaining the lowest aggregate 
score. When examined through Pearson's correlational analysis, each of the seven consumer 
decision-making dimensions demonstrated a statistically significant yet modestly positive relationship 
with behavioral reward outcomes. Brand devotion orientation generated the strongest correlational 
coefficient at r = 0.475, whereas habitual orientation produced the weakest at r = 0.244. These findings 
collectively suggest that despite M.A.C's stature as a globally recognized cosmetics authority, its market 
penetration relative to consumer reinforcement remains comparatively limited. The researchers 
consequently recommend that strategic prioritization be directed toward skin-type-specific customization, 
ingredient transparency, consistent service quality, and the implementation of structured loyalty 
mechanisms as pathways toward enhancing brand visibility and revenue generation. 
 
Key Concepts: Consumer Decision Approaches, Behavioral Reward Mechanisms, Cost-Based 
Orientation, Assortment-Seeking Behavior, Habitual Buying, Brand Devotion, Experiential Variety. 
 
Introduction and Review of Related Literature 
The historical origins of cosmetic application are conventionally attributed to ancient Egyptian 
civilization, wherein excavated burial chambers have consistently yielded containers and implements 
associated with personal adornment. Notable among ancient cosmetic practices, Cleopatra was recorded 

as having derived lipstick pigmentation from pulverized carmine beetles, while contemporaneous women 
employed clay-and-water mixtures as labial colorants. Broadly defined, cosmetics constitute substances 
applied externally to the face, body, or hair for purposes of aesthetic enhancement or visual 
transformation. The category encompasses foundational products, ocular preparations, blush formulations, 
and lip colorants, among others. Women historically employed such items to augment their attractiveness, 
while theatrical performers utilized them to achieve character-specific transformations (Edmonds, 2018). 
Make-Up Art Cosmetics, more widely recognized by its abbreviated designation M.A.C, has 
secured its position as the foremost global authority in professional cosmetic application, an achievement 
rooted in its unmatched proficiency in the art of makeup. 
The brand's commercial origins trace to the early 1980s, when M.A.C Cosmetics was established 
and rapidly gained traction in the marketplace. Under the creative direction of Toskan and with Angelo 
serving as the executive responsible for marketing, operations were initially conducted from a kitchen 
space situated at the rear of one of Angelo's boutique establishments. The eventual inclusion of Toskan's 
brother-in-law, a chemist by the name of Vic Casale, proved instrumental in producing the brand's 
inaugural lipstick formulation—a product conceptualized from the vivid pink hue of a Crayola crayon 
designated Flamingo. Toskan himself identified the product as the first matte lip formulation to render 
identically on the lip surface as it appeared within its cylindrical container. Following its favorable 
reception, the product line was extended to encompass 23 additional pastel-inspired shades, along with a 
limited assortment of pencils, base products, and powders. 
Among cosmetics enterprises that have retained sustained commercial and cultural appeal, M.A.C 
stands as a particularly enduring example. The brand has consistently distinguished itself through a 
commitment to bold creative campaigns and unconventional collaborative ventures. Embracing an ethos 
of inclusivity, M.A.C's founding directors deliberately recruited individuals of unconventional aesthetic 
expression—employees of varying gender identities who prominently featured vivid hair colorations, 
body piercings, and tattoos. As the first cosmetics brand in the industry's history to formalize investment 
in staff training and consumer education at the point of sale, M.A.C distinguished itself further by 
foregoing traditional promotional mechanisms such as complimentary purchase incentives and sampling 
programs. Instead, the organization placed its confidence in its meticulously crafted formulations and its 
visually arresting brand identity. Serving a broad spectrum of gender expressions and skin tones, the 
Canadian company effectively redefined the conceptual boundaries of cosmetic beauty. 
As conceptualized by Hoyer and MacInnis (2008), Learning Choice Tactics represent condensed 
cognitive heuristics employed by consumers to navigate purchasing decisions with minimal cognitive 
expenditure. The applicability of these tactics tends to vary according to product classification and is most 

pronounced in contexts characterized by repeated acquisition, low elaboration, and relatively automatic 
decision-making. 
Within the domain of consumer decision-making, choice tactics may be categorized along two 
principal axes: those grounded in cognitive evaluation, encompassing performance assessment, habitual 
behavior, brand devotion, price sensitivity, and normative influence; and those anchored in emotional 
experience, including affective response, variety-seeking tendencies, and impulse orientation. Repeated 
purchase behavior serves as the mechanism through which these tactics are internalized, a process 
consistent with operant conditioning principles. 
When consumers were surveyed regarding the determinants of their brand selection, several 
recurring strategic categories were identified. These included price-driven rationales, affective 
preferences, performance evaluations, social influence, habitual repetition, brand allegiance, and 
novelty-seeking motivations. Responses ranged from cost-based explanations to expressions of personal 
affinity and habitual loyalty. 
Hoyer and MacInnis (2008) further observe that cost-oriented heuristics are most frequently 
activated when consumers perceive little meaningful differentiation among available brands and maintain 
low involvement with the choice set. Paradoxically, though price is a primary decision criterion for many 
consumers, price data is seldom retained in memory even following a completed purchase. This occurs 
because pricing information remains consistently accessible within the retail environment, thereby 
diminishing the cognitive motivation to memorize it. Notably, consumers whose decision-making is 
governed by loss aversion demonstrate heightened price sensitivity, whereas those oriented toward 
anticipated gains evaluate brand features alongside pricing considerations as components of perceived 
value. 
According to Alton (2018), pricing decisions rank among the most consequential yet frequently 
underexamined dimensions of business management. A firm's price point exerts influence across virtually 
every operational dimension. Prices set too low may generate volume but undermine profitability; prices 
positioned too high may preserve margins while suppressing unit movement to levels insufficient for 
covering personnel costs. Pricing, in this sense, transcends its numerical expression. 
Gencler (2017) contends that effective pricing strategy extends considerably beyond simple 
competitive matching. Organizations equipped with robust competitive intelligence capabilities and an 
analytical approach to rival monitoring are positioned to achieve meaningful market advantages. Six 
specific tactical frameworks are identified as mechanisms for establishing sustainable differentiation. 

According to Hoyer and MacInnis (2008), emotionally-driven choice tactics function through a 
process of categorical affect retrieval, wherein consumers draw upon stored emotional associations when 
evaluating brand options. This process, termed affect referral or the 'How do I feel about it?' heuristic, is 
illustrated through the Starbucks example: upon encountering the brand name, consumers activate 
pre-existing positive emotional residue, which may then guide selection without engaging in systematic 
attribute evaluation. Research supports this mechanism, demonstrating that under conditions of time 
pressure, consumers preferentially select the option with the strongest affective valence. When afforded 
greater deliberative opportunity, however, rational evaluation reasserts its influence. Affective responses 
to promotional stimuli have also been shown to generalize beyond the advertised offering to encompass 
otherwise unrelated products. 
Vizcaino (2018) documents that affect-oriented merchandising tactics, particularly the strategic 
deployment of window signage, serve a dual communicative function: enhancing visual attractiveness and 
conveying store-specific promotional information. Empirical evidence drawn from both naturalistic and 
experimental settings confirms that window displays generate favorable consumer inferences regarding 
store image and promotional value, which in turn shape patronage intentions. A supplementary 
mechanism is proposed wherein such signage cultivates a sense of place identity, a perception that 
partially mediates the relationship between consumer attitudes toward the display and their resultant 
behavioral intentions. 
Performance-related choice tactics, as documented by Hoyer and MacInnis (2008), are activated 
when the outcome of prior consumption produces positive reinforcement, thereby orienting subsequent 
decision-making toward performance-based criteria. These criteria may manifest as generalized quality 
assessments or as evaluations of specific functional attributes. Consumer satisfaction functions as the 
central mechanism in this dynamic: satisfied purchasers are disposed to internalize positive brand 
appraisals and to engage in repeat purchase behavior accordingly. 
Fallon (2017) situates performance tracking within a broader organizational management 
framework, noting that virtually all enterprises maintain some form of performance metric. Such 
measurement systems provide the evaluative foundation upon which managers and employees establish 
shared expectations, developmental goals, and role alignment with organizational objectives. 
Normative tactics, according to Hoyer and MacInnis (2008), reflect the capacity of social actors 
to shape consumer decision-making, particularly among those with limited experience in a product 
category. Three distinct influence pathways are identified: direct influence, in which others actively seek 
to shape behavior; observational influence, in which consumer behavior is modeled on that of others; and 
indirect influence, in which the anticipated assessments of others constrain choices. The prominence of 

normative influence in digital environments is amplified by the ease with which consumers can access 
one another's expressed opinions. Organizations may capitalize on this dynamic through advertising 
strategies that foreground social validation and stimulate word-of-mouth dissemination. 
Nolan (2014) further contextualizes normative social influence within consumer behavior, noting 
that the phenomenon of social conformity directly shapes purchasing decisions. Practitioners engaged in 
marketing and advertising must develop an understanding of how normative influence operates, as this 
awareness enables the construction of promotional strategies that resonate with prevailing social norms. 
Human beings are, by nature, creatures of routine. As Hoyer and MacInnis (2008) observe, once a 
satisfactory behavioral pattern is established, repetition occurs with minimal deliberation. Daily routines, 
travel patterns, and shopping preferences exemplify this dispositional tendency. Such regularity simplifies 
cognitive demands and enhances manageability in everyday life. 
Habitual decision-making represents one of the most cognitively economical forms of consumer 
behavior, characterized by negligible information-seeking activity and virtually no comparative 
evaluation of alternatives. Importantly, habituation does not require strong brand preference; rather, it is 
sustained by behavioral repetition and routine purchasing. Decision-making governed by habit 
simultaneously reduces perceived risk, as repeated positive experiences with a brand provide confidence 
in its continued suitability. Evidence in support of habitual purchasing behavior is particularly robust in 
contexts involving low-cost, frequently purchased items. However, extended intervals between purchases 
within a product category are associated with reduced likelihood of habitual brand repetition. 
Lindsay (2014) frames habitual purchasing as fundamentally a mechanism for cognitive 
simplification. Consumers who have internalized routinized buying patterns value the reduction in 
decision-making demands that such patterns afford. The failure to adequately account for habit as a 
primary behavioral driver may explain why many marketing initiatives fail to achieve their intended 
return on investment. When brand growth depends on converting consumers from a competitor's product, 
what is effectively being requested is behavioral habit disruption—a substantially more complex 
undertaking than standard persuasion. 
Brand loyalty, in the formulation of Hoyer and MacInnis (2008), emerges when consumers 
engage in deliberate comparative evaluation and determine that a particular brand satisfies their 
requirements more fully than available alternatives, subsequently committing to repeated acquisition of 
that brand. This disposition arises not merely from habitual inertia but from affirmatively reinforced 
performance-based evaluation. Brand loyalty may also crystallize as a function of increasing consumer 
proficiency with a specific offering, such as a financial management software platform. Confronted with 

the learning investment required for brand switching, the loyal consumer is inclined toward continued 
commitment—a phenomenon attributable to cognitive lock-in. 
Kopp (2019) characterizes brand loyalty as the positive attachment consumers form with specific 
products or commercial entities. Consumers exhibiting brand loyalty demonstrate sustained commitment 
to a preferred offering, maintaining their patronage even when exposed to competitive inducements. 
Rouse and Shadley (2019) describe brand loyalty as an affective bond that motivates continued 
and expanded purchasing within a brand's portfolio. Organizations harness brand loyalty as a mechanism 
for driving incremental sales across both existing product lines and brand extensions. This loyalty-based 
commitment establishes behavioral patterns that transcend singular repurchase and extend to 
cross-category purchasing within the same organizational family. In doing so, brand loyalty 
simultaneously functions as a competitive barrier by disinclining consumers from migrating to alternative 
brands. 
According to Hoyer and MacInnis (2008), variety-seeking is a recognized low-effort consumer 
decision strategy characterized by the desire to explore alternatives beyond one's established 
preferences—a phenomenon technically described as assortment exploration. A consumer who habitually 
selects a particular coffee brand may, without any dissatisfaction with that brand, elect to sample a 
competing product before returning to the preferred option. Consumers engage in variety-seeking 
behavior for two primary reasons: satiation and tedium. The experience of satiation, arising from repeated 
consumption of an identical product or stimulus, motivates behavioral diversification. Similarly, repetitive 
decision-making may generate monotony, prompting switching behavior even when the habitual option 
would have produced greater immediate gratification. Social considerations also motivate variety-seeking, 
as consumers may perceive their diversified selections to be evaluated more favorably by external 
observers. Additionally, the perceived cost of switching is a key moderator: consumers who regard brand 
transition as low-cost are more inclined toward variety-seeking than those who do not. 
Adomavicius, Bockstedt, and Curley (2015) document that in the domain of consumable goods, 
variety preference is consistently amplified when selections are made simultaneously as a bundled unit 
rather than as a series of sequential individual choices. 
Positive reinforcement, as explicated by Hoyer and MacInnis (2008), originates principally from 
the subjective experience of fulfillment that arises when a consumer perceives their needs to have been 
adequately addressed. This reinforcing experience amplifies the probability that the same brand will be 
selected in subsequent purchasing occasions. Following the application of any of the seven established 
decision-making tactics, consumers evaluate their acquired brand during the consumption phase, 

generating outcomes that may be categorized as reinforcement (positive affect coupled with repurchase 
intention), absence of reinforcement, or negative reinforcement. 
Reinforcement, in its technical behavioral sense, refers to any stimulus that is contingent upon a 
specific behavior and that functions to increase the probability of that behavior being replicated (Smith, 
2017). In its most straightforward conceptual rendering, positive reinforcement involves the introduction 
of a pleasurable stimulus following the execution of a particular behavior (Cherry, 2018). 
As a foundational construct within Operant Conditioning theory developed by B.F. Skinner, 
positive reinforcement describes the process by which rewarding consequences strengthen behavioral 
repetition. Skinner's experiments with laboratory rodents demonstrated that consistent delivery of a food 
reward following lever activation resulted in progressively more frequent lever-pressing behavior. The 
parallel for human consumers is direct: when a particular purchasing behavior yields satisfying 
consequences, repetition of that behavior is substantially more probable (Cherry, 2018). 
 
Statement of the Problem 
The central aim of this investigation was to determine the nature and magnitude of the association 
between consumer decision-making approaches and behavioral reinforcement outcomes as evidenced 
among M.A.C cosmetics patrons. Specifically, the study sought to address the following inquiries: 
1. In what manner can each of the following consumer decision-making dimensions be characterized: 
   a. Cost-based orientation 
   b. Affect-based orientation 
   c. Performance-based orientation 
   d. Normative orientation 
   e. Habitual orientation 
   f. Brand devotion orientation 
   g. Assortment-seeking orientation 
2. How may the positive reinforcement profile of surveyed participants be generally characterized? 
3. Does a statistically meaningful association exist between consumer decision-making orientations and 
positive reinforcement outcomes? 
 

Research Hypotheses 
H1: Cost-based orientation and positive reinforcement share no significant association. 
H2: Affect-based orientation and positive reinforcement share no significant association. 
H3: Performance-based orientation and positive reinforcement share no significant association. 
H4: Normative orientation and positive reinforcement share no significant association. 
H5: Habitual orientation and positive reinforcement share no significant association. 
H6: Brand devotion orientation and positive reinforcement share no significant association. 
H7: Assortment-seeking orientation and positive reinforcement share no significant association. 
 
Conceptual Framework 
The analytical model underlying this investigation operationalizes consumer decision-making as a 
multi-dimensional independent construct composed of seven discrete orientations: cost-based, 
affect-based, performance-based, normative, habitual, brand devotion, and assortment-seeking. Each 
dimension is posited to exert a measurable directional influence upon the dependent construct of positive 
reinforcement. The framework positions these seven orientations as antecedents of reinforcement 
outcomes, with the strength and nature of each relationship empirically assessed through correlational 
analysis. 
 
Significance of the Study 
The present inquiry carries meaningful implications for multiple constituencies. Its primary utility 
lies in advancing understanding of how low-effort consumer decision-making frameworks operate within 
the cosmetics sector, with particular relevance to M.A.C patrons. The study yields value for the following 
stakeholders: 
For M.A.C Cosmetics: The findings provide actionable intelligence for refining customer 
satisfaction strategies and strengthening the structural conditions for sustained repeat patronage through 
relationship-based communication. 
For Commercial Enterprises Broadly: The study offers transferable insights into how 
decision-making heuristics function across product categories, facilitating more strategically informed 
business development. 

For Consumers: An empirically grounded understanding of the cognitive and behavioral 
mechanisms underlying purchasing choices equips consumers with improved self-awareness in their 
consumption decisions. 
For Prospective Researchers: The study contributes to the cumulative scholarly record on 
consumer behavior and reinforcement dynamics, serving as a reference foundation for future 
investigations. 
 
Scope and Limitation 
The scope of the present study was delimited to the examination of consumer decision-making 
orientation factors as they influence purchasing behavior specifically among M.A.C cosmetics patrons at 
the SM City Clark branch. The study's findings are therefore bounded by the specific characteristics of 
this consumer population and are not intended for generalization beyond it. While the seven factors 
examined constitute an analytically sufficient basis for informing M.A.C's strategic direction, it is 
acknowledged that additional variables may exert influence on consumer choice and reinforcement 
outcomes that fall outside the parameters of this inquiry. 
 
Method 
Research Design 
A quantitative descriptive methodology was employed in this investigation to assess the 
functional role of consumer decision-making orientations as predictors of reinforcement outcomes within 
the M.A.C cosmetics context. Descriptive quantitative designs are distinguished by their capacity to 
characterize relationships between independent and dependent variables without the manipulation 
inherent to experimental designs. The present inquiry did not seek causal inference but rather the 
systematic documentation of associational patterns. 
 
Participants of the Study 
Survey instruments were administered to 306 patrons of the M.A.C brand at SM City Clark. The 
sample was derived using the Raosoft online calculator, which yielded a recommended sample size of 306 
based on a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error, a population proportion of 50%, and an estimated 
total population of 1,500 individuals. Through their responses, participants provided empirical data on 
how each of the seven decision-making orientations influenced their purchasing behavior and their 
subsequent reinforcement experiences relative to M.A.C products. 

 
Research Instruments 
Data collection was conducted through a researcher-constructed survey questionnaire specifically 
designed to capture the perceptions and experiences of M.A.C cosmetics consumers. The instrument was 
structured to elicit responses across all seven decision-making dimensions, with items completed via a 
checkmark system indicating the respondent's level of agreement. 
 
Data Gathering Procedure 
Prior to data collection, formal authorization was sought and obtained from the store management 
of the M.A.C branch at SM City Clark. A pilot study utilizing 40 survey instruments was conducted to 
assess the reliability of the questionnaire prior to full-scale deployment. Following confirmation of 
instrument reliability, surveys were distributed, and completed responses were systematically collected, 
verified, and tallied. 
 
Data Processing and Analysis Procedure 
Survey responses were quantified using a four-point Likert scale, operationalized as follows: 1 = 
Strongly Disagree; 2 = Disagree; 3 = Agree; 4 = Strongly Agree. Weighted mean scores were computed 
for each item and dimension to characterize consumer perceptions. Pearson's product-moment correlation 
coefficient was subsequently applied to examine the directional strength and statistical significance of the 
relationship between each decision-making orientation and positive reinforcement outcomes.

Conclusion 
Analysis of the data revealed that the cost-based and assortment-seeking orientations attained the 
highest composite means among all consumer decision-making dimensions examined, both registering an 
aggregate value of 3.42, classified as Strongly Agree. Within the cost-based dimension, the item 
addressing relative affordability achieved the peak item-level mean of 3.45, while the item addressing 
promotional incentives registered the lowest item-level mean of 3.38. Within the assortment-seeking 
dimension, the item pertaining to foundation texture and scent produced the highest item-level mean of 
3.48, while the lipstick shade exploration item yielded the lowest at 3.36. 

By contrast, the habitual orientation achieved the lowest composite mean across all seven 
dimensions at 3.26, also classified as Strongly Agree. Within this dimension, the item addressing need 
fulfillment registered the highest item-level score at 3.29, while the weekly or monthly frequency item 
recorded the lowest at 3.24—uniquely classified as Agree rather than Strongly Agree. 
Correlational analysis identified the brand devotion orientation as producing the strongest 
association with positive reinforcement (r = 0.475, p = 0.001, Weak Positive Correlation), while the 
habitual orientation generated the weakest associative coefficient (r = 0.244, p = 0.001, Weak Positive 
Correlation). At the aggregate level, all consumer decision-making orientations collectively exhibited a 
Weak Positive Correlation with positive reinforcement. 
 
Recommendation 
M.A.C occupies a pre-eminent position in the professional cosmetics market by virtue of its 
unmatched expertise in cosmetic artistry. Despite this standing, the findings indicate that M.A.C does not 
hold an uncontested position of dominance within the broader market, as competing brands also command 
significant consumer patronage. In response to these findings, the researchers put forward the following 
strategic recommendations: 
The brand should develop formalized consumer loyalty programs designed to incentivize and 
sustain regular customer engagement through the provision of meaningful benefits to high-frequency 
patrons. Exclusive rewards for regular customers would not only elevate their experiential satisfaction but 
would also catalyze organic social dissemination. Research conducted as part of this study consistently 
revealed that product packaging constitutes a meaningful point of consumer evaluation. M.A.C is 
therefore encouraged to invest in packaging designs that communicate product attributes effectively and 
attract consumer attention. Furthermore, the development and promotion of environmentally responsible 
product formulations aligned with consumer preferences for sustainability is recommended as a means of 
broadening the brand's appeal and reinforcing its value proposition. 
 
Definition of Terms 
Consumer Decision-Making Approaches – Abbreviated cognitive heuristics employed by 
purchasers to facilitate low-exertion selections. 
Cost-Based Orientation – A purchasing heuristic predicated upon price as the dominant 
evaluative criterion. 

Affect-Based Orientation – A purchasing approach governed by emotional or affective states. 
Performance-Based Orientation – A purchasing approach anchored in functional assessment, 
feature evaluation, or brand appraisal. 
Normative Orientation – A purchasing approach wherein selections are shaped by the actual or 
perceived opinions of significant others. 
Habitual Orientation – Behavioral repetition of a previously established purchasing pattern 
without deliberative evaluation. 
Brand Devotion Orientation – Sustained commitment to a specific brand driven by strong 
affective or performance-based preference. 
Assortment-Seeking Orientation – A disposition toward exploring novel or differentiated product 
options. 
Positive Reinforcement – A behavioral outcome arising from the perceived fulfillment of 
consumer needs, which increases the probability of repurchase.
