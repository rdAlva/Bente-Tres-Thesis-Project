Entrepreneurial Inclinations Among University Undergraduates in 
Oman: 
Examining the Influence of Institutional Image, Mentorship Figures, 
Pedagogical Strategies, 
Academic Syllabus Design, and Instructional Modalities 
Joefrelin Collado Ines, Marilou Q. Tolentino, Cirilo E. Mirano Jr., Reynald A. Funtinilla 
Abstract 
Conducted at the University of Technology and Applied Sciences (UTAS) during the first 
semester of Academic Year 2020–2021 — a period coinciding with the height of the 
COVID-19 health crisis — this investigation examined undergraduate students' 
orientations toward enterprise creation. The central analytical objective was to uncover 
the primary determinants shaping respondents' propensity to pursue self-employment. 
Five distinct dimensions were interrogated: the perceived societal standing of 
entrepreneurship as a vocation, the influence exerted by mentorship figures and 
entrepreneurial role models, the design and content of the relevant academic 
curriculum, the nature of personal independent learning orientations, and the holistic 
attitudinal dispositions of participants with respect to undertaking entrepreneurial 
ventures amidst pandemic conditions. 
Analytically, descriptive statistics were employed to characterize the dataset, Cronbach 
Alpha coefficients were computed to establish instrument reliability, and multivariate 
tests of distributional normality — specifically skewness and kurtosis assessments — 
were conducted to confirm data suitability. A non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test was 
subsequently applied to determine the statistical significance of the results. Broadly, the 
examined constructs generated favorable responses; the evidence suggests an 
increased disposition among students to engage in business creation and 
entrepreneurial activity. 
Keywords: Entrepreneurial Motivation, Pedagogical Approach, Syllabus Content, 
Course Delivery, University Students, Oman. 
 
1. Introduction 
For recent graduates or individuals who have grown dissatisfied or fatigued by 
conventional organizational employment, the prospect of launching small enterprises or 
pursuing independent business ventures presents itself as a perpetually available 
alternative. Across Oman's economic sectors, discourse surrounding entrepreneurship 
has gained considerable momentum; governmental bodies have championed numerous 

initiatives designed to cultivate entrepreneurial interest, while stakeholders including 
policymakers, private enterprises, commercial chambers, women's development 
organizations, academic institutions, and university students have each expressed 
active engagement with the concept. Given that unemployment continues to constitute a 
structural concern within Oman, self-directed employment has emerged as not merely a 
viable option but, for many — particularly among the university-educated population — 
a more fulfilling and rewarding professional trajectory. 
Entrepreneurship occupies a foundational position in the architecture of national 
economic growth, technological innovation, and competitive positioning within the global 
marketplace (Scarborough and Zimmerer, 2003; Kuratko and Hodgetts, 2004). Its 
academic and policy prominence has intensified: as Lee, Chang, and colleagues (2005) 
observed, the subject has ascended to among the most heavily researched domains 
within management and business scholarship, precisely because of growing recognition 
of its contributions to employment generation and productivity. In response, students 
globally have demonstrated heightened interest in acquiring the skills necessary to 
operate ventures or generate employment independently (Brown, 1999; Henry, 2003). 
Accordingly, higher education institutions across the world have moved deliberately 
toward embedding entrepreneurship as both an academic discipline and a recognized 
professional pathway (Postigo and Tamborini, 2002). 
The principal aim of this investigation was to systematically analyze the entrepreneurial 
motivational structures of students enrolled at the University of Technology and Applied 
Sciences. The study profiles the respondent cohort, evaluates the forces propelling 
students toward entrepreneurial ambition, and identifies the specific characteristics and 
exposures that drive or inhibit such aspirations. The significance of the entrepreneurship 
subject itself in channeling and reinforcing these orientations was also assessed. 
Outcomes of the study are intended to inform institutional strategies for cultivating 
entrepreneurial interest and readiness among the student population. 
Entrepreneurship education has been widely recognized as the foundational 
mechanism through which academic institutions stimulate and operationalize 
entrepreneurial intent among students (Linan, 2004). Its integration into formal curricula 
reflects a broader institutional recognition that entrepreneurship must not only be 
theorized but actively taught, practiced, and internalized (EC, 2008). The discipline has 
accordingly been embedded across multiple institutional contexts and program levels. 
Across developing and lower-income nations, entrepreneurship has increasingly been 
examined as a potential structural remedy for entrenched poverty and economic 
exclusion (Resurrection, 2011). Governmental actors in these contexts have been active 
in constructing programmatic supports and institutional frameworks designed to 
mobilize entrepreneurial energy — particularly among younger demographics — as a 
mechanism for economic participation and social uplift. Progress has nonetheless been 
uneven, with students' orientations remaining shaped in significant part by concerns 
relating to economic access and stability. 
Academic institutions face a substantial challenge in translating entrepreneurial 
competency development into pedagogical practice, particularly in reconciling 
curriculum design with effective instructional methodology (Garavan and O'Cinneide, 

1994). Entrepreneurship, when properly positioned, affords students a dual benefit: the 
attainment of financial autonomy and a meaningful contribution to the broader 
socioeconomic fabric through job creation, innovation stimulation, and economic 
expansion. Within the Omani context specifically, the development of entrepreneurial 
culture among graduates and students is regarded as a strategic lever for economic 
diversification, enabling young people to function not merely as participants in the 
workforce but as generators of it. 
 
2. Methodology 
The methodological framework for this investigation integrated reliability assessment 
with normality testing and inferential statistical analysis. Instrument dependability was 
evaluated through Cronbach Alpha reliability coefficients applied across the study's key 
measurement variables. Distributional normality was examined through both univariate 
and multivariate skewness and kurtosis calculations to ensure the appropriateness of 
the analytical approach. Because parametric assumptions were not fully met, the 
Mann-Whitney U non-parametric test was selected as the primary inferential tool, 
enabling p-value computation for evaluating evidence against the formulated research 
objectives. 
Participants 
The study drew its participant base from students concurrently enrolled in 
Entrepreneurship coursework during the third semester of Academic Year 2020–2021 at 
the University of Technology and Applied Sciences, Shinas campus, Oman. The Google 
Forms-based survey instrument was distributed to 94 eligible and targeted respondents 
via digital link. Following a validation process that excluded submissions with critical 
unanswered items, 88 responses were confirmed as analytically usable. 
Data Analysis 
Descriptive analysis techniques were applied to tabulate and initially evaluate the 
collected responses. Reliability was quantified through the Cronbach Alpha scale, while 
normality was verified across both univariate and multivariate dimensions using 
skewness and kurtosis diagnostics. The Mann-Whitney U non-parametric test 
generated the inferential p-values used to evaluate the weight of evidence relative to the 
study's research propositions. All closed-ended questionnaire items were anchored to a 
five-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) through 5 (strongly agree), 
with a midpoint of 3 (no opinion). 
 
3. Results 
Of the 94 questionnaires distributed, 88 were accepted as valid for analytical purposes. 
Male respondents (49) outnumbered female respondents (39), yielding a gender 

distribution of 55.7% male to 44.3% female. Disciplinary composition was dominated by 
Engineering students, who constituted 58 respondents (65.9%), with the remaining 30 
respondents (34.1%) drawn from Information Technology programs. 
Reliability Analysis 
Cronbach Alpha coefficients were calculated for each of the six primary construct 
variables included in the study's instrument. The results confirmed high internal 
consistency across all dimensions. The lowest reliability coefficient was registered for 
the Personal Independent Learning construct (PIL = 0.769), while the Entrepreneurial 
Curriculum and Content construct recorded the strongest reliability (ECC = 0.949). All 
other constructs — Entrepreneurial Inclination (EI = 0.827), Image of Entrepreneurship 
(IE = 0.869), Role Models (RM = 0.837), and Entrepreneurship Internship Programs 
(EIP = 0.875) — fell within an acceptable to strong reliability range. These values affirm 
that the variables selected for subsequent correlation analysis are methodologically 
sound.

Application of the Mann-Whitney U test produced a substantive body of evidence 
supporting the original research propositions. Across every tested construct — role 
model influence, the perceived societal image of entrepreneurship, academic curriculum 
and content quality, independent learning orientation, and the holistic attitudinal 
disposition of respondents — a consistent motivational effect on entrepreneurial 
engagement was identified. Students' affirmative stance toward entrepreneurship as a 
viable professional trajectory — both within the pandemic period and extending beyond 
it — was robustly affirmed. 
The range of significance values extended from 0.052 (observed for EI2) to 0.996 (for 
ECC7). While the majority of p-values fell comfortably above the conventional 
significance threshold, the broad pattern of results reinforces the general directional 
finding that students maintained positive entrepreneurial orientations.

4. Conclusion 
Within the unprecedented context of a global health pandemic, this investigation 
systematically analyzed the multiple factors bearing upon entrepreneurial motivation 
among students at the University of Technology and Applied Sciences. Taken 
collectively, the study's findings corroborate the established scholarly position that 
entrepreneurship education, institutional image cultivation, mentorship exposure, 
curriculum quality, preferred learning styles, and instructional delivery mechanisms all 
function as meaningful drivers of entrepreneurial engagement (Edwards and Muir, 2005; 
Postigo, Iacobucci, et al., 2006; Nurmi and Paasio, 2007). 
This pattern of association is interpretable through the lens of students' escalating 
demand for academically rigorous, vocationally relevant instruction from institutions 
capable of equipping them with the entrepreneurial competencies required for 
professional relevance. The evidence further demonstrates that higher education 
institutions can serve as incubators for entrepreneurial cultural formation during the 
formative years of students' academic development. It follows, therefore, that Higher 
Education Institutions (HEIs) bear a structural obligation to architect institutional 
environments that not only tolerate but actively cultivate and sustain an 
entrepreneurship-oriented culture. 
Exposure to entrepreneurship-specific coursework has been shown to meaningfully 
shape students' enthusiasm and readiness for independent business creation. The 
evidence underscores the centrality of academic institutions in galvanizing 
self-employment as a viable and desirable outcome. Universities play a concrete 
developmental role in shaping an entrepreneurially oriented mindset among their 
students. HEIs seeking to translate this potential into action must build environments 
where entrepreneurial risk-taking is normalized and supported. Entrepreneurship should 
be woven into the fabric of the teaching and learning experience rather than positioned 
as an elective or supplementary dimension, with students actively supported in 
conceptualizing and preparing their own ventures. 
Programmatic design that integrates student-centered needs alongside industry-aligned 
expectations will significantly strengthen students' motivation to pursue business 
creation. Immersive, experiential learning modalities — through which students are 
exposed to working enterprises and entrepreneurial practitioners — generate a catalytic 
effect on entrepreneurial intent. HEIs' instructional portfolios should thus include 

structured business visits, practitioner engagement through guest lectures, and the 
coordination of co-curricular entrepreneurial activities designed to ignite and sustain 
student interest. 
Curriculum design must evolve beyond passive lecture-based instruction toward active, 
applied pedagogical formats. Course materials and content should be dynamically 
aligned with the prevailing business environment, reflecting both local market realities 
and global trends. Competency-based pedagogical models — emphasizing student 
agency, applied problem-solving, and reflective practice — should be embedded within 
all entrepreneurship modules. The value orientation required for entrepreneurship must 
be treated not as supplementary but as a constitutive dimension of every relevant 
curriculum component. Perhaps most importantly, academic institutions must reframe 
entrepreneurship not as a fallback career option but as a primary and mainstream 
employment pathway — one that is systematically normalized, resourced, and 
celebrated within the institutional culture.
