Prof. Marilou Q. Tolentino, DBA — Dean, College of Business, Systems Plus College Foundation, Angeles City,

Abstract 
The study aimed to identify the motivation behind the attitude of Filipino millennials in their purchase 
and consumption of luxury brands. Various studies in other nations, demographic profiles, and different 
cultural contexts had provided rich knowledge and vantage point on the perspectives of buyers on luxury 
brands towards purchase and use. The attitude of Filipino millennials regarding their actual and ideal 
self-congruity towards the luxury brand is the main primary focus of the study, i.e., determining the 
factors that influence or motivate Filipino millennials in the purchase and consumption of luxury brands. 
Furthermore, additional considerations of materialistic value and status values were examined regarding 
how and to what extent they affect consumer motivation. The research questions and the hypothesis of the 
study were handled and resolved through quantitative research. The study utilized a survey method using 
a structured survey questionnaire given to university students, young graduates, and young professionals 
classified as millennials in various organizations. The study found out that significantly, the motivation 
indicating that Filipino millennial consumers' towards ideal self-congruency are higher than the effect of 
actual self-congruity towards their attitudes on luxury brands. The influence is clearly defined from 
among the respondents in terms of their attitude towards materialistic value and prestige value. The 
implication of the study will provide a body of knowledge to both business and academic perspectives. 
The research results will be a scientific foundation towards understanding the brand attitude of Filipino 
millennials which make up one-third of the country's total population; considerably half of the labor force 
population. The effect of variables used in the study was not studied in depth in the Philippine context. 
Moreover, the results of the study will contribute to luxury brand managers who need direction in 
choosing the right marketing mix and marketing strategies when targeting millennial consumers. 
Keywords: consumer attitude, luxury brand, Filipino millennials, actual and ideal self-congruity, 
purchase and consumption, motivational factors. 
 
Introduction 
The luxury industry, as monitored by Bain, includes both luxury goods and experiences. It has nine 
segments, led by luxury cars, luxury hospitality and personal luxury goods, which together account for 
more than 80% of the total market. Overall, the luxury market grew 5% in 2018, to an estimated €1.2 
trillion globally, with positive results across most segments. Sales of luxury cars continued to lead the 
market, growing 5% at constant exchange rates to €495 billion. Luxury experiences remained very 
appealing to consumers, as shown by sales growth of luxury hospitality (up 5% from last year), gourmet 
food and fine dining (up 6%) and luxury cruises (up 7%). Moreover, online purchases reached an 8% 
market share, and overall discounted sales reached 37% of the total market (D'Arpizio et al., 2016). 
Luxury brand purchases from among younger generations, generation-y or the millennials, i.e., people 
born in the 1980s and 1990s value luxury products at a younger age than the traditional luxury customers 
had increased considerably (The Economist, 2014). 

They are also very different in their buying behavior, being more spontaneous, experimenting, and 
exploring new brands. Thus, they are a contrast of the traditional luxury consumers that have more 
consistent spending behavior and brand loyalty (Jay, 2012). It is projected that by 2026 the main 
consumers of luxury will be the millennials and, therefore, managers need to adapt their marketing and 
brand management strategies accordingly to capture this large and rapidly growing customer segment to 
outperform the competition (The Economist, 2014). 
The brand is a central part of consumer decision making. However, currently, there is a limited amount of 
research regarding the factors of luxury consumption and luxury brand management. Particularly the 
connection between consumer perception of luxury and what causes customers to purchase luxury brands 
is rightly understood and widely unexplored (Hennigs et al., 2013). This is a classic case of the 
Philippines. Luxury brands can satisfy consumers' psychological and functional needs, having a 
combination of four dimensions of value: financial, operational, individual, and social value (Wiedmann 
et al., 2007). From these four different luxury values, the customers can be grouped by focusing on 
personally oriented vs. socially-oriented customers. In other words, whether luxury brands are purchased 
for their pleasure or demonstration of success (Kapferer & Bastien, 2009). Therefore, luxury brands need 
to deliver value by customers' subjective expectations and individual perceptions (Hennigs et al., 2013). 
Millennials are now the subject of many studies, which is a puzzling phenomenon to generations of 
humans. The generation is socially connected and obsessed with social media and IT applications and 
infrastructure. One-third of the Filipinos are millennials age ranges from 15 to 35 years old. The 
Millennials share the same values and identities that are of different layers. Accordingly, there are 
different versions of Millennials depending on what they want to show the world. Within this perspective, 
brands should engage more the Millennials and converting them into their marketability and affiliation. 
Posit to this, and the millennial adhere to integrity based on shared experience rather than in the power of 
authority. 
 
Literature Review 
The term luxury is widely used, and an absolute definition for it does not exist. What is luxury for some is 
not for others (Kapferer & Bastien, 2014). However, there are specific attributes that are used to define 
luxury, which is: excellent quality, high price, scarcity and uniqueness, aesthetics and polysensuality, 
ancestral heritage and personal history, and superfluousness (Dubois et al., 2001). 
Luxury is not the same as fashion or premium, even though these concepts are linked to each other and 
borrow associations from each other (Kapferer & Bastien, 2014: 35). However, by only having a 
particular attribute of the previously mentioned, such as high price, is not enough by itself to define 
luxury. Therefore, the upper-range branded premium products are often confused with luxury. Whereas 
for premium products price is based on functionality, performance, and quality, for leisure, it is related to 
scarcity, brand, and storytelling (Turunen, 2015). On the other hand, many luxury brands adopt the 
behavior of fashion brands, whereas fashion seeks to belong to the world of luxury to improve their status 
(Kapferer & Bastien, 2014). 
Furthermore, luxury products are related to being a dream, whereas essential products correspond to a 
need, and in between are the branded products corresponding to a desire that lasts for a while, and they 
are substitutable. Dreams are beyond requirements or want and are what luxury products respond to and 
separates luxury products from non-luxury products (Kapferer & Bastien, 2014). Purchasing a luxury 
product is buying into a dream, and these purchases are often impulsive, emotional or extravagant 
(Dubois & Paternault, 1995). Consequently, luxury is an industry with a high level of profitability with 
high margins in luxury product prices, and top brand equity and brands' fame generate additional value for 
expensive, original quality products (Kapferer, 1997). 

Luxury brands can be grouped by the degree of luxury, i.e., the accessibility level, divided into accessible, 
intermediate, and inaccessible luxury brands (De Barnier et al., 2011). Other proposed luxury degree 
categorizations that are based on previous literature are summarized in existing studies (Turunen, 2015: 
122). Mainly various brand extensions of luxury brands expanding to new categories have caused 
additional unclarity of how to define luxury. When luxury brands introduce lower-end extensions, 
consumers get a taste of the luxury brand at this accessible product level of, for example, perfumes 
(Patrick & Prokopec, 2015). To understand consumer motivations for purchasing luxury, it is important to 
understand the underlying value that the product has for the consumer when in use (Ballantyne & Varey, 
2006). People consume products in different ways, and the interpersonal and personal actions vary for 
different luxury consumers (Holt, 1995). Consumers seek to create a meaningful life regarding personal 
identity and social relationships so that products consumed contribute to the consumer's particular quality 
of life through the enjoyment coming from owning and consuming products and experiences (Pollay, 
1983; Solomon et al., 2016). The consumer perception of luxury brands affects thus the relevance of 
consumption. To understand how consumers individualize their opinions of luxury and how luxury brands 
change their daily lives, consumers' consumption values should be investigated (Seo & Buchanan-Oliver, 
2014). 
Luxury consumers can be split into heavy users and occasional users, according to Kapferer and Bastien 
(2014). Until about the year 2000 luxury market was mostly growing because of a large number of 
middle-class consumers purchasing luxury occasionally. Nowadays, the first volume of luxury purchases 
comes from customers that consume frequently. The Western middle classes are increasingly worried 
about their future, thus, feeling poorer and moving towards the less affluent categories, whereas the 
wealthy purchasers have maintained their optimism, making up for the majority of the luxury market. 
This polarization of the market has allowed luxury brands to grow sales of their entry-level masstige 
products to the middle-class consumers who have kept their aspirations but are now more deliberate about 
their spending. On the other hand, young Chinese middle-class customers are the opposite of the popular 
Western ones as, even though they have significantly less money, their income is noticeably increasing, so 
they feel more optimistic and prosperous, making the luxury market in China very dynamic. A similar 
pattern could be explaining the growing numbers of generation-y consumers purchasing luxury, 
particularly after the recession of 2008 as they increasingly look for self-indulgence through luxury 
consumption (Jay, 2012). 
In a research done by Kapferer (1998) with an international sample of affluent young consumers, four 
types of luxury clients were identified in terms of what luxury means to them and what characteristics 
they value in luxury products. Firstly, it is the group that appreciates the beauty, excellence, and 
uniqueness of the luxury product. Secondly, for the next segment, the focus is on luxury products' 
creativity and sensuality. Thirdly, the luxury value is based on timelessness and international reputation, 
thus being a safe option. And finally, the fourth type of benefit is focusing on rarity and the most 
inaccessible products, these being the buyers of the most expensive items. It was also found that the 
fourth group consisted mostly of males, whereas the second segment mainly consisted of females. This 
could give some idea of differences between luxury consumption value distinction between males and 
females, indicating that males consume more the obvious signs of success, whereas females value more 
creativity, sensuality, and beauty. In this aspect, young males instead consume the sign to show their 
progress. Also, the third group of consumers is motivated by active and visible signs and well-recognized 
brand names. In contrast, the second group is more concerned about showing their individuality, and the 
first group sees themselves as connoisseurs, aesthetes and capable of appreciating exceptional products. 
Another type of segmentation of luxury consumers is done by Han et al. (2010). This study takes the 
aspect of consumers' motivational factors further, distinguishing customers based on the role of brand 
prominence for signaling status, meaning the conspicuousness of the logo on a product. The more a 
consumer needs status, the more they use these so-called loud signals of showing the brand logo on 
products they consume. The opposite is for people with a low need for status. Consumers with high 

wealth and lesser need of status instead signal between each other with quiet, not so distinct brand signals, 
the logo not being conspicuously displayed. Their premium-priced luxury products are recognized by 
other people belonging to the same customer segment. On the other hand, wealthy customers in need of 
status consume loud luxury products to signal the less affluent that they are not one of them. And the less 
affluent with a high demand for status is consuming full, mostly counterfeit luxury products since they 
cannot afford the real ones, to mimic the more affluent and want to be perceived higher class. Therefore, 
consumers can be influenced by their group, those that they aspire to be like, and those with whom they 
do not want to be associated with (Han et al., 2010). 
According to Veblen (1965), accumulation of wealth does not always give a person status, but rather the 
evidence of wealth confers status. This can explain the obvious consumption of luxury as the symbol of 
one's success. People often define others through objects that are consumed by them. People also regard 
their possessions as parts of themselves. Furthermore, individuals might use personal belongings, 
particularly luxury products, to help define themselves and show their own identity. An individual might 
consume his or her products to express a distinct sense of being or can also express belonging to a 
particular group. With material possessions, people seek happiness and remind themselves of experiences, 
accomplishments and other people. Products that people possess convey a sense of past show who they 
are, where they come from, and where they are going (Belk, 1988). 
Not all brands are equally luxurious, and, as discussed earlier, luxury does not have one clear definition. 
Also, the degree of luxury for the same brand can be considered and perceived on a different level in 
different product categories and different situations. For example, Cartier may have a higher perceived 
luxury in the jewelry category and lower perceived luxury in the apparel or fragrance category. In the 
same way, Rolls-Royce is considered a luxury brand in the car category, but not a luxury brand in airplane 
engines type (Vigneron & Johnson, 2004; Kapferer & Bastien, 2014). 
To help distinguish luxury brands, Vigneron and Johnson (2004) have developed a valuation model for 
measuring the amount of luxury in a given brand. This Brand Luxury Index (BLI) shall consist of five 
perceived dimensions of a luxury brand. This framework includes three non-personal perceptions which 
are perceived conspicuousness, perceived uniqueness, and perceived quality. Furthermore, there are two 
personal-oriented perceptions of perceived hedonism and perceived the extended self. According to 
Vigneron and Johnson (2004), these five are the key luxury dimensions that should be established and 
monitored for having a lasting luxury brand. A brand needs to manage both the personal and interpersonal 
perceptions for the long-lasting financial success of a luxury brand (Kapferer & Bastien, 2014). 
Moreover, these five main perceptional factors are interrelated and are underlying in the decision-making 
process of a consumer when assessing luxury brands. Consumers value all these five dimensions. 
However, some aspects have different significance for different customers. The BLI scale can also be 
used for comparing different luxury brands and, thus, for recognizing competitive advantages identified in 
the target market (Vigneron & Johnson, 2004). 
According to Berthon et al. (2009), consumers' perceptions of luxury brands have three value dimensions: 
functional, experiential, and symbolic. Vickers and Renald (2003) also argue that there is a basic 
difference between these three dimensions for luxury brands and non-luxury brands as consumers expect 
more from luxury brands. Since these three value dimensions of luxury are contextual, they can change 
over time depending on the individual and socio-cultural beliefs, thus being co-created between a luxury 
brand and the consumers (Tynan et al., 2010). 
The functional value dimension refers to the material representation of an object and the core benefit and 
essential utilities, such as quality, functionality, reliability, durability, or craftsmanship. In this value 
dimension, especially quality is vital since it shows how well the product can perform and what it does. 

The experiential value dimension represents the individual subjective value, referring to an individual's 
taste and where one finds hedonic value in a brand. This can be triggered by brand experience related 
stimuli that are part of the brand's identity, such as design, packaging, communication, and environments, 
affecting consumer's sensations, feelings, cognitions, perceptions, and behavioral responses (Berthon et 
al., 2009). Hence, luxury products provide need- or want-satisfying consumption experiences (Holbrook, 
1999). Luxury being perceived as rare, precious and unique can guide individual thoughts and feelings 
towards a luxury brand because of uniqueness-seeking motivations as well, besides the hedonic motives 
(Hung et al., 2011). Experiential value, thus, answers what does the brand means to the individual. 
The symbolic value dimension looks into what the brand means to others and the symbolic nature of the 
brand in the social collective (Berthon et al., 2009). The nominal value indicates conspicuousness, 
expensiveness and wealth (Hung et al., 2011). It is very important for premium-priced products and 
services as the symbolic nature signifies the developed dream and brand story. There is an aspect of what 
luxury brand signals to others about the person consuming the brand, i.e., other-directed signaling, such as 
showing wealth, prestige, performance, or style (Berthon et al., 2009). The symbolic dimension also has 
the self-directed value aspect by what the signaling has to the signaler, extending one's self and one's 
conspicuousness (Belk, 1988). However, a brand's symbolic value works only in a community, it is 
recognized at, and when being outside of this community, the social purpose is diminished. 
The symbolic value is a particularly important consideration for global luxury brands' management and 
marketing as social influence affects the purchase intention, particularly for socially oriented consumers 
(Tsai, 2005). Luxury brands need to manage well all these three dimensions and not overly focus on one 
size, neglecting the other two. Luxury brands should be a combination of material (objective, functional), 
individual (subjective, experiential), and social (collective, symbolic) spheres (Berthon et al. 2009). 
Wiedmann et al. (2007) have developed a conceptual framework. It shows that financial, functional, 
individual, and social dimensions affect the consumers' luxury value perception and consumption. These 
four key luxury value dimensions are strongly correlated but not identical with each other, and influence 
consumers' overall luxury value perception. The sub-dimensions of these four critical dimensions of 
luxury value perception are price value (financial value), usability value, quality value and uniqueness 
value (functional values), self-identity value, hedonic value and materialistic value (individual values), 
conspicuousness value and prestige value (social values). Even if the overall luxury level of a brand may 
be perceived equally, the critical luxury dimensions are seen differently by different consumer segments. 
The luxury value model by Wiedmann et al. (2007) helps to understand the conditions and drivers of 
luxury perception and, thus, consumer behavior as well. It, therefore, also helps to create, market and 
monitor luxury brands. 
In general, Wiedmann et al. (2007) model seems to be the most complex and comprehensive. However, 
taking into account various previous research regarding the topic, there could also be other values that 
could be taken into consideration. For example, Ciornea et al. (2011) have added investment value to the 
financial value dimension and heritage value to the functional value dimension of the model. Many 
sub-dimensions could be added to the proposed benefits, such as superfluousness, which relates closely to 
the amount of materialism (Ciornea et al., 2011). 
Filipino millennials are addicted to gadgets, which financially drain their pockets, to a certain extent, used 
ICT to save and get discounts on airfare tickets, food packages, travel deals, and other offers. Research by 
Visa found that 80% of the Filipino Millennials prefer cash transactions over using credit cards due to 
secrecy and confidentiality concerns. According to Madelaine B. Miraflor, the purchasing power of 
millennials rapidly increases, which shows in buying personal care products, home care, and food items in 
retail stores, a notion supported by Kantar Worldpanel. 
 

Background of the Study 
The limited study on the attitude of Filipino Millennials in the purchase and consumption of luxury 
brands had been the primary interest of the study to establish scientific results for further studies that will 
provide a body of knowledge to both business and academic purposes. The effect of variables used in the 
study based on the Philippine context is minimal; the subject area was not yet thoroughly researched 
during the conduct of the study. 
 
Significance of the Study 
The research results will be a scientific basis towards understanding the brand attitude of Filipino 
millennials, which make up one-third of the country's total population; considerably half of the labor force 
population. 
 
Objectives of the Study 
The purpose of this study is to study the effect that actual and ideal self-congruity has on millennial 
consumers' brand attitudes towards luxury brands. Furthermore, the impact of worldly value and status 
value dimensions is studied in terms of to what extent they affect millennials' brand attitudes regarding 
luxury brands. It aims to add to the knowledge of understanding young consumers' brand attitudes and 
further buying behavior regarding luxury brands to maximize the commercial potential of this particular 
target group. The study purposively aims to determine: 
•​
Determine the motivation from among the Filipino Millennials in their purchase and consumption 
of luxury brands in terms of actual self-congruity and ideal self-congruity. 
•​
Know the variables across the educational group, income groups, gender groups and age groups 
that significantly affect the purchase and consumption of luxury brands from Filipino Millennials. 
•​
Determine the effect of materialistic value, status value, and social desirability scale results on the 
purchase and use of luxury brands from Filipino Millennials. 
 
Statement of the Problem 
This study focuses on the effects of actual and ideal self-congruity and how a consumer's self-image and 
luxury brand-image congruity affects brand attitude regarding luxury brands. The ideal self-image is the 
way an individual wants to be or would like to see him or herself and the meaning of self that the 
individual wishes others to associate with him or her, including attitudes, perceptions and feeling (Ross, 
1971; Schenk & Holman, 1980). Luxury brand consumption has a positive effect on approaching the ideal 
self-congruity by conveying the image and symbolic meaning of luxury brands to improve an individual's 
perfect self-image. Self-concept is of value to an individual, an individual's behavior is directed toward 
the improvement of own self-concept through consumption of luxury goods and brands as symbols to 
communicate the symbolic meaning to the individual him or herself and others (Grubb & Grathwohl, 
1967). Brand attitude is an important component for valuing a brand's equity. Brand attitude is an 
individual's overall evaluation of a brand, and it depends mainly on a consumer's perceptions of the brand 
(Aaker, 1997). Additionally, self-congruity can be used to explain and predict brand attitude and purchase 
intention (Sirgy et al., 1997; Helgeson & Supphellen, 2004). Self-identity value, materialistic value, and 

status value are closely related to each other (Richins & Dawson, 1992; Dittmar, 1994; Webster & Beatty, 
1997). 
The study's research questions include: 
•​
What extent are the Filipino Millennials motivated in their purchase and consumption of luxury 
brands in terms of actual self-congruity and ideal self-congruity? 
•​
What is the effect of materialistic value and status value on the purchase and consumption of 
luxury brands from Filipino Millennials? 
•​
Which variables across the educational group, income groups, gender groups and age groups 
significantly affect the purchase and consumption of luxury brands from among Filipino 
Millennials? 
•​
Which item shows significant correlations on the purchase and consumption of luxury brands 
among Filipino Millennials? 
 
Methodology 
The purpose of this study is to look into the influence of actual and ideal self-congruity when forming 
attitudes towards luxury brands. The study uses the descriptive research design which aims to study the 
relationship between the variables in the Philippine context. The survey was used for this study. Data was 
collected by using a questionnaire and then standardizing the data for easy comparison. The study used 
primary and secondary data. The primary data was gathered to answer the research question and to meet 
the research objectives. The questionnaire contains closed questions with pre-set answers to choose from, 
not providing open items. Thus, it is a highly structured questionnaire as the answers in respondents' own 
words are held to the minimum. 
To specify luxury and non-luxury, some well-known brands in the Philippines were shown for both 
categories for respondents to choose from at the beginning of the questionnaire so that they can visualize 
a brand for the following questions. The study used a Likert Scale ranging from 1 to 5, i.e., from strongly 
disagree to agree on the response scale strongly. The study also used a shortened version of the 
Crowne-Marlowe social desirability scale (Crowne & Marlowe, 1960) to assess to what extent 
respondents are concerned with social approval, as social desirability bias is considered to be one of the 
most common biases affecting the results of survey research (King & Bruner, 2000). 
The respondents of the study were Filipino millennials from a higher education institution, young 
entrepreneurs, employees, employers and graduates who consume luxury brands were chosen to collect 
the data from. The sample size was 134 respondents for this study which is sufficient enough to fulfill the 
requirements of efficiency, representativeness, reliability, and flexibility according to Kothari (2004). The 
study used the non-probability, convenience sampling selection based on the easiness of getting 
responses. The questionnaire was distributed through the help of OJT students and alumni as enumerators. 
 
Data Analysis 
The analysis of the data collected from the survey questionnaire has been done using SPSS, where the 
collected information was analyzed with the use of statistical tools. Particularly descriptive statistics have 
been used to describe and to compare variables numerically. 
 

Results 
First, descriptive statistics were analyzed to generate background participants. The hypotheses were tested 
to validate the relationship of actual and ideal self-congruity concerning the materialistic value and status 
value which would reflect the brand attitudes towards luxury brands, looking into non-luxury brands. The 
study gathered data from 134 respondents. 
The sample consisted of 47.8% (64) male respondents and 52.2% (70) female respondents. Regarding the 
education level of the sample, in terms of currently studying or the highest degree completed, the sample 
consisted of 51.5% (69) Bachelor, 7.5% (10) Master, and 3.7% (5) Ph.D., 14.2% (19) Secondary, and 
23.1% (31) other respondents. The age range of the sample was from 18 to 37, with a median of 25 and a 
mean of 27.5. Thus, the distribution of gender is quite good, whereas the sample consists of a high 
number of respondents obtaining or obtained a master's degree. For the sample, the income level was not 
unusually high as most respondents were currently full-time students. As a result, it does not take the 
results of this question into account for the analysis. 
The Marlowe-Crowne social desirability scale results show that more answers were totaled in the socially 
desirable responses (698 / 74.42%), implying that the survey results should be relatively reliable and 
respondents are, in particular, looking for social desirability in their answers to the questionnaire.

Conclusion 
The Filipino millennials attitude in the purchase and consumption luxury brand are more motivated due to 
ideal self congruity than on actual self congruity. The marketers should consider that Filipino millennials 
are motivated in the purchase and consumption of luxury brand based on their ideal self congruity, hence 
they: 
•​
Would like to see themselves on the luxury brand. 
•​
Would like people they look up to consume the luxury brand. 
•​
Would like to be identified with those people who consume the luxury. 
 
Recommendations 
The marketers should consider that Filipino millennials are motivated in the purchase and consumption of 
luxury brand based on their ideal self congruity, hence: 
•​
The luxury brand should be relatable, consistent, conform, coincide or in agreement with their 
ideal self. 
•​
The marketers should consider endorsers, models, advertising theme/slogans that are in 
agreement with ideal self of the Filipino millennials which focuses on functionality, 
image/reputation, and social desirability in the promotional strategy, packaging, and distribution 
channel which are construed as high end and posh. 
•​
Would like to see themselves on the luxury brand, would like people they look up to consume the 
luxury brand, and would like to be identified with those people who consume the luxury. 
Finally, one more limitation regarding this study is the lack of open-ended questions, which would have 
allowed respondents to express and explain their opinions in more detail. This would provide a more 
thorough understanding of underlying reasons of luxury consumption, though, many answers could be 
irrelevant regarding the chosen specific luxury value perceptions. The research method used could also be 
different by doing a qualitative study to get broader insights into the phenomenon. Luxury brand 
consumption is a large and growing phenomenon and needs to be researched in more detail.
