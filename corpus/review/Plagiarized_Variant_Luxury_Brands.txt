Purchase and Consumption Orientations Among Filipino Millennials Toward 
Luxury Brands: Brand Attitude as Mediated by Actual and Ideal Self-
Congruity 
Prof. Marilou Q. Tolentino, DBA 
Dean, College of Business 
Systems Plus College Foundation, Angeles City, Philippines 
Abstract 
The present investigation sought to examine the underlying motivations driving Filipino 
millennials' orientations toward the acquisition and utilization of luxury brands. Extensive 
scholarly work conducted across various nations, demographic categories, and diverse cultural 
frameworks has generated substantial knowledge and perspectives regarding consumer viewpoints 
on luxury brands and their purchase and use. The central focal point of this investigation is the 
orientation of Filipino millennials with respect to their actual and ideal self-congruity as it pertains 
to luxury brand attitude — specifically, an examination of the factors that drive or shape Filipino 
millennials in the acquisition and utilization of luxury brands. In addition, supplementary 
dimensions of materialistic and status values were scrutinized in relation to the degree to which 
they exert influence over consumer motivation. Research questions and investigative hypotheses 
were addressed and resolved through a quantitative research framework. A structured survey 
questionnaire was administered to university students, young graduates, and young professionals 
categorized as millennials across various organizations, constituting the primary data-gathering 
instrument. The investigation revealed that, notably, the motivation reflecting Filipino millennial 
consumers' orientation toward ideal self-congruency surpasses the influence of actual self-
congruity on their brand attitudes toward luxury brands. This influence is clearly discernible 
among respondents in terms of their orientation toward materialistic value and prestige value. The 
study's implications are expected to contribute a body of knowledge to both business and academic 
domains. The findings of the research will constitute a scientific basis for comprehending the brand 
orientation of Filipino millennials, who comprise approximately one-third of the country's 
aggregate population and represent nearly half of the labor force. The variables examined in this 
investigation were not subjected to in-depth study within the Philippine context. Furthermore, the 
results are anticipated to be of utility to luxury brand managers requiring direction in the selection 
of appropriate marketing mix elements and marketing strategies when targeting millennial 
consumer segments. 
Keywords: consumer attitude, luxury brand, Filipino millennials, actual & ideal self-congruity, 
purchase and consumption, motivational factors

Introduction 
As tracked by Bain, the luxury industry encompasses both luxury goods and experiences, 
comprising nine distinct segments led by luxury automobiles, luxury hospitality, and personal 
luxury goods, which together account for upwards of 80% of the total market. Overall, the luxury 

market expanded 5% in 2018 to an estimated €1.2 trillion globally, with favorable performance 
observed across most segments. Sales of luxury cars continued to dominate the market, growing 
5% at constant exchange rates to €495 billion. Luxury experiences remained highly attractive to 
consumers, as evidenced by sales growth in luxury hospitality (up 5% from the prior year), 
gourmet food and fine dining (up 6%), and luxury cruises (up 7%). Moreover, online purchases 
reached an 8% market share and overall discounted sales reached 37% of the total market 
(D'Arpizio et al., 2016). Purchases of luxury brands from among younger generational cohorts — 
generation-y or millennials, i.e., individuals born in the 1980s and 1990s — have increased 
considerably, as this group assigns value to luxury products at an earlier age than conventional 
luxury consumers did (The Economist, 2014). 
Their purchasing behavior is markedly distinct, characterized by greater spontaneity, 
experimentation, and a tendency to explore emerging brands — constituting a sharp contrast to 
traditional luxury consumers, who display more predictable expenditure patterns and brand loyalty 
(Jay, 2012). It is projected that by 2026, millennials will constitute the primary consumers of 
luxury goods; accordingly, managers must adapt their marketing and brand management strategies 
to effectively capture this expansive and rapidly expanding consumer segment in order to 
outperform competitors (The Economist, 2014). 
Brand is a central consideration in consumer decision-making. Nevertheless, a limited body of 
research currently exists regarding the determinants of luxury consumption and the management 
of luxury brands. The relationship between consumer perception of luxury and what motivates 
customers to purchase luxury brands, in particular, remains poorly understood and is largely 
unexplored (Hennigs et al., 2013). This situation is characteristic of the Philippine context. Luxury 
brands are capable of satisfying consumers' psychological and functional needs through a 
combination of four value dimensions: financial, operational, individual, and social (Wiedmann et 
al., 2007). Based on these four distinct luxury value dimensions, customers may be segmented by 
distinguishing personally oriented from socially oriented consumers — that is, whether luxury 
brand acquisition is driven by personal enjoyment or by a desire to demonstrate success (Kapferer 
& Bastien, 2009). Therefore, luxury brands must deliver value that aligns with customers' 
subjective expectations and individual perceptions (Hennigs et al., 2013). 
Millennials are currently the focus of numerous scholarly investigations, representing a perplexing 
phenomenon to preceding human generations. This generation is socially interconnected and 
deeply engaged with social media and information and communication technology applications 
and infrastructure. 
Approximately one-third of Filipinos are millennials, with an age range spanning 15 to 35 years. 
This generational cohort shares common values and identities expressed across multiple layers. 
Accordingly, varying versions of the millennial identity emerge depending on how individuals 
wish to present themselves to the world. Within this framework, brands are encouraged to engage 
more deeply with millennials and to convert them into affiliates. Consistent with this orientation, 
millennials tend to adhere to integrity grounded in shared experience rather than positional 
authority (http://pana.com.ph/decoding-millennials/). 
Literature Review 
Luxury is a widely employed term for which no universally accepted definition exists. What 
constitutes luxury for certain individuals may not do so for others (Kapferer & Bastien, 2014). 

Nevertheless, specific attributes are commonly employed to define luxury, encompassing: 
exceptional quality, elevated price, scarcity and distinctiveness, aesthetics and polysensuality, 
ancestral heritage and personal history, and superfluousness (Dubois et al., 2001). 
Luxury is not synonymous with fashion or premium, despite the conceptual linkages and borrowed 
associations among these domains (Kapferer & Bastien, 2014: 35). However, possession of only 
a single attribute from the aforementioned list — such as elevated price — is insufficient by itself 
to constitute luxury. Consequently, upper-range branded premium products are frequently 
conflated with luxury. Whereas pricing in premium products is determined by functionality, 
performance, and quality, in luxury goods it is associated with scarcity, brand identity, and 
narrative (Turunen, 2015). On the other hand, numerous luxury brands adopt behavioral 
characteristics of fashion brands, while fashion itself aspires to membership in the luxury domain 
in order to elevate its standing (Kapferer & Bastien, 2014). 
Luxury products are associated with the notion of a dream, while essential products correspond to 
need; intermediate to these are branded products, which correspond to desire that is transient and 
substitutable. Dreams transcend requirements or wants, and it is to dreams that luxury products 
respond — a quality that distinguishes luxury from non-luxury offerings (Kapferer & Bastien, 
2014). The acquisition of a luxury product is tantamount to purchasing into a dream, and such 
acquisitions are frequently impulsive, emotionally driven, or extravagant (Dubois & Paternault, 
1995). Consequently, luxury constitutes an industry characterized by elevated profitability, with 
high margins in luxury product pricing, and strong brand equity and brand renown generating 
supplementary value for expensive, premium-quality products (Kapferer, 1997). 
Luxury brands may be categorized according to their degree of luxury — i.e., the level of 
accessibility — classified as accessible, intermediate, and inaccessible luxury brands (De Barnier 
et al., 2011). Other proposed categorizations of luxury degree, derived from prior literature 
(Vigneron & Johnson, 1999; Kapferer, 2008; Corbellini & Saviolo, 2009), are summarized in 
Figure 2 (Turunen, 2015: 122). Brand extensions of luxury brands into new product categories 
have introduced further ambiguity in defining luxury. When luxury brands launch lower-end 
extensions, consumers are afforded access to the luxury brand at this accessible product tier — for 
instance, through fragrances (Patrick & Prokopec, 2015). To comprehend consumer motivations 
for purchasing luxury, it is imperative to understand the underlying value that the product holds 
for the consumer at the point of use (Ballantyne & Varey, 2006). Consumers engage with products 
in varied ways, and the interpersonal and personal dimensions of consumption differ across luxury 
consumer types (Holt, 1995). Consumers aspire to construct a meaningful existence in relation to 
personal identity and social relationships, such that the products consumed enhance their 
individual quality of life through the gratification derived from owning and using products and 
experiences (Pollay, 1983; Solomon et al., 2016). Consumer perception of luxury brands 
accordingly shapes the relevance of consumption. In order to comprehend how consumers 
individualize their perceptions of luxury and how luxury brands alter their daily lives, consumers' 
consumption values warrant examination (Seo & Buchanan-Oliver, 2014). 
According to Kapferer and Bastien (2014), luxury consumers may be divided into heavy users and 
occasional users. Until approximately the year 2000, growth in the luxury market was attributable 
largely to a substantial volume of middle-class consumers making occasional luxury purchases. 
More recently, the primary volume of luxury acquisitions has originated from consumers who 
purchase frequently. Western middle-class segments are increasingly apprehensive about their 

futures and, consequently, perceive themselves as less affluent, migrating toward lower 
consumption categories, while wealthy purchasers have maintained their optimism, constituting 
the majority of the luxury market. This market polarization has enabled luxury brands to grow 
sales of entry-level masstige products among middle-class consumers who have retained their 
aspirations but are now more deliberate in their spending. By contrast, young Chinese middle-class 
consumers diverge from their Western counterparts in that, despite possessing considerably less 
wealth, their incomes are perceptibly increasing, generating a sense of greater optimism and 
prosperity and rendering the Chinese luxury market highly dynamic. A comparable dynamic may 
explain the growing number of generation-y consumers purchasing luxury — particularly 
following the 2008 economic recession — as they increasingly seek self-indulgence through 
luxury consumption (Jay, 2012). 
In research conducted by Kapferer (1998) with an international sample of affluent young 
consumers, four types of luxury clients were identified on the basis of what luxury signifies to 
them and what product characteristics they prioritize. The first group prizes the beauty, excellence, 
and uniqueness of luxury products. The second segment emphasizes creativity and sensuality as 
the primary luxury values. The third group bases luxury value on timelessness and international 
reputation, thus perceiving luxury as a reliable choice. The fourth group focuses on rarity and the 
most inaccessible products, comprising the buyers of the most costly items. It was further observed 
that the fourth group consisted predominantly of males, while the second segment was 
predominantly female. This distinction may offer some indication of differences in luxury 
consumption value preferences between males and females, suggesting that males are more 
inclined to consume conspicuous markers of success, while females place greater emphasis on 
creativity, sensuality, and aesthetics. In this regard, younger males tend to consume status 
signifiers to communicate their progress. The third group of consumers is driven by active and 
visible cues and widely recognized brand names, while the second group is more concerned with 
expressing individuality. The first group regards themselves as connoisseurs, aesthetes, and 
individuals capable of appreciating exceptional craftsmanship. 
An alternative segmentation of luxury consumers was proposed by Han et al. (2010). This study 
advances the analysis of consumers' motivational factors, distinguishing consumers on the basis 
of the role that brand prominence plays in status signaling — specifically, the conspicuousness of 
the logo displayed on a product. The greater a consumer's need for status, the more prominently 
they employ these so-called loud signals of brand affiliation in their consumption behavior. The 
inverse holds for individuals with a diminished need for status. Consumers with high wealth and 
a lesser need for status instead signal to one another through quiet, understated brand cues, with 
logos not prominently displayed, and their premium-priced luxury products are recognized by 
others within the same consumer segment. Conversely, affluent consumers with a pronounced 
need for status employ conspicuous luxury products to signal to the less affluent that they do not 
belong to the same category. The less affluent with high status needs, unable to afford genuine 
items, consume highly conspicuous — largely counterfeit — luxury products in an attempt to 
emulate the more affluent and to be perceived as occupying a higher social stratum. Consumers 
can be influenced by their reference group, by those whom they aspire to resemble, and by those 
with whom they do not wish to be associated (Han et al., 2010). 
According to Veblen (1965), the accumulation of wealth does not in itself confer status; rather, it 
is the visible evidence of wealth that confers status. This observation illuminates the conspicuous 
consumption of luxury as a symbolic representation of one's success. Individuals frequently 

evaluate others through the objects those individuals consume, and tend to regard their own 
possessions as extensions of themselves. Furthermore, individuals may employ personal 
belongings — particularly luxury products — to assist in self-definition and to reflect their own 
identity. An individual may consume products to convey a distinct sense of selfhood or to signal 
belonging to a particular social group. Through material possessions, individuals pursue happiness 
and preserve memories of experiences, accomplishments, and significant relationships. Possessed 
objects communicate a sense of historical continuity, reflecting who one is, where one originated, 
and where one is headed (Belk, 1988). 
Not all brands are equally luxurious, and, as previously noted, luxury does not possess a singular, 
unambiguous definition. Furthermore, the degree of luxury attributed to a given brand may be 
perceived differently across product categories and situational contexts. For instance, Cartier may 
be accorded a higher perceived luxury in the jewelry category and a comparatively lower perceived 
luxury in the apparel or fragrance category. In a similar vein, Rolls-Royce is recognized as a luxury 
brand in the automotive category but is not regarded as such in the context of aircraft engines 
(Vigneron & Johnson, 2004; Kapferer & Bastien, 2014). 
To facilitate the differentiation of luxury brands, Vigneron and Johnson (2004) developed a 
valuation model for assessing the degree of luxury embodied in a given brand. This Brand Luxury 
Index (BLI) is composed of five perceived dimensions of a luxury brand (Vigneron & Johnson, 
2004: 488). The framework incorporates three non-personal perceptions — perceived 
conspicuousness, perceived uniqueness, and perceived quality — as well as two personally 
oriented perceptions: perceived hedonism and the perceived extended self. According to Vigneron 
and Johnson (2004), these five dimensions constitute the essential luxury attributes that must be 
established and sustained for a luxury brand to endure. A brand is required to manage both personal 
and interpersonal perceptions to ensure the long-term financial success of a luxury brand (Kapferer 
& Bastien, 2014). 
Moreover, these five core perceptual dimensions are interrelated and underlie consumer decision-
making processes when evaluating luxury brands. While consumers assign value to all five 
dimensions, certain aspects carry differential significance for different consumer profiles. The BLI 
scale may also be employed to compare distinct luxury brands and, accordingly, to identify 
competitive advantages within the targeted market (Vigneron & Johnson, 2004). 
Berthon et al. (2009) assert that consumers' perceptions of luxury brands encompass three value 
dimensions: functional, experiential, and symbolic. Vickers and Renald (2003) further contend 
that a fundamental distinction exists between these three dimensions for luxury versus non-luxury 
brands, given that consumers hold higher expectations of luxury brands. Because these three 
luxury value dimensions are contextual in nature, they may evolve over time in accordance with 
individual and socio-cultural orientations, and are thus co-constructed between a luxury brand and 
its consumers (Tynan et al., 2010). 
The functional value dimension refers to the material representation of an object along with its 
core benefit and essential utilities — encompassing quality, functionality, reliability, durability, 
and craftsmanship. Within this dimension, quality is of particular significance as an indicator of 
product performance and functional capability. 
The experiential value dimension embodies individual subjective value, referencing personal taste 
and the hedonic value an individual derives from a brand. This dimension may be evoked by brand 

experience-related stimuli that are embedded in brand identity — including design, packaging, 
communication, and environmental elements — which collectively influence consumers' 
sensations, emotions, cognitions, perceptions, and behavioral responses (Berthon et al., 2009). 
Hence, luxury products deliver need- or want-satisfying consumption experiences (Holbrook, 
1999). The perception of luxury as rare, precious, and distinctive may guide individual thoughts 
and feelings toward a luxury brand through uniqueness-seeking motivations, in addition to hedonic 
motivations (Hung et al., 2011). Experiential value therefore addresses the question of what the 
brand signifies to the individual consumer. 
The symbolic value dimension pertains to what the brand communicates to others and to the 
brand's symbolic function within the social collective (Berthon et al., 2009). Symbolic value 
connotes conspicuousness, costliness, and affluence (Hung et al., 2011). It is of particular 
importance for premium-priced products and services, given that symbolic attributes embody the 
aspirational dream and brand narrative. An aspect of symbolic value concerns what the luxury 
brand signals to others about the consumer — i.e., other-directed signaling, such as the 
communication of wealth, prestige, achievement, or personal style (Berthon et al., 2009). The 
symbolic dimension also encompasses a self-directed value component: the significance of the 
signal to the sender, as an extension of one's self and conspicuousness (Belk, 1988). However, a 
brand's symbolic value is operative only within communities in which it is recognized; its social 
utility diminishes when one moves outside such communities. 
Symbolic value constitutes a particularly significant consideration for the management and 
marketing of global luxury brands, as social influence shapes purchase intention — particularly 
among socially oriented consumers (Tsai, 2005). Luxury brands are required to manage all three 
of these dimensions effectively and must avoid excessive focus on any single dimension at the 
expense of the others. For instance, the quality of a luxury brand should not be compromised in 
favor of an overemphasis on symbolic attributes. Luxury brands ought to represent a convergence 
of material (objective, functional), individual (subjective, experiential), and social (collective, 
symbolic) spheres (Berthon et al., 2009). 
Wiedmann et al. (2007) have developed a conceptual framework demonstrating that financial, 
functional, individual, and social dimensions collectively influence consumers' luxury value 
perception and consumption behavior. These four primary luxury value dimensions are strongly 
correlated but not identical, and each contributes to consumers' overall luxury value perception. 
The sub-dimensions of these four primary dimensions of luxury value perception are as follows: 
price value (financial value); usability value, quality value, and uniqueness value (functional 
values); self-identity value, hedonic value, and materialistic value (individual values); and 
conspicuousness value and prestige value (social values). Even when the overall luxury level of a 
brand is perceived similarly, the core luxury value dimensions are interpreted differently by 
distinct consumer segments. The luxury value model of Wiedmann et al. (2007) facilitates an 
understanding of the determinants and drivers of luxury perception, and by extension, of consumer 
behavior, thereby contributing to the creation, marketing, and monitoring of luxury brands. 
In general, the Wiedmann et al. (2007) model is considered the most complex and comprehensive 
framework available. However, drawing upon various preceding studies on the topic, additional 
values may warrant incorporation. For instance, Ciornea et al. (2011) have supplemented the 
financial value dimension with investment value and the functional value dimension with heritage 
value. Multiple sub-dimensions could be added to the proposed model, such as superfluousness — 

which is closely related to the degree of materialism (Ciornea et al., 2011). Furthermore, the 
individual value and social value dimensions, along with consumers' overall perceived luxury 
value and luxury brand purchase intention, and the value dimensions that must be accounted for, 
will be examined in greater detail in subsequent sections and in the research proper. 
Filipino millennials exhibit a pronounced dependence on technological devices, which exerts a 
notable financial strain on their resources; to a certain extent, they employ information and 
communication technology to economize and access discounts on airline tickets, food packages, 
travel promotions, and related offerings. 
Research conducted by Visa revealed that 80% of Filipino millennials favor cash-based 
transactions over credit card usage. The results further indicated that Filipino millennials harbor 
reservations regarding the credit card system, attributable to concerns over confidentiality and data 
security. Accordingly, the promotion of financial literacy concerning credit card usage is 
considered necessary (https://www.ecomparemo.com/info/who-are-the-filipino-millennials-and-
what-are-their-spending-habits/). 
According to Madelaine B. Miraflor, the purchasing power of millennials is rapidly escalating, as 
manifested in their acquisition of personal care products, home care items, and food commodities 
from retail establishments. This assertion was corroborated by Kantar Worldpanel, which states 
that the capacity of Filipino millennials to purchase personal and home cleanliness, convenience, 
and leisure items is expanding (https://business.mb.com.ph/2016/11/19/millennials-purchasing-
power-growing-study/). 
Background of the Study 
The primary impetus for the present investigation was the limited body of scholarship addressing 
the orientation of Filipino millennials toward the purchase and consumption of luxury brands, with 
the objective of generating empirical findings for subsequent studies that will contribute a body of 
knowledge to both business and academic domains. 
The effect of the variables examined within the Philippine context remains minimal; the subject 
area had not been thoroughly investigated at the time the study was conducted. 
Significance of the Study 
The findings of the research will serve as a scientific foundation for comprehending the brand 
orientation of Filipino millennials, who constitute approximately one-third of the nation's 
aggregate population and represent nearly half of the labor force. 
Objectives of the Study 
The purpose of this investigation is to examine the influence exerted by actual and ideal self-
congruity on millennial consumers' brand orientations toward luxury brands. Furthermore, the 
study examines the effects of materialistic value and status value dimensions in terms of the extent 
to which they shape millennials' brand orientations regarding luxury brands. 
This study aims to contribute to the existing body of knowledge pertaining to young consumers' 
brand orientations and subsequent purchasing behavior with respect to luxury brands, in order to 
maximize the commercial potential of this specific target group and to address the precise needs 

required to grow sales within this target segment of generation-y consumers among Filipino 
millennials. The investigation was designed to specifically: 
• 
Determine the motivations among Filipino millennials in their purchase and consumption 
of luxury brands in terms of actual self-congruity and ideal self-congruity. 
• 
Identify the variables across educational groupings, income categories, gender 
classifications, and age brackets that significantly influence the purchase and 
consumption of luxury brands among Filipino millennials. 
• 
Determine the effect of materialistic value, status value, and social desirability scale 
outcomes on the purchase and utilization of luxury brands among Filipino millennials. 
Statement of the Problem 
The present investigation centers on the effects of actual and ideal self-congruity and the manner 
in which a consumer's self-image and luxury brand-image congruity shape brand orientation 
regarding luxury brands. The ideal self-image is defined as the manner in which an individual 
wishes to be or would prefer to perceive themselves, along with the self-concept the individual 
desires others to associate with them — encompassing attitudes, perceptions, and affect (Ross, 
1971; Schenk & Holman, 1980). 
Luxury brand consumption exerts a positive influence on the approximation of ideal self-congruity 
by transmitting the image and symbolic significance of luxury brands, thereby enhancing an 
individual's aspirational self-image. 
Self-concept is of value to the individual; accordingly, individual behavior is directed toward the 
enhancement of one's own self-concept through the consumption of luxury goods and brands as 
symbolic vehicles for communicating symbolic meaning to the individual themselves and to others 
(Grubb & Grathwohl, 1967). 
Brand orientation is an essential element in the valuation of brand equity. Brand orientation 
constitutes an individual's comprehensive evaluation of a brand and is primarily contingent upon 
consumer perceptions of that brand (Aaker, 1997). Additionally, self-congruity may be employed 
to explain and predict brand orientation and purchase intention (Sirgy et al., 1997; Helgeson & 
Supphellen, 2004). Self-identity value, materialistic value, and status value are closely interrelated 
(Richins & Dawson, 1992; Dittmar, 1994; Webster & Beatty, 1997). 
The research questions guiding this investigation are as follows: 
• 
To what extent are Filipino millennials motivated in their purchase and consumption of 
luxury brands in terms of actual self-congruity and ideal self-congruity? 
• 
What is the effect of materialistic value and status value on the purchase and 
consumption of luxury brands among Filipino millennials? 
• 
Which variables across educational groupings, income categories, gender classifications, 
and age brackets significantly influence the purchase and consumption of luxury brands 
among Filipino millennials? 
• 
Which items demonstrate significant correlations with the purchase and consumption of 
luxury brands among Filipino millennials? 

Methodology 
The purpose of this investigation is to examine the influence of actual and ideal self-congruity in 
shaping orientations toward luxury brands. The study employs descriptive research design, which 
aims to investigate the relationships among variables in the Philippine context. A survey 
instrument was utilized for data collection. Data were obtained through a questionnaire and 
subsequently standardized to facilitate comparison. The study drew upon both primary and 
secondary data sources. Primary data were collected to address the research questions and fulfill 
the research objectives. The questionnaire comprised closed-ended items with pre-established 
response options, with no open-ended items included. As a result, the questionnaire is highly 
structured, with respondents' own words held to a minimum. 
To operationalize the distinction between luxury and non-luxury brands, a selection of well-known 
brands in the Philippines was presented to respondents at the outset of the questionnaire, enabling 
them to identify a brand as a reference point for subsequent items. The study employed a Likert 
Scale ranging from 1 to 5, spanning from strongly disagree to strongly agree. A shortened version 
of the Crowne-Marlowe social desirability scale (Crowne & Marlowe, 1960) was additionally 
administered to assess the extent to which respondents are concerned with social approval, given 
that social desirability bias is recognized as one of the most prevalent biases affecting survey 
research outcomes (King & Bruner, 2000). Lower scores on this scale may indicate that the 
respondent is less preoccupied with social approval and is more inclined to respond to survey items 
truthfully and to represent themselves accurately (Crowne & Marlowe, 1960). 
The study's respondents comprised Filipino millennials drawn from a higher education institution, 
as well as young entrepreneurs, employees, employers, and graduates who consume luxury brands. 
This population was selected because it corresponds to the specific group of interest. The sample 
consisted of 134 respondents, a size considered sufficient to satisfy the requirements of efficiency, 
representativeness, reliability, and flexibility — the criteria of an optimal sample, according to 
Kothari (2004). 
A non-probability convenience sampling approach was adopted, premised on the ease of obtaining 
responses. The study combines self-selection sampling with convenience sampling. The 
questionnaire was disseminated through the assistance of on-the-job training students and alumni 
serving as enumerators. 
Data Analysis 
Analysis of the data obtained from the survey questionnaire was conducted using SPSS, wherein 
the collected information was examined through the application of statistical tools. Descriptive 
statistics were employed in particular to describe and numerically compare variables.

luxury brands, with reference to non-luxury brands as well. The study collected data from 134 
respondents. This section examines the demographic characteristics of the survey sample, 
alongside the Marlowe-Crowne social desirability scale results.

Conclusion 
The following conclusions were derived from the investigation: 
• 
Filipino millennials' orientations in the purchase and consumption of luxury brands are 
more strongly driven by ideal self-congruity than by actual self-congruity. Marketers 
should take into account that Filipino millennials are motivated in the purchase and 

consumption of luxury brands on the basis of ideal self-congruity: they would like to see 
themselves reflected in the luxury brand; they would like individuals whom they admire 
to consume the luxury brand; and they would like to be associated with those who 
consume luxury. 
• 
Filipino millennials' orientations in the purchase and consumption of luxury brands are 
more strongly driven by ideal self-congruity than by actual self-congruity. Marketers 
should take into account that Filipino millennials are motivated in the purchase and 
consumption of luxury brands on the basis of ideal self-congruity: they would like to see 
themselves reflected in the luxury brand; they would like individuals whom they admire 
to consume the luxury brand; and they would like to be associated with those who 
consume luxury. 
• 
Filipino millennials' orientations in the purchase and consumption of luxury brands are 
more strongly driven by ideal self-congruity than by actual self-congruity. Marketers 
should take into account that Filipino millennials are motivated in the purchase and 
consumption of luxury brands on the basis of ideal self-congruity: they would like to see 
themselves reflected in the luxury brand; they would like individuals whom they admire 
to consume the luxury brand; and they would like to be associated with those who 
consume luxury. 
Recommendation 
Marketers should take into account that Filipino millennials are motivated in the purchase and 
consumption of luxury brands on the basis of ideal self-congruity. Accordingly: 
• 
The luxury brand should be relatable, consistent, and in alignment or agreement with the 
consumer's ideal self. 
• 
Marketers should consider endorsers, models, and advertising themes or slogans that 
accord with the ideal self of Filipino millennials, emphasizing functionality, 
image/reputation, and social desirability in promotional strategies, packaging, and 
distribution channels that are perceived as high-end and prestigious. 
The orientations of Filipino millennials in the purchase and consumption of luxury brands are more 
strongly driven by ideal self-congruity than by actual self-congruity. 
• 
Marketers should take into account that Filipino millennials are motivated in the purchase 
and consumption of luxury brands on the basis of their ideal self-congruity: they would 
like to see themselves reflected in the luxury brand; they would like individuals whom 
they admire to consume the luxury brand; and they would like to be associated with those 
who consume luxury. 
One additional limitation of this investigation pertains to the absence of open-ended questions, 
which would have enabled respondents to articulate and elaborate upon their perspectives in 
greater depth. Such items would have contributed to a more thorough comprehension of the 
underlying determinants of luxury consumption; however, many responses might have proven 
tangential to the specific luxury value perceptions under examination, given that several other 
relevant perceptions could also have been investigated. It was therefore determined not to include 
open-ended questions so as to avoid rendering the survey excessively lengthy. 

An alternative research method could have been employed through the conduct of a qualitative 
study to obtain broader insights into the phenomenon, allowing respondents to express their 
perspectives more freely. This could have been integrated with a survey questionnaire designed in 
response to the findings of the qualitative study, focusing on the most pertinent aspects of the 
phenomenon and potentially generating new avenues for future investigation. 
Luxury brand consumption is a substantial and expanding phenomenon that warrants more detailed 
investigation, thus presenting numerous opportunities for the advancement of research in this 
domain.
