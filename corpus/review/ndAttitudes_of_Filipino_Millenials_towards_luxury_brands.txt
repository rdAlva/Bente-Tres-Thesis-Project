Attitudes of Filipino Millennials Towards Purchase and Consumption of
Luxury Brands: Actual and Ideal Self-Congruity on Brand Attitude
Prof. Marilou Q. Tolentino, DBA
Dean, College of Business
Systems Plus College Foundation, Angeles City, Philippines
Abstract
This study set out to uncover what drives the attitudes of Filipino millennials when it comes to buying and
using luxury brands. A wide body of research from other countries, spanning different demographics and
cultural settings, has already given useful insight into how buyers elsewhere view luxury purchasing and
use. Here, though, the central focus is on how Filipino millennials' own sense of actual versus ideal
self-congruity shapes their attitude toward luxury — in other words, pinpointing exactly what pushes or
pulls Filipino millennials toward buying and using these brands.
The study also brought in materialistic value and status value as additional angles, examining how much
weight these carry in shaping consumer motivation. Both the research questions and the hypotheses were
tackled and answered using a quantitative approach. A structured survey questionnaire was handed out to
university students, recent graduates, and young professionals across a range of organizations, all falling
under the millennial bracket. The findings revealed that ideal self-congruity carries noticeably more weight
than actual self-congruity in shaping how Filipino millennial consumers feel about luxury brands. This
influence came through clearly in how respondents valued materialism and prestige.
What this study contributes should be useful on both the business and academic side. Since Filipino
millennials make up roughly a third of the country's population and about half its labor force, the results
offer a solid, evidence-based starting point for understanding their brand attitudes — a topic that hadn't
been examined in much depth within the Philippine context before. The findings should also be of practical
use to luxury brand managers looking for direction on the right marketing mix and strategy when targeting
this generation of consumers.

and consumption, motivational factors

Introduction
Bain's tracking of the luxury industry covers both luxury goods and luxury experiences, spanning nine
segments in total. Of these, luxury cars, luxury hospitality, and personal luxury goods together make up
more than 80% of the overall market. In 2018 the global luxury market grew by 5%, reaching an estimated
€1.2 trillion, with most segments posting gains. Luxury car sales remained the market's biggest driver,
climbing 5% at constant exchange rates to €495 billion. Experience-based luxury also held strong appeal,
with hospitality sales up 5%, gourmet food and fine dining up 6%, and luxury cruises up 7%. Online sales
climbed to an 8% market share, while discounted sales made up as much as 37% of total sales (D'Arpizio
et al., 2016). Meanwhile, younger buyers — Generation Y, or millennials, born in the 1980s and 1990s —
have been developing an appetite for luxury goods earlier in life than traditional luxury shoppers ever did,
and their share of luxury purchases has grown substantially (The Economist, 2014).
These younger buyers also behave very differently at the point of purchase: more impulsive, more willing
to experiment, and more inclined to try new brands, in contrast to older luxury consumers who tend to
spend consistently and stay loyal to established brands (Jay, 2012). Projections suggest millennials will
become the dominant luxury buyers by 2026, meaning brand managers will need to rework their
marketing and brand strategies to capture this large, fast-growing segment and stay ahead of competitors
(The Economist, 2014).
Brand plays a central role in how consumers make decisions, yet research into what actually drives luxury
consumption and how luxury brands should be managed remains fairly thin. In particular, the link between
how consumers perceive luxury and what actually pushes them to buy luxury brands is still not well
understood or explored (Hennigs et al., 2013) — and the Philippines is a clear example of this gap. Luxury
brands meet both psychological and functional needs through a blend of four value dimensions: financial,
operational, individual, and social (Wiedmann et al., 2007). These four dimensions allow customers to be
grouped as either personally-oriented or socially-oriented — essentially, whether someone buys luxury for
personal enjoyment or to show off success (Kapferer & Bastien, 2009). This means luxury brands have to
deliver value that matches each customer's own expectations and perceptions (Hennigs et al., 2013).
Millennials themselves have become a popular research subject, largely because they puzzle older
generations. They are deeply connected socially, and heavily reliant on social media and digital tools.
Roughly a third of Filipinos fall into the millennial bracket, aged 15 to 35. While millennials broadly share
certain values and identities, these come in different layers, meaning there isn't just one type of millennial
— it depends on how they want to present themselves to the world. Given this, brands need to engage
millennials more directly to build marketability and affiliation with them. Millennials, more than previous

Literature Review
The word "luxury" gets used constantly, yet no single, fixed definition of it exists — what counts as luxury
for one person may not for another (Kapferer & Bastien, 2014). Still, certain traits are commonly used to
define it: excellent quality, high price, scarcity and uniqueness, aesthetics and polysensuality, ancestral
heritage and personal history, and superfluousness (Dubois et al., 2001).
Luxury isn't the same thing as fashion or as "premium," even though the concepts overlap and borrow
from one another (Kapferer & Bastien, 2014). Simply having one of the traits above — high price, for
instance — isn't enough on its own to qualify something as luxury, which is why upper-range premium
products are often mistaken for luxury goods. For premium products, price tracks functionality,
performance, and quality; for true luxury, price tracks scarcity, brand, and storytelling instead (Turunen,
2015). At the same time, many luxury brands borrow behaviors from fashion brands, just as fashion
brands try to associate themselves with luxury to boost their own status (Kapferer & Bastien, 2014).
Luxury goods are also tied to the idea of a dream, whereas essential goods answer a need, and branded
goods in between answer a passing desire and can usually be substituted. Dreams go beyond need or
want, and it's this dream-like quality that luxury goods respond to and that sets them apart from non-luxury
goods (Kapferer & Bastien, 2014). Buying a luxury item is really buying into that dream, and such
purchases tend to be impulsive, emotional, or extravagant (Dubois & Paternault, 1995). This is part of why
the luxury industry runs on unusually high profitability and margins — strong brand equity and reputation
add extra value to already expensive, high-quality products (Kapferer, 1997).
Luxury brands can also be sorted by how accessible they are — available, intermediate, or inaccessible
(De Barnier et al., 2011). Other classification schemes drawn from earlier literature (Vigneron & Johnson,
1999; Kapferer, 2008; Corbellini & Saviolo, 2009) are summarized elsewhere (Turunen, 2015). One
reason luxury has become harder to define cleanly is that brands keep extending into new categories;
when a luxury brand launches a cheaper line-extension — a perfume, say — it gives consumers a taste of
the brand at a more accessible price point (Patrick & Prokopec, 2015). Understanding what motivates
luxury purchases really comes down to understanding what value the product holds for the person using it
(Ballantyne & Varey, 2006). People consume differently, and the personal and social behaviors behind
luxury consumption vary from one consumer to the next (Holt, 1995). Consumers look to build a
meaningful life around their identity and relationships, so that what they consume adds to their quality of
life through the enjoyment that comes from owning and using products and experiences (Pollay, 1983;
Solomon et al., 2016). How a consumer perceives a luxury brand therefore shapes how relevant that
consumption feels to them; understanding a person's broader consumption values helps explain how they
individualize their views on luxury and how luxury brands end up shaping their daily lives (Seo &
Buchanan-Oliver, 2014).
Kapferer and Bastien (2014) split luxury consumers into heavy users and occasional users. Up until
around 2000, the luxury market grew mainly because large numbers of middle-class shoppers bought
luxury items now and then. Today, though, the bulk of luxury purchases comes from frequent buyers.
Western middle classes, worried about their financial futures, increasingly feel poorer and are drifting
toward less affluent spending categories, while wealthier buyers have stayed optimistic and now account
for most of the luxury market. This split has let luxury brands grow their entry-level, "masstige" product
lines aimed at middle-class shoppers who still aspire to luxury but spend more cautiously. Young Chinese
middle-class consumers show the opposite pattern — despite having less money overall, their income
keeps rising, leaving them feeling optimistic and prosperous, which has made China's luxury market

especially dynamic. A similar dynamic may help explain the rising number of Gen-Y luxury buyers,
particularly since the 2008 recession, as this generation increasingly turns to luxury consumption as a
form of self-indulgence (Jay, 2012).
Kapferer's 1998 research on an international sample of affluent young consumers identified four distinct
types of luxury buyers, based on what luxury means to them and which qualities they value most. The first
group values the beauty, excellence, and uniqueness of the product itself. The second cares most about
creativity and sensuality. The third bases luxury value on timelessness and international reputation —
luxury as a "safe" choice. The fourth group focuses on rarity and the most exclusive, hard-to-get products,
and tends to buy the priciest items available. Interestingly, this fourth group skewed mostly male, while the
second skewed mostly female — suggesting that men lean toward visible signs of success, while women
place more weight on creativity, sensuality, and beauty. Young men, in this sense, consume luxury mainly
to display their progress. The third group, meanwhile, responds to active, visible signals and well-known
brand names, while the second group is more focused on individuality, and the first sees itself as
connoisseurs capable of appreciating truly exceptional products.
Han et al. (2010) offer another way of segmenting luxury consumers, this time based on how much a
consumer relies on brand prominence — that is, how visible the logo is — to signal status. The stronger
someone's need for status, the more they lean on "loud" signals like conspicuous logos. Those with less
need for status tend to do the opposite. Wealthy consumers with a lower need for status prefer quiet
signals — premium products without an obvious logo, recognizable mainly to others within the same social
circle. Wealthy consumers who do need status, on the other hand, use loud luxury products specifically to
distinguish themselves from those less well-off, while less affluent consumers with a high need for status
often turn to counterfeit luxury goods to mimic higher social standing since they can't afford the real thing.
This dynamic shows how consumers are shaped by the groups they aspire to belong to, as well as the
ones they want to distance themselves from (Han et al., 2010).
Veblen (1965) argued that simply accumulating wealth doesn't automatically confer status — it's visible
evidence of that wealth that does. This helps explain conspicuous luxury consumption as a symbol of
success. People often define others, and even themselves, through the objects they own, treating
possessions as extensions of their own identity. A person might use luxury goods specifically to express
who they are, or to signal belonging to a certain group. Material possessions offer people happiness and
serve as reminders of experiences, achievements, and relationships — they carry a sense of one's past,
showing who someone is, where they've come from, and where they're headed (Belk, 1988).
Not every brand carries the same degree of luxury, and, as already noted, there's no single fixed definition
of luxury to begin with. Even the same brand's perceived luxury level can shift depending on the product
category or situation — Cartier, for example, may feel more luxurious in jewelry than in apparel or
fragrance, and Rolls-Royce is seen as luxury in the automotive world but not in aircraft engines (Vigneron
& Johnson, 2004; Kapferer & Bastien, 2014).
To help sort luxury brands more systematically, Vigneron and Johnson (2004) built a valuation model —
the Brand Luxury Index (BLI) — made up of five perceived dimensions. Three of these are non-personal:
perceived conspicuousness, perceived uniqueness, and perceived quality. The other two are personal:
perceived hedonism and perceived extended self. According to the authors, all five dimensions need to be
tracked and managed for a luxury brand to endure, since a brand has to balance both personal and
interpersonal perceptions to succeed financially over the long run (Kapferer & Bastien, 2014).

These five factors are interconnected and sit underneath how consumers decide whether to buy into a
luxury brand. All five matter to consumers generally, though different customers weigh them differently.
The BLI scale can also be used to compare brands against each other and spot competitive advantages
within a given market (Vigneron & Johnson, 2004).
Berthon et al. (2009) break consumer perceptions of luxury down into three value dimensions instead:
functional, experiential, and symbolic. Vickers and Renand (2003) similarly argue that luxury brands differ
fundamentally from non-luxury brands along these lines, since consumers simply expect more from
luxury. Because these three dimensions are shaped by context, they can shift over time depending on the
individual and the surrounding culture, effectively being co-created between the brand and its consumers
(Tynan et al., 2010).
The functional dimension covers the material, core benefits of a product — quality, functionality, reliability,
durability, craftsmanship — with quality standing out as especially important since it signals how well a
product performs.
The experiential dimension reflects individual, subjective value — a person's own taste and where they
find hedonic enjoyment in a brand. This can be triggered by design, packaging, communication, and
environment, all of which shape a consumer's sensations, feelings, perceptions, and behavior (Berthon et
al., 2009). Luxury products, in this sense, deliver consumption experiences that satisfy needs or wants
(Holbrook, 1999). Because luxury is seen as rare, precious, and unique, it can also shape thoughts and
feelings through a consumer's desire for uniqueness, on top of pure hedonic motives (Hung et al., 2011).
In short, the experiential dimension answers what the brand means to the individual.
The symbolic dimension, meanwhile, is about what the brand signals to others — its meaning within the
broader social group (Berthon et al., 2009). This nominal value conveys conspicuousness, expense, and
wealth (Hung et al., 2011), and matters enormously for premium products since it carries the brand's
dream and story. It covers what the brand communicates about the person using it — wealth, prestige,
performance, style — to others (other-directed signaling), as well as what that signaling does for the
person doing the signaling, extending their sense of self (Belk, 1988). That said, a brand's symbolic value
only really works within the community that recognizes it; outside that community, its social meaning
fades.
This symbolic dimension matters a great deal for how global luxury brands manage and market
themselves, since social influence strongly shapes purchase intent, especially among socially-oriented
consumers (Tsai, 2005). Luxury brands need to balance all three dimensions rather than overemphasizing
one at the expense of the others — a brand's quality, for instance, shouldn't suffer just because its
symbolic appeal gets all the attention. Ideally, luxury brands combine the material (objective, functional),
individual (subjective, experiential), and social (collective, symbolic) spheres (Berthon et al., 2009).
Wiedmann et al. (2007) built a conceptual framework showing that financial, functional, individual, and
social dimensions all shape how consumers perceive and consume luxury value. These four dimensions
are closely related but distinct, and each feeds into a consumer's overall sense of luxury value. Within
them sit sub-dimensions: price value under financial value; usability, quality, and uniqueness under
functional value; self-identity, hedonic, and materialistic value under individual value; and
conspicuousness and prestige value under social value. Even when a brand's overall luxury level is
perceived similarly across different customer groups, these underlying dimensions can be weighed quite
differently. This model helps explain what drives luxury perception and, by extension, consumer behavior
— useful not just for understanding customers but also for creating, marketing, and monitoring luxury

brands.
Wiedmann et al.'s (2007) framework is generally regarded as the most thorough and complete of its kind,
though later research has proposed additions. Ciornea et al. (2011), for instance, added investment value
to the financial dimension and heritage value to the functional dimension, along with other possible
sub-dimensions like superfluousness, which ties closely to materialism. There's also room to look more
closely at how the individual and social value dimensions shape both overall perceived luxury value and
purchase intent — questions this study explores further in the sections that follow.

Background of the Study
Given how little research exists on the attitudes of Filipino millennials toward buying and using luxury
brands, this study was built to establish a scientific foundation that future research can build on,
contributing to both business and academic understanding.
Within the Philippine context specifically, the variables examined here have barely been studied, since this
subject area had not been thoroughly researched at the time this work was conducted.
Significance of the Study
The findings offer a scientific foundation for understanding the brand attitudes of Filipino millennials — a
group that makes up about a third of the country's population and roughly half its labor force.
Objectives of the Study
This study looks at how actual and ideal self-congruity shape millennial consumers' attitudes toward luxury
brands, along with how materialistic and status-value dimensions factor into those attitudes.
It aims to build a fuller picture of young consumers' brand attitudes and buying behavior toward luxury
goods, with the goal of helping brands unlock the commercial potential of this segment and meet the
specific needs of Gen-Y consumers among Filipino millennials. Specifically, the study aims to:
1. Determine what motivates Filipino millennials to buy and use luxury brands, in terms of actual and ideal
self-congruity.
2. Identify which variables — across education, income, gender, and age groups — significantly affect the
purchase and consumption of luxury brands among Filipino millennials.
3. Determine how materialistic value, status value, and social desirability scores affect the purchase and
use of luxury brands among Filipino millennials.
Statement of the Problem
This study centers on how actual and ideal self-congruity — that is, how closely a consumer's self-image
matches a luxury brand's image — shapes brand attitude. The ideal self-image refers to how a person
wants to be, or wishes to be seen, including the attitudes, perceptions, and feelings they hope others will
associate with them (Ross, 1971; Schenk & Holman, 1980).
Consuming luxury brands can help someone move closer to their ideal self by projecting the image and
symbolic meaning tied to those brands, reinforcing a more perfect self-image.
Because self-concept matters so much to individuals, behavior is often geared toward reinforcing one's
own self-concept — consuming luxury goods and brands as symbols that communicate meaning both to
oneself and to others (Grubb & Grathwohl, 1967).
Brand attitude plays a central role in a brand's overall equity. It reflects an individual's overall evaluation of
a brand, shaped mainly by how they perceive it (Aaker, 1997). Self-congruity, in turn, helps explain and
predict both brand attitude and purchase intent (Sirgy et al., 1997; Helgeson & Supphellen, 2004).
Self-identity value, materialistic value, and status value are all closely tied together (Richins & Dawson,

1992; Dittmar, 1994; Webster & Beatty, 1997).
1. To what extent are Filipino millennials motivated to purchase and consume luxury brands in terms of
actual and ideal self-congruity?
2. What effect do materialistic value and status value have on the purchase and consumption of luxury
brands among Filipino millennials?
3. Which variables — across education, income, gender, and age groups — significantly affect the
purchase and consumption of luxury brands among Filipino millennials?
4. Which items show significant correlations with the purchase and consumption of luxury brands among
Filipino millennials?

Methodology
This study looks at how actual and ideal self-congruity shape attitudes toward luxury brands, using a
descriptive research design meant to examine the relationship between variables within the Philippine
setting. A survey was the main tool used, with data gathered through a questionnaire and later
standardized for easier comparison. Both primary and secondary data were used, with the primary data
collected specifically to answer the research questions and meet the study's objectives. The questionnaire
relied entirely on closed, pre-set questions rather than open-ended ones — a highly structured format that
kept respondents' own wording to a minimum.
To help respondents distinguish luxury from non-luxury, the questionnaire opened by presenting several
well-known Philippine brands in both categories, giving respondents a concrete brand to picture before
answering the questions that followed. A five-point Likert scale was used throughout, running from
strongly disagree to strongly agree. The study also included a shortened version of the Crowne-Marlowe
social desirability scale (Crowne & Marlowe, 1960) to gauge how much respondents cared about social
approval, since social desirability bias is one of the most common distortions in survey research (King &
Bruner, 2000). A lower score on this scale suggests a respondent is less worried about social approval
and more likely to answer honestly and represent themselves accurately (Crowne & Marlowe, 1960).
Respondents were Filipino millennials drawn from a higher education institution, along with young
entrepreneurs, employees, employers, and graduates who consume luxury brands, since this was the
population the study was interested in. The sample totaled 134 respondents — a number considered
sufficient to meet the standards of efficiency, representativeness, reliability, and flexibility that Kothari
(2004) sets out for an optimal sample.
Non-probability convenience sampling was used, chosen for how easy it made gathering responses; this
combined self-selection sampling with convenience sampling. OJT students and alumni helped distribute
the questionnaire as enumerators.
Data Analysis
Survey data was processed using SPSS, relying mainly on descriptive statistics to describe and compare
variables numerically.

to check the relationship between actual and ideal self-congruity, materialistic value, and status value, and
how these shape brand attitudes toward both luxury and non-luxury brands. In total, 134 respondents took
part in the study.

Conclusion
The Filipino millennials' attitude toward buying and using luxury brands leans more heavily on ideal
self-congruity than on actual self-congruity. Marketers should keep in mind that Filipino millennials are
driven to buy and use luxury brands based on how they want to see themselves, which means they:
– Want to picture themselves as part of the luxury brand's world
– Want the people they admire to also use the luxury brand
– Want to be associated with the kind of people who consume that luxury brand
Recommendation
Since Filipino millennials are motivated to buy and use luxury brands mainly through ideal self-congruity,
marketers should consider the following:
1. Luxury brands should feel relatable and consistent with, and should align closely with, the millennial
consumer's ideal self.
2. Marketers should choose endorsers, models, and advertising themes or slogans that align with Filipino
millennials' ideal self — emphasizing functionality, image or reputation, and social desirability — across
promotional strategy, packaging, and distribution channels that come across as upscale and refined.
Because Filipino millennials' attitude toward luxury brand purchase and consumption is driven more by
ideal self-congruity than actual self-congruity, marketers should keep this consistently in mind when
shaping their strategy, since millennials:
– Want to picture themselves as part of the luxury brand's world
– Want the people they admire to also use the luxury brand
– Want to be associated with the kind of people who consume that luxury brand
One limitation worth noting is the absence of open-ended questions, which could have let respondents
explain their views in more depth and offered a fuller picture of what really drives luxury consumption. That
said, allowing open responses could also have produced answers only tangentially related to the specific
luxury value dimensions under study, given how many other dimensions exist beyond those examined
here. Open-ended questions were therefore left out to keep the survey from becoming too long.
Future research could also take a qualitative approach instead, offering broader insight into the
phenomenon and letting respondents express their views more freely. This could then be paired with a
follow-up survey questionnaire built around whatever themes emerge from the qualitative findings, helping
to zero in on the most relevant aspects of the phenomenon and surface new research directions.
Luxury brand consumption remains a large and growing area, and there is plenty of room for further
research into it.
