Pros and Cons of Transnational and Multinational Corporations and the Integration of the 
Philippines into the Global Economy 
 
What are Transnational Corporations (TNCs) and Multinational Corporations (MNCs)? 
TNCs and MNCs are corporations that operate manufacturing or services in different countries in 
order to take advantage of their differences in cost of labor, resources, market situations, and 
regulations. Regarding the development economy of the Philippines, there are many advantages 
and disadvantages associated with these organizations. MNCs have greatly sped up the 
Philippines’ integration into global production networks. At the same time, it has shown the 
structural weaknesses that limit the development of the country. 
 
The positive of MNCs and TNCs. Among the many strengths that MNCs offer, perhaps 
the most important is the inflow of capital, which local economies have difficulty generating on 
their own. There are various types of foreign businesses operating in different sectors and 
capacities. For instance, within a single month of 2025, foreign investors were granted ₱4.575 
billion worth of investments, both in new and expansion projects, according to the Philippine 
Economic Zone Authority (PEZA). This investment is expected to create over 9,000 jobs and 
generate $300 million in exports. Such firms include Foundever, an information technology firm 
based throughout the IT parks of the country, and Superl Philippines, a leather-goods 
manufacturing company operating in the province of Pampanga, providing employment to tens 
of thousands of Filipino workers as well as billions of pesos worth of investments (Philippine 
Economic Zone Authority, 2025). In addition to the jobs and capital, the transfer of skills and 
standards is another advantage of having MNCs in any particular place. Workers equipped with 
international standards of quality control, logistics, and software development will eventually 
increase their productive capabilities in the local labor market. 
 
However, these benefits come with significant costs. A part of the value produced never 
becomes domestic capital because overseas companies frequently transfer their profits rather 
than reinvesting them within the country. The tax discounts and other incentives that 
governments provide to attract foreign investment should be compared to the employment and 
export possibilities that are created in order to determine how much tax revenue is lost (U.S. 
Department of State, 2025). Furthermore, the country's net foreign direct investment inflow 
decreased by roughly 17% in 2025 compared to the previous year and by nearly 40% in early 
2026 compared to the same period in 2025 due to growing geopolitical risks, demonstrating the 
inherent risk of relying on such investment (Philippines Foreign Direct Investment, 2026). An 
economy that depends significantly on foreign investment is vulnerable to changes in 

 
international confidence that are unrelated to domestic circumstances. Other issues that are 
well-known from the larger literature on MNCs include the risk that flexible capital will relocate 
as soon as a more affordable location becomes available, limited backward connections to local 
suppliers when businesses import the majority of their inputs, and downward pressure on wages 
and labor standards as nations compete for investment. 
 
The involvement of MNCs or TNCs is vital in the integration of the Philippines into the 
international economy. MNCs remain the primary means of integrating the Philippines into the 
global economy despite all the downsides. PEZA-controlled zones serve as crucial centers in 
international supply chains, hosting advanced manufacturing facilities, electronics and 
semiconductor production sites, and components for renewable energy. These zones produce 
goods for both domestic markets and international consumers, including foreign-based parent 
companies, thereby facilitating the Philippines direct integration into regional and global 
production systems (Tribune, 2025). In the context of services, the business process outsourcing 
sector is relevant. Organizations such as PEZA and the Board of Investments are designed to 
attract and keep foreign investors while reform initiatives such as the CREATE MORE Act are 
made to keep the country competitive relative to its Southeast Asian competitors for attracting 
the same mobile capital (U.S. Department of State, 2025; PEZA, 2025). The organization has 
approved investments of around ₱261 billion in 2025 alone. Foreign investors, mostly in 
manufacturing businesses, include South Korea, Singapore, Japan, and the Cayman Islands 
(Zapanta, 2025). Each one of these investments is another connection between a local area in the 
Philippines and a global production or service chain due to either export requirements, 
technological standardization, or similar international business cycles experienced by the 
company itself in other places. 
 
In conclusion, rather than being absolutely good or bad, MNCs and TNCs should be 
considered as a two-sided tool for development. MNCs and TNCs are the Philippines’ closest 
contact point for international manufacturing and services networks, since they offer capital, 
employment, and technology that the Philippines finds it extremely hard to develop within its 
own economy. However, because of incentives and sensitivity to the demands of global capital 
flows, which local governments cannot fully control, they are holders of the threat of revenue 
and profit loss. Thus, what the Philippines needs to do is not to pick for openness versus 
protectionism but to build institutions and incentive frameworks that turn foreign capital’s 
temporary presence into local competence.
