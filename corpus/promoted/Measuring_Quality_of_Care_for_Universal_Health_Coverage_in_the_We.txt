Abstract
To deliver on the promise of universal health coverage, regions and countries need to invest in high-quality health systems and put quality of 
care at the forefront of priority setting and agenda. The Western Pacific Region and its member countries should establish measurements and 
standards to ensure delivery of high-quality services. In order to effectively do so, individual and system capacities to measure and use quality-
of-care data should be strengthened, localized initiatives to develop quality-of-care measures of practice and policy relevance need to be carried 
out, accountability mechanisms should be in place, and the role of quality as a driver of better health outcomes and economic benefits should 
be emphasized among all stakeholders. Such initiatives will ensure that quality-of-care measures are “measurements for action” toward quality 
improvement and reform.
Keywords: quality of care; universal health coverage; Philippines; Western Pacific

The importance of quality of care and how to 
measure it
Quality of care is fundamental in the pursuit of universal 
health coverage (UHC) and the Sustainable Development 
Goals [1,2]. Its importance has been reaffirmed through the 
United Nations (UN) Political Declaration on UHC and other 
global development priorities and agenda. Despite being a key 
focus area, however, around 8.4 million people in low- and 
middle-income countries (LMICs) continue to die every year 
because of poor quality of care resulting in US$ 6 trillion in 
economic losses [1,2]. This highlights the need to invest in 
patient-centered health systems that deliver high-quality ser-
vices to ensure the efficient use of limited health resources and 
save millions of lives from preventable diseases and mortality 
[1,2].
Recognizing that meaningful and accurate measurements 
are central to high-quality health systems, global and inter-
national efforts have been made to develop measurements 
of quality. These include the Healthcare Access and Quality 
Index based on amenable mortality [3] and a set of indicators 
proposed in the World Health Organization (WHO) monitor-
ing and evaluation framework for primary health care [4]. At 
the regional level, the WHO Africa Region has constructed 
a multidimensional index for their setting, while the Eastern 
Mediterranean Region has identified their priority indicators 
[5,6]. Many others have been developed, but regions and 
countries need to lead localized initiatives to ensure appropri-
ateness to the setting, responsiveness to needs, acceptability to 
users, and usability for quality improvement. Extensive collec-
tion and reporting of indicators, as in the experience of Mex-
ico with more than 1000 collected [2], prove to be inefficient 
and ineffective in providing and acting on evidence-informed 
recommendations.
Progress and challenges in the Western Pacific: 
illustrative example from the Philippines
In the region, quality healthcare has been articulated as an 
aspiration in national health policies for UHC, acknowledging 
that poor-quality health services contribute to health system 
inefficiencies and poor health outcomes [7]. For 2020–2025, 
the region’s vision “For the Future” is explicit on its commit-
ment to quality healthcare as it relates to patient safety and 
combating antimicrobial resistance. However, it is viewed as 
a secondary priority to more primordial issues such as avail-
ability, service delivery, and financing [7,8]. This is reflected in 
the absence of a regional quality-of-care index, with various 
indicators and methodologies applied depending on country 
priorities, capacities, and resources.

The Philippines, an archipelagic LMIC, continues to 
address issues on service delivery and financing, but now 
needs to put quality of care at the forefront with its transition 
toward UHC and primary health care. Numerous data sources 
report on quality at the national, subnational, community, 
facility, or individual level. However, quality measurements 
are fragmented by disease and not standardized, owing to 
(i) gaps in capacities to develop indicators that are of high 
impact and value and (ii) the lack of a common understand-
ing of quality among service providers, patients, and political 
actors. There are also limited data on experiential quality 
or patient experience, as current systems focus on service 
availability, selected processes, and health outcomes. More 
importantly, findings are not translated into strategies and 
policies for improved quality healthcare, even with estab-
lished accreditation and regulatory standards. This is partly 
due to the absence of a uniform set of measures in both 
public and private facilities [9], embedded within accountabil-
ity mechanisms and legal frameworks. Additionally, health 
information systems are siloed—many of which in public pri-
mary care facilities in remote settings remain paper-based and 
error-prone—and therefore lack transparency, interoperabil-
ity, usability, and scalability. This is further aggravated by the 
decentralized Philippine health system, which makes routine 
collection of indicators and storage of large volumes of data 
difficult especially in resource-constrained settings. Collec-
tively, these prevent comprehensive, comparable, and regular 
measurements for UHC monitoring and healthcare reform 
across geographical settings and population groups.
Recommendations
For quality-of-care measures to be “measurements for action” 
[2], the region and the countries themselves will need to deter-
mine their highest priorities and make the necessary invest-
ments. The development of metrics may be done through 
localized initiatives to establish measures that allow for more 
granular and context-specific estimates, and to identify appro-
priate equity stratifiers (i.e., demographic, social, economic, 
racial, or geographic characteristics that identify population 
subgroups, and detect disparities within and across coun-
tries). These approaches can lead to a better understanding of 
sources of variations in quality, while ensuring that their rou-
tine collection does not place undue burden on information 
systems and stakeholders [5]. Furthermore, quality-of-care 
data should be centered on people and their experiences and 
perceptions of the health system and service delivery, ideally 
collected throughout the patient pathway [10]. Health infor-
mation systems especially in low-resource settings should be 
strengthened toward the following key features for timely 
and cost-efficient assessments: (i) comprehensive to capture 
inputs, processes, and outcomes including experiential qual-
ity and patient-care-sensitive measures; (ii) interoperable to 
integrate data across different systems and platforms; and 
(iii) responsive to account for evolving priorities and needs 
over time. To effectively drive action and reform, measure-
ments and performance accountability mechanisms should 
be aligned and institutionalized through a national policy 
on health system quality [2]. All key stakeholders including 
patients and the public, community health volunteers, health 
professionals and managers, researchers, and those from the 
civil society, the private sector, and the government should be 
informed of the importance of measuring quality of care as a 
necessary input to quality health systems. More importantly, 
they should be involved in creating a supportive environment 
for quality healthcare using a multisectoral approach with 
strong governance structures. In doing so, we develop high-
quality health systems that are patient-centered, responsive to 
a broad array of health conditions, cost-efficient, sustainable, 
and socially inclusive and accountable.
