A B S T R A C T   
Ozone (O3)-induced health effects vary in terms of severity, from deterioration of lung function and hospitali­
zation to death. Several studies have reported a linear increase in health risks after O3 exposure. However, 
current evidence suggests a non-linear U- and J-shaped concentration-response (C-R) function. The potential 
increasing risks with decreasing O3 concentrations may seem counterintuitive from the traditional standpoint 
that decreasing exposure should lead to decreasing health risks. Tus, the question of whether the increasing risks 
with decreasing concentrations are truly O3-induced or might be from other C-R mechanisms. If these potential 
risks were not accounted for, this may have contributed to the risks observed at the low ozone concentration 
range. In this study, we examined the short-term effects of photochemical oxidant (Ox, parts per billiion) on 
outpatient cardiorespiratory visits in 21 Japanese cities after adjusting for other air pollutant-specific C-R 
functions. Daily cardiorespiratory visits from January 1, 2014 to December 31, 2016 were obtained from the 
Japanese Medical Data Center Co. Ltd. Similar period of meteorological and air pollution variables were obtained 
from relevant data sources. We utilized a time-stratified case crossover design coupled with the generalized 
additive mixed model (TSCC-GAMM) to estimate the association between Ox and cardiorespiratory outpatient 
visits, after adjusting for several covariates. A total of 2,588,930 visits were recorded across the study period, 
with a mean of 111.87 and a standard deviation of 138.75. The results revealed that crude Ox-cardiorespiratory 
visits exhibited a U-shaped pattern. However, adjustment of the oxides of nitrogen, particularly nitrogen mon­
oxide (NO), attenuated the lower risk curve and subsequently altered the shape of the C-R function, with a 
substantial reduction observed during winter. NO- and nitrogen dioxide (NO2)-adjusted Ox-cardiorespiratory 
associations increased nearly linearly, without an apparent threshold. Current evidence suggests the importance 
of adjusting the oxides of nitrogen in estimating the Ox C-R risk functions.   
1. Introduction 
Ozone (O3)-induced health effects vary in terms of severity, from 
lung function deterioration, and hospitalization to death (Nuvolone 
et al., 2018). These health risks, in turn, translate into a disease burden 
in terms of morbidity and mortality. Malley et al. (2017) noted a global 
estimate of 1.04–1.23 million respiratory deaths attributable to O3 
exposure, whereas Anenberg et al. (2018) estimated that 9–23 million 
annual asthma emergency room visits globally in 2015 were attributed 
to O3. In Japan, country-led air pollution reduction efforts have been 
instrumental in the decline of annual concentrations of particulate 
matter with 2.5 μm or less in diameter (PM2.5); however, annual O3 
concentrations have remained mostly unchanged with annual maximum 
1-h daytime O3 concentrations hovering between 109.9 and 118.5 parts 
per billion (ppb) from 1990 to 2018 (Ito et al., 2021). These unchanged 
concentration range elicit an array of short- and long-term toxicological

and epidemiological effects in the exposed population. 
Several epidemiological studies have revealed both short- and long- 
term positive associations between O3 and human mortality, which is a 
result of the oxidative stress caused by O3 exposure (Hulo et al., 2011; 
Sunil et al., 2013). In Japan, Ng et al. (2013) noticed a statistically 
significant short-term O3 effect on all-cause mortality (0.69%; 95% CI: 
0.27–1.10) during the intermediate season between summer and winter. 
In addition to mortality, several studies have documented both 
short-term and long-term effects on morbidity outcomes such as hospi­
talizations (Lin et al., 2008; Luong et al., 2018) and emergency room 
visits (Carlsen et al., 2013; Tian et al., 2018). 
Health risks associated with exposure to O3 are largely driven by 
underlying physiological mechanisms, which are usually illustrated 
through concentration-response (C-R) curves. However, studies 
regarding O3-health outcome C-R curves have been largely inconsistent, 
reporting a combination of linear (Chen et al., 2017) and non-linear C-R 
(Bae et al., 2015; Faustini et al., 2019; Huang et al., 2019) functional 
shapes. The motivation behind the linear fit is the conservative 
assumption of linearity in the first instance, driven by data, with further 
optimization whenever the data warrants a non-linear treatment. 
Studies utilizing a linear fit have mostly observed an increasing risk with 
increasing concentrations (Bell et al., 2004; Gryparis et al., 2004; 
Vicedo-Cabrera et al., 2020). In several instances, O3-health outcome 
associations potentially exhibit thresholds, with risks increasing beyond 
a set point concentration (Kim et al., 2004). However, several current 
studies suggest that there are increasing health risks to the left and right 
sides of the set point concentration (where risks are assumed to be the 
lowest), which are apparent in either J- or U-shaped C-R curves (Bae 
et al., 2015; Faustini et al., 2019; Huang et al., 2019). The increasing risk 
with decreasing O3 concentration, based on a J- and U-shaped C-R curve, 
may seem counterintuitive from the standpoint of exposure science 
because decreasing exposure is tantamount to decreasing health risk. 
However, there is a plausible mechanism that entails photochemical 
processes involving nitrogen oxides, namely nitrogen dioxide (NO2) and 
nitrogen monoxide (NO), and their role in the titration of O3 (Han et al., 
2011; Song et al., 2011). Several studies have noted that the adjustment 
of nitrogen oxides lead to a reduction in O3-related health risks (Faustini 
et al., 2019; Tao et al., 2012). 
Against this backdrop, we sought to understand this phenomenon by 
determining the short-term effects of O3 on cardiorespiratory visits, 
particularly considering the potential role of nitrogen oxides. Owing to 
the seasonal variation in O3, we further examined the season-specific 
associations. 
2. Methods 
2.1. Study areas 
The 21 study areas are located across the country as shown in Fig. 1. 
All cities, excluding Tokyo, are ordinance-designated cities (ODC) 
established by the Japanese government. ODC cities are characterized 
by 1) population size greater than 500,000, 2) a population density of 
greater than 2000 population per km2, 3) being considered as a major 
city in their region, and 4) having less than 10% of their employment in 
primary industries (Ohsugi, 2011; Steiner, 1965). ODCs differ from or­
dinary cities because they can execute functions that are originally 
performed or delegated to prefectural governments (Jacobs, 2011). 
2.2. Health outcome data 
Daily cardiorespiratory visits of 21 cities from January 1, 2014 to 
December 31, 2016 were obtained from the Japanese Medical Data 
Center (JMDC) Co. Ltd. The anonymized information was based on the 
health insurance claims of the specific treatments and other de­
mographic variables for employees of Japanese companies and their 
familiees, most of them were aged 65 years or younger (Kimura et al., 
2010). Cardiorespiratory visits were coded using the International 
Classification of Disease (ICD) 10 for cardiovascular (I00–I99) and res­
piratory (J00-J99) diseases. Cardiorespiratory outpatient visits in July, 
August, and September were categorized as summer-related visits, 
whereas winter months included December, January, and February. 
Hereafter, we refer to these visits as cardiorespiratory outpatient visits, 
unless otherwise stated. This study was approved by the Ethics Com­
mittee of the Graduate School of Engineering, Kyoto University with the 
Institutional Review Board (IRB) No. 201714.

2.3. Air pollution data 
Air pollution data from January 1, 2014 to December 31, 2016, were 
obtained from the National Institute for Environmental Studies (NIES). 
Hourly air pollution data were obtained from background monitoring 
stations located within each city. Background monitoring stations with 
continuous and non-missing air pollution data were used in this study. 
The number of eligible background monitoring stations per city is pre­
sented in Table S1. For locations with more than one eligible back­
ground monitoring station, the mean across these locations was used. 
We took the 24-h mean concentration of NO (ppb; parts per billion), NO2 
(ppb) and PM2.5 (μg/m3). When monitoring stations with more than four 
out of the 24 missing data points, these were assumed to be missing data 
days. For O3, we used photochemical oxidant (Ox) (ppb). In Japan, 
because O3 is the major component of Ox, the Japanese Ministry of 
Environment decided to equate O3 concentrations with Ox concentra­
tions in 1996 (Fukunaga et al., 2021). Several studies have also used Ox 
in lieu of O3when examining ozone-related effects on health (Chen et al., 
2014; Ng et al., 2013; Vicedo-Cabrera et al., 2020). Ox is defined as an 
oxidizing substance produced mainly through photochemical reactions 
based on the potassium iodide (KI) method, a wet chemical method that 
responds to photochemical oxidants, primarily ozone (≥90%), followed 
by peroxyacetyl nitrate (PAN), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), and organic 
hydroperoxides (Akimoto, 2017). 
Specifically, we utilized the maximum 8-hour moving mean ozone of 
the day. Days with missing values were interpolated utilizing the in­
formation of the neighboring days’ data via the k-nearest neighbor 
interpolation (Daberdaku et al., 2020). 
2.4. Meteorological data 
Daily mean ambient air temperature (◦C) and relative humidity (%) 
during the same study period were obtained from NIES. The summary 
statistics for the air pollution and meteorological variables are shown in 
Table S1. 
2.5. Statistical analyses 
We utilized a time-stratified case crossover design coupled with the 
generalized additive mixed model (TSCC-GAMM) to estimate the asso­
ciation between ozone and cardiorespiratory outpatient visits in the 21 
cities after adjusting for the relevant covariates (as shown in Equation 
(1)). 
Briefly, a case-crossover (CCO) study is a self-matched case-control 
study as proposed by Maclure (1991), wherein the exposure at each 
event is matched with the exposures for the same subject at his/her 
referent times. This is further expanded by Lumley and Levy (2000) to a 
time-stratified CCO, which takes referent times within the same stratum 
(i.e., month and day of the week) (Carracedo-Martínez et al., 2010). A 
more detailed specification of the study design is discussed elsewhere 
(Carracedo-Martínez et al., 2010; Janes et al., 2005; Lumley and Levy, 
2000).

The daily cardiorespiratory outpatient visits (yi,t) of city (i) in time (t) 
followed a Poisson distribution with a logarithmic link function. A quasi- 
Poisson distribution was used for overdispersion in the health outcomes. 
The outpatient visit (yi,t) was modeled with the intercept (β0), linear 
terms for holidays (hodt), and binary influenza epidemic term 
(influenzat), whereby we coded weeks (and eventually converted to 
days) by taking the influenza count over the study period exceeding 80% 
as 1, otherwise, 0 (Wang et al., 2017). The stratum of year, month, and 
day of the week (stratumt) was used to represent the time-stratification 
within the case-crossover design. Non-linear terms included the natu­
ral cubic spline for same-day ozone, ns(ozone,3), with three degrees of 
freedom (df), 3-day lagged mean air temperature with 5df, and 3df for 
relative humidity. The oxides of nitrogen, NO and NO2, were further 
adjusted in the model via the ns(airpolt,df), with 4 df and 3 df (based on 
the sensitivity analysis in Table S2), respectively. In subsequent sections, 
we draw comparisons among the three models, namely the ozone-only 
(crude), NO-adjusted, and NO2-adjusted models. Since TSCC-GAMM or 
mixed models, in general, generated a pooled estimate of the 21 cities 
(Spieler and Schumacher, 2019), the succeeding results presented in this 
study are for the pooled association. 
Based on our initial analysis, we observed a non-linear association 
between ozone concentration and visits. Thus, we defined the minimum 
risk point (MRP) as the concentration at which the risk of a cardiore­
spiratory visit was the lowest. Further preliminary results also revealed 
that accounting for the oxides of nitrogen yielded a candidate threshold 
model for the ozone-cardiorespiratory visit association. To ascertain 
whether a threshold existed, we followed the threshold analysis imple­
mented in previous studies (Bell et al., 2006; Chen et al., 2017). In brief, 
the threshold analysis assumes that the ozone-cardiorespiratory risk 
below a potential threshold is null, whereas risks beyond (the potential 
threshold) are amonotonically increasing. We performed a potential 
threshold analysis for each city at each potential threshold over a range 
of concentrations from 0 to 80 ppb in 1 ppb increments. For each po­
tential city and potential threshold, we estimated model performance 
using the Akaike Information Criterion for quasi-Poisson models (qAIC) 
(Akaike, 1974). We then calculated the mean of the qAIC values per 
potential threshold (Bell et al., 2006). The rationale of this approach is to 
simply compare the mean qAIC when the potential threshold is equal to 
zero, equivalent to the no-threshold model, with the lowest potential 
threshold across the range of potential thresholds. If a threshold exists, 
the mean qAIC will be lower than that of the no-threshold model. A 
lower qAIC value indicates a better fit. Specifically, if the difference 
between the potential threshold at 0 and the lowest potential threshold 
is more than two, there is likely to be a difference in the model fit 
(Burnham and Anderson, 2004; Chen et al., 2017). We further adjusted 
for the non-linear terms of same-day NO and NO2 with 4df and 3df, 
respectively. The df specifications for the non-linear terms included in 
the model are summarized in Table S2. 
In view of the seasonal variability of ozone, we also conducted a 
season-specific analysis in summer and winter. In addition, we used the 
1st and 99th percentiles of the C-R curve to illustrate the changes below 
and above the MRP, respectively. Similarly, we utilized the season- 
specific 1st and 99th percentiles in determining the season-specific 
risks below and above the MRP. All analyses were performed using R 
Statistical Programming version 4.2.2 (R Core Team, 2019). 
3. Results 
3.1. Descriptive statistics 
A total of 2,588,930 visits were recorded across 21 cities during the 
study period, with a mean of 111.87 and a standard deviation of 138.75 
(Table 1). The 21 Japanese cities are located across the country, 
including the Sapporo (in the north) and the southern city of Kumamoto, 
highlighted in Fig. 1. Northern cities are relatively colder with Sapporo 
recording a year-round daily mean air temperature of 9.55 ◦C compared 
to southern cities, which are approximately 7 ◦C higher (Table S1). Air 
pollution concentrations are well below the daily national standards. 
Year-round, 24-h mean NO2 concentrations as well as the mean 8-h 
maximum Ox during the study period are below the national daily 
standards of 40–60 ppb (for NO2) and 60 ppb (for Ox), respectively. A 
year-round correlational association revealed a statistically weak 
negative correlation between Ox and cardiorespiratory visits (r = -0.05), 
whereas a moderately-weak and positive association was observed with

3.2. Overall Ox -visit association 
We observed a U-shaped crude association between Ox and visits, 
with a minimal risk at 32 ppb, as shown in Fig. 2. After adjusting for NO2 
(grey, triangular central estimates), changes, though apparent, were 
minimal and not statistically different from the Ox -only model (in 1st 
percentile, Figure S2). However, NO-adjustment (grey, square-shaped 
central estimates) resulted in a substantial and statistically significant 
(p-value < 0.001) reduction in the lower risk curve, suggesting the 
probable importance of NO exposure on cardiorespiratory clinic visits. 
While we observed an increasing risk with increasing concentration 
beyond the MRP, at the 99th percentile all pollutant-adjusted models 
were not statistically different from the Ox -only model (Fig S2). Visu­
ally, NO- or NO2-adjusted Ox -cardiorespiratory associations exhibited a 
potential threshold model, with a linear increase beyond the MRP. 
However, the results from the threshold analysis indicated a lack of 
evidence for a threshold (Fig S5). 
3.3. Season-specific Ox C-R associations 
As shown in Fig. 3, the Ox -related risks in summer increased nearly 
linearly with increasing Ox concentrations. During winter, the Ox C-R 
function included a null association at the upper concentrations, with a 
departure from the null association at the lower concentrations. How­
ever, we noted a substantial reduction in the risks at the lower con­
centration range after adjustment for NO and NO2, with a more evident 
reduction with NO, which approached the null association during 
winter. This is supported by the significant reduction in the lower risk 
curve (represented by the 1st percentile, as shown in Fig S3) after 
adjusting for NO (p-value = 0.02) during winter. We did not observe any 
difference between the crude model and the adjusted models for the 1st 
percentile in either summer or winter, or in the 99th percentile of winter. 
4. Discussion 
A crude Ox -cardiorespiratory visit exhibited a U-shaped pattern. 
However, adjustment of the oxides of nitrogen, particularly NO, atten­
uated the lower risk curve and subsequently altered the shape of the 
crude Ox C-R function. Season-specific analysis revealed the relative 
importance of specific oxides of nitrogen in the attenuation of the Ox C-R 
function.

other studies (Bae et al., 2015; Faustini et al., 2019). 
There is no clear physiological mechanism, however, which may 
explain how low Ox concentrations, particularly those approaching zero 
would lead to an increase in the risk. This leads to an inquiry as to 
whether the estimated incremental risk with decreasing Ox concentra­
tion is a true association or an artefact of the limited modeling flexibility 
in covariate adjustment across the exposure range. After adjusting for 
several C-R functions, we found that adjustment for either NO or NO2 led 
to a substantial reduction in the Ox-visit risk curve, particularly in the 
lower concentration range (Fig. 2). The observed reduction may be 
related to the chemical mechanism, specifically, the photochemical 
process involved in the Ox and oxides of nitrogen titration. 
4.2. Photochemical processes of Ox alongside NO and NO2 
Photochemical processes involved in the production of tropospheric 
O3 include precursors such as NO and NO2, as well as volatile organic 
compounds (VOCs). NO2 and NO, in particular, titrate O3, hence in high 
NO2 and NO concentrations, O3 is depleted (Han et al., 2011; Song et al., 
2011). More detailed photochemical processes have been discussed 
previously (Crutzen, 1979; Wu et al., 1973). A large body of evidence 
has linked oxides of nitrogen exposure to various physiological inflam­
matory responses (Persinger et al., 2001; Ricciardolo et al., 2004; van 
der Vliet et al., 2000), and which may lead to the development of 
short-/long-term morbidity outcomes (Gillespie-Bennett et al., 2011; 
Studnicka et al., 1997), and even death (Faustini et al., 2014; Samoli 
et al., 2006). The high concentration of the oxides of nitrogen, during 
the low Ox concentration period, together with the documented health 
risks, posits a candidate mechanism, which may partially explain the 
observed health risks at low Ox concentrations. This is further high­
lighted in the following section in relation to the season-specific 
analysis. 
4.3. Ox C-R functions by season 
In Fig. 3, we observed a generally increasing risk during summer and 
a weakly decreasing risk during winter. Several studies have also noted 
similar yet inconsistent trends of increasing risks during summer and 
statistically significant and non-significant reduced risks during winter 
(Chen et al., 2014; Zanobetti and Schwartz, 2008). Such trends may be 
related to photochemical processes leading to different exposure range 
and thus a difference in the risks, as mentioned in the earlier section. It is 
noteworthy that the adjustment of NO or NO2 did not substantially 
change the crude Ox C-R function during summer. However, this was not 
the case for winter, when an apparent reduction in the risk, especially in 
the lower part of the crude Ox C-R function, was observed. Williams et al. 
(2014) noted that changes in O3-related mortality risks after adjusting 
for NO2 were more apparent in winter than in summer, in London. On 
the contrary, Bero Bedada et al. (2016) noted a larger risk reduction in 
summer than in winter after NO2 adjustment in Stockholm County. To 
date, there is no conclusive evidence on the direction of the risks related 
to the oxides of nitrogen-adjusted Ox-health outcomes during summer 
and winter, and thus this remains to be further elucidated in future 
studies.

attenuation of the lower risk curve than the NO2-adjustment, as shown 
in Figs. 2 and 3. The adjustment diminished the lower risk curve, which 
crossed the null association below the MRP, while maintaining a positive 
and near-linear increase in risk above the MRP. There is no existing

oxides of nitrogen on the lagged Ox exposure-response associations (as 
shown in Figs S7 and S8). The impact of NO and NO2 was limited to the 
same-day association (lag 0) and exhibited minimal to no impact from 
the subsequent lags from lag 1 to 7. The immediate effect of the oxides of 
nitrogen adjustment on the Ox exposure-response association may be 
related to the titration process between nitrogen and Ox, which occurs 
over a short period of time. 
4.5. Linearity of the Ox-cardiorespiratory visit: no evidence of a threshold 
The lowest potential threshold was 32 ppb (Fig S5). The potential 
threshold (at 32 ppb) offered a marginally better model fit than the no- 
threshold model, with a meager difference of 0.21 between the two 
models. However, because the difference was less than 2, compelling 
evidence of a threshold is lacking in this case. This concurs with previous 
studies whereby in contiguous 98 US urban communities, Bell et al. 
(2006) noted that amidst the use of a spline approach, there was a 
monotonic linear increase in the risk from the minimum concentration 
range towards the uppermost exposure range. Chen et al. (2017) shares a 
similar observation in Jiangsu, China, where the potential threshold at 
90 ppb offered a minimally better fit than the no-threshold model, but 
ultimately within a 2 qAIC difference. 
5. Limitations 
Although we have employed rigorous statistical modeling techniques 
to adjust for the relevant covariates and conducted several sensitivity 
analyses to establish the robustness of our results, there are still several 
key limitations to which succeeding studies can refer and build upon. 
The current study is limited to clinic visit information aggregated at the 
city level, which may have affected exposure matching. We crudely 
assumed that these clinic visits occur in populated areas in the city 
where these monitoring stations are co-located. However, with the lack 
of granular data, we cannot fully ascertain this assumption; hence, there 
is a need for more detailed address information whenever available. 
More detailed data in terms of sex, age and cause-specific categories 
would provide deeper insights into the role of possible effect modifiers. 
Concurrent with the limited number of cities is a generalization of the 
results. Caution should be exercised with the interpretation of results, 
particularly regarding the generalization to other cities across the 
country. After a sensitivity analysis adjusting for particulate matter with 
a size of 2.5 μm or less in diameter (PM2.5), the risk curve remained 
unchanged (Fig S6). However, there may be other pollutants or aerosols 
that may impact the C-R curve due to data unavailability; these effects 
could not be explored. Finally, we offer only a partial explanation of the 
difference in the C-R functions for NO and NO2 from a data-driven 
perspective. It is reasonable to believe that the functional C-R shapes 
are driven by differential toxicity, especially at the upper extreme con­
centrations. Future studies could benefit from relevant toxicological 
studies (i.e. mouse models) to explore this phenomenon. 
6. Conclusions 
After adjusting for NO, the risk of cardiorespiratory visits after Ox 
exposure increased linearly with no apparent evidence of a threshold. 
Contrary to expectations, NO adjustment led to a statistically significant 
reduction in Ox-cardiorespiratory risks in the low concentration range 
compared with NO2-adjustment. The observed differences after the 
respective adjustments and their impact on the Ox-cardiorespiratory C-R 
curve may, in part, be related to the differential toxicity of the expo­
sures. Further investigation of the role of differential toxicity, in relation 
to NO and NO2 exposure is required to elucidate these results.
