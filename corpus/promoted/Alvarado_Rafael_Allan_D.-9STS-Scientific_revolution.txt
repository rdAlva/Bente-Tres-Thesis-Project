The Scientific Revolution 
 
The Scientific Revolution was a significant movement in scientific ideas that happened in 
the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. A new concept of nature emerged during the Scientific 
Revolution, replacing the Greek approach that had dominated science for nearly 2,000 years. 
Science developed as a distinct topic distinct from both philosophy and technology, and it began 
to be thought to serve utilitarian ends. 
 
Sir Isaac Newton's work symbolizes the peak of the Scientific Revolution towards the 
end of the 17th century. His massive Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica (1687; 
Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy) addressed the fundamental mechanical and 
cosmological issues brought by the Scientific Revolution. It established the issues and techniques 
that dominated much of astronomy and physics for well over a century, provided a physical basis 
for Kepler's laws, unified celestial, and terrestrial physics under one set of principles, and 
established the issues and methods that dominated much of astronomy and physics for well over 
a century. Through the concept of force, Newton was able to combine two basic components of 
the Scientific Revolution: mechanical philosophy and nature mathematization. To apply his laws 
to astronomy, Newton had to go above Descartes' limitations. He hypothesized a gravitational 
force acting between any two objects in the universe, but he couldn't explain how it could be 
conveyed. Using his equations of motion and a gravitational force proportionate to the inverse 
square of the distance between the centers of two bodies, Newton was able to calculate Kepler's 
laws of planetary motion. Galileo's law of free fall agrees with Newton's laws. The same force 
that causes objects to fall near the Earth's surface is also responsible for keeping the Moon and 
planets in their orbits (Scientific Revolution | Definition, History, Scientists, Inventions, & Facts, 
2019). 
 
The growing flow of information that resulted from the Scientific Revolution put 
immense strain on conventional institutions and conventions. It was no longer sufficient to 
publish scientific results in a pricey book that few could afford; information needed to be 
communicated widely and fast. Natural philosophers wanted to be certain of their data, therefore 
they sought independent and critical validation of their findings. To attain these objectives, new 
methods were devised.
