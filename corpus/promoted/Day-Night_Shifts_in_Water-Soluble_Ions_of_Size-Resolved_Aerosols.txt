ABSTRACT: The COVID-19 pandemic-driven lockdowns offer a
unique opportunity to examine how reductions in anthropogenic
emissions impacted atmospheric aerosol composition in urban
environments. This study investigates the day-night variability of
size-resolved water-soluble ions in ambient particulate matter (PM)
collected in Metro Manila before (November 2019−February
2020) and after (November 2020−February 2021) lockdown
implementation. Using tandem Micro-Orifice Uniform Deposit
Impactors (MOUDIs), aerosol samples were collected during
daytime (06:00−18:00) and nighttime (18:00−06:00) periods and
analyzed for key ionic species (sulfate, ammonium, nitrate, oxalate,
sodium, chloride, calcium, and magnesium) via ion chromatog-
raphy. Submicrometer water-soluble mass declined post-lockdown,
particularly during daytime, reflecting suppressed secondary formation under reduced anthropogenic activity, with substantial
reductions in sulfate and ammonium. In contrast, concentrations in the supermicrometer range increased due to naturally higher sea
salt levels. Chemical ratios reveal notable features post-lockdown and during daytime due to especially reduced sulfate levels:
reduced chloride depletion (on percent basis), higher ammonium-to-sulfate ratios pointing to more excess ammonia available for
reactions beyond neutralizing sulfate, and support for aqueous-phase processing preferentially forming more oxalate relative to
sulfate. These findings underscore how both photochemistry and changes in anthropogenic activity influence aerosol composition,
with implications for air quality and atmospheric processing in coastal urban cities.
KEYWORDS: Metro Manila, size-segregated water-soluble ions, diurnal variation, chloride depletion, COVID-19
1. INTRODUCTION
Water-soluble aerosol species, which include both inorganic
and organic ions, constitute a substantial fraction of particulate
matter (PM) mass.1,2 These species play a critical role in
shaping aerosol properties and have wide-ranging impacts on
air quality,3 human health,4 and climate through their influence
on hygroscopicity, acidity, and radiative effects.5
The diurnal variability of water-soluble species depends on
their chemical formation mechanisms as well as atmospheric
conditions and sources that differ between daytime and
nighttime. In urban environments, meteorological conditions
and anthropogenic activities differ markedly over the course of
the day.6 Daytime is typically characterized by enhanced solar
radiation that drives photochemical reactions that facilitate the
oxidation of precursor gases (e.g., SO2, NOx, and VOCs),
promoting the formation of secondary aerosol species.6,7 In
contrast, nighttime processes are influenced by shallower
boundary layers, reduced photochemical activity, and alter-
native oxidation pathways such as reactions involving nitrate
radicals.8,9 Because these processes influence particles of
different sizes in different ways, size-resolved measurements
are essential to understand their atmospheric transformation,
transport, and fate.10,11
The 2019 novel coronavirus disease (COVID-19) pandemic
led to widespread reductions in anthropogenic emissions due
to strict lockdown measures implemented globally. These
changes, such as decreased vehicular traffic and reduced
industrial activity, resulted in notable declines in air pollutants,
including PM and NOx, in many urban regions, e.g.,12−14

due to differences in lockdown stringency and emission
sources, e.g.,.16,17 In Metro Manila, stringent restrictions
beginning in March 2020 led to a decline in vehicular traffic
and industrial operations, resulting in lower PM2.5 levels
compared to pre-lockdown conditions. As restrictions
gradually eased, PM2.5 concentration rebounded but still
remained lower than pre-lockdown levels.15 While most
studies have focused on concentrations of broad classes of
pollutants, fewer have examined how lockdown-related
emission changes affected aerosol chemical composition,
particularly size-resolved water-soluble species in coastal
megacities such as Metro Manila.
Few studies have examined how potential compositional
changes vary diurnally, highlighting the need to assess diurnal
patterns in context of Metro Manila’s complex air quality,
which is primarily driven by anthropogenic sources such as
vehicular traffic, industrial activities, and waste processing,
along with other contributors including sea salt, aged aerosols,
and biomass burning, e.g.,.18−21 Prior studies at the same
sampling site used in this work have characterized the size-
resolved water-soluble ionic composition of PM using 48-h
bulk samples. Aged aerosol and sea salt were identified as some
of major sources of ionic PM in the region, with aged aerosol
dominated by sulfate and ammonium.20 Further work has
shown that chloride depletion varies with pollutant type,22 and
that water-soluble species, particularly sulfate and ammonium,
are more sensitive to rainfall events lasting more than an
hour.23 Variations in water-soluble species have also been
associated with changes in aerosol composition linked to long-
range transport and local emissions.21
Leveraging daytime and nighttime sampling across pre- and
post-lockdown during COVID-19 pandemic, this study
investigates the variability of size-resolved ionic species and
gravimetric mass in Metro Manila, Philippines. The objective is
to assess how water-soluble ions vary between both daytime
and nighttime and also between pre-lockdown and post-
lockdown periods. In doing so, special attention is given to a
few ratios of interest related to sea salt reactivity and secondary
formation mechanisms. Findings from this work provide
insights into diurnal variability and pandemic-driven changes
in aerosol composition, with broader implications for under-
standing atmospheric processing in coastal urban environ-
ments.
2. EXPERIMENTAL METHODS
2.1. Aerosol Sampling and Chemical Analysis. This
study builds upon a previous measurement campaign
conducted as part of the Cloud, Aerosol, and Monsoon
Processes Philippines Experiment (CAMP2Ex) weatHEr and
CompoSition Monitoring (CHECSM) at the Manila Observ-
atory (MO) site in Quezon City, Philippines (14.64° N,
121.08° E), which also employed Micro-Orifice Uniform
Deposit Impactors (MOUDIs) to collect size-segregated
aerosol samples over continuous 48-h periods.24 The MOUDIs
operated at ∼30 L/min with aerodynamic cut-point diameters
(Dp) of 0.056, 0.10, 0.18, 0.32, 0.56, 1.0, 1.8, 3.2, 5.6, 10, and
18 μm.25 In the present study, two MOUDIs worked in
tandem with one sampling during daytime (06:00−18:00 local
time) and the other at nighttime (18:00−06:00) to capture
diurnal variability over three consecutive days, with total
runtimes of 33−35 h per sample set. On selected days, the two
MOUDIs were simultaneously used with one allocated for
chemical analysis and the other for gravimetric analysis. For
these sets, daytime filters were swapped out with nighttime
filters when the nighttime period began and vice versa as soon
as the daytime period started again the next day. The daytime
period captures typical traffic rush hours, which occur from
07:00−09:00 and 16:00−18:00 local time.26
Sampling initially began in November 2019 and continued
through February 2020. It was temporarily suspended due to
the COVID-19 pandemic, then resumed in November 2020 as
restrictions eased (Table S1), continuing until February 2021.
The start and end dates for each MOUDI set are summarized
in Table S2. In total, 36 sets (18 pairs of daytime and
nighttime samples) were collected for ion chromatography
(IC) analysis, and 12 sets (6 pairs) for gravimetric analysis.
The Teflon substrates were precleaned by soaking each side
in Milli-Q water for at least 12 h, then dried and stored in
methanol-cleaned Petrislides, and sealed with Parafilm to
prevent contamination from particles or gases. Mass
concentrations for each size range were determined by pre-
and post-weighing the filters using a Sartorius ME5-F
microbalance with a sensitivity of ±1 μg in a temperature
and humidity controlled room. Each substrate designated for
IC analysis was divided in half using ceramic scissors that were
cleaned with methanol before and after each cut to avoid heavy
metal contamination.24 One half was extracted using 8 mL of
Milli-Q water (18.2 MΩ·cm), followed by sonication and
subsequent analysis for ionic species (Na+, NH4
+, Mg2+, Ca2+,
oxalate, Cl−, NO3
−, SO4
2−) using IC (Thermo Scientific
Dionex ICS-2100 system). The other halves were preserved for
future analyses. A more detailed discussion of the sampling
procedures and analytical methods is provided in Stahl et al.24

Daytime and nighttime samples were further categorized
into two groups: pre-lockdown and post-lockdown. Pre-
lockdown refers to samples collected before the onset of the
COVID-19 pandemic (November 2019−February 2020),
while post-lockdown refers to samples collected after lockdown
restrictions were lifted but still during the pandemic period
(November 2020−February 2021). During the pandemic,
three different types of community quarantines were imposed
depending on the COVID-19 risk levels.30 Our post-lockdown
period measurement fell under the General Community
Quarantine (GCQ) classification (Table S1). Although public
transport was already permitted during this period, most
classes remained online, and remote work continued in some
institutions (Table S1). As a result, anthropogenic emissions
such as from vehicular transport, especially near the campus
where sampling was conducted, were expected to be reduced
due to continued remote arrangements. Additionally, because
MOUDI set (MO5) was strategically conducted in a way to
sample emissions from New Year’s Eve celebrations, it was
excluded from bulk analyses presented in Sections 3.2−3.4, but
included in the overview discussion in Section 3.1.
3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
3.1. Cumulative Water-Soluble Ionic Composition.
The total water-soluble mass concentration results are
summarized in Table S2 ranging from 1.4−12.9 μg m−3
during daytime and 2.2−10.2 μg m−3 during nighttime. For
the select sets when gravimetric analysis was conducted, the
total bulk mass ranged from 7.4−25.6 μg m−3 during the day
and 19.1−38.9 μg m−3 at night. During pre-lockdown, when
comparing the contribution of water-soluble species to bulk
mass across six gravimetric sets (3 daytime, 3 nighttime), the
pre-lockdown period showed contributions ranging from
18.9−23.2% during daytime and 11.0−23.7% during nighttime.
The elevated nighttime contribution of 23.7% was observed
during a sampling period influenced by New Year’s Eve
celebrations (MO5-N), reflecting enhanced emissions of
firework-related species that are ionic such as sulfate, nitrate,
and chloride.31 One of the lowest percentage contributions,
11.0% (MO4-N) was recorded during a period impacted by
Typhoon Phanfone (category 2), which landed on 25
December 2019 and brought high amounts of rainfall (44.4
mm), and stronger winds compared to the other sets (Table
S2). During the post-lockdown period, six gravimetric sets
were also collected (3 daytime and 3 nighttime). The sums of
water-soluble species accounted for 22.0−32.0% of the total
gravimetric mass during daytime and 14.8−29.3% during
nighttime, both of which were higher than their respective pre-
lockdown values. For comparison, the water-soluble fraction
was 33.4% of the total PM mass at the same sampling site
between July−October 2018 using bulk 48-h sampling.20
3.2. Diurnal Variability. Figure 1 and Table S3 present the
mean concentrations of water-soluble ions across different size
fractions (0.056−1.0, 1.0−18.0, 0.056−18.0 μm) for both pre-
lockdown and post-lockdown periods, separated into daytime
and nighttime sampling. In the submicrometer range, sulfate
was the most dominant species, followed by ammonium.
During the pre-lockdown daytime, sulfate reached a mean
concentration of 2.01 μg m−3, which significantly decreased to
0.83 μg m−3 post-lockdown. A similar trend was observed at
night, with sulfate levels declining from 1.74 to 1.11 μg m−3.
These reductions are consistent with decreased SO2 emissions
from vehicles and industrial activities during the COVID-19
pandemic. Ammonium also showed notable reductions from
pre- to post-lockdown during the daytime (1.42 to

measurement further indicated contributions from crustal

In the supermicrometer range (1.0−18.0 μm), nitrate was
the dominant species, followed by sulfate, calcium, sodium,
and chloride. Nitrate showed no notable decrease in daytime
levels post-lockdown, remaining nearly constant from 0.49 to
0.48 μg m−3, whereas nighttime levels decreased only slightly
more from 0.40 to 0.34 μg m−3. Calcium decreased only
slightly post-lockdown, with a reduction during daytime from
0.42 to 0.37 μg m−3 and at night from 0.38 to 0.36 μg m−3.
Magnesium showed no difference between daytime and
nighttime during pre- and post-lockdown (0.03−0.04
μg m−3). In contrast, sodium and chloride both increased
during the post-lockdown period. Daytime sodium concen-
trations increased from 0.22 to 0.40 μg m−3, while nighttime
levels increased slightly from 0.32 to 0.33 μg m−3. Chloride
concentrations increased more substantially, from 0.10 to 0.36
μg m−3 during the day and from 0.25 to 0.39 μg m−3 at night. A
potential explanation for these increases are discussed further
in the next section.
Daytime and nighttime differences in ionic concentrations
were evaluated using the coefficient of divergence (CD)39,40
and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for each species and size
fraction (Section S2). CD values above 0.2 indicated notable
day-night heterogeneity during the pre-lockdown period for
ammonium (both sub- and supermicrometer), chloride (both
sub- and supermicrometer), and sodium (supermicrometer)
(Table S4). Correspondingly, statistically significant day-night
differences (p < 0.05) were observed pre-lockdown for sodium
and chloride in the supermicrometer range and for chloride,
magnesium, and ammonium in the submicrometer fraction.
During post-lockdown period, only sulfate in the submicrom-
eter range showed statistically significant day-night differences
(Table S4).
Finally, the total water-soluble mass distributions shown in
the right-hand panels of Figure 1 reveal a distinct post-
lockdown decline in water-soluble aerosol mass, particularly
within the submicrometer range. This reduction is especially
pronounced during daytime periods, highlighting the sensi-
tivity of secondary aerosol formation to local anthropogenic
emissions and photochemical activity,7,41 which is enhanced by
high solar radiation, elevated temperatures, higher wind speed,
and deeper boundary layer heights (Figure S1 and Section S1).
In contrast, an enhancement in the supermicrometer range
(1.8−5.6 μm), mainly attributed to increases in sodium and
chloride concentrations, was observed post-lockdown, as
indicated by a more distinct bimodal distribution during
both daytime and nighttime.
3.3. Chloride’s Anomalous Behavior. The previous
section showed that, post-lockdown, only sodium and chloride
increased in mass concentration, with chloride exhibiting the
most substantial enhancement. As sea salt is the predominant
documented source of these species in the study,20,22 sea salt
reactivity may explain the lower chloride concentrations pre-
lockdown. This is because reduced anthropogenic emissions
would result in depressed concentrations of acidic ions that
would otherwise deplete more chloride in sea salt particles.
Referred to as chloride depletion, this topic was previously
examined at the same site using 48-h continuous MOUDI
measurements by AzadiAghdam et al.22 Building on that work,
chloride depletion was quantified for samples collected
specifically during daytime and nighttime, allowing for a
more detailed investigation of temporal variations (Figure 2) as
there are limited reports of how this process varies diurnally
and especially when comparing pre- and post-lockdown
periods in any region.
Chloride concentrations exhibited a clear peak in the coarse
mode (1.0−5.6 μm) during both daytime and nighttime across
the pre- and post-lockdown periods. Notably, nighttime Cl−
levels in the supermicrometer range were elevated post-
lockdown compared to pre-lockdown (e.g., increasing from

pre- and post-lockdown, no statistically significant differences

While percent depletion is useful to report, it is just as
important to quantify the absolute amount of Cl−loss.29 The
submicrometer fraction showed relatively low values (<0.03 μg
m−3), with no significant day-night variability between pre- and
post-lockdown periods. On the other hand, the super-
micrometer range exhibited higher Cl−mass loss, especially
during the post-lockdown daytime (0.17 ± 0.17 μg m−3), with
values nearly double those observed in the daytime pre-
lockdown period. This indicates that although depletion
percentages were lower in the supermicrometer range, the
absolute mass loss was more substantial. The reason that the
absolute Cl−loss is higher post-lockdown in the super-
micrometer range even though the percent depletion was lower
is that there were higher sodium (and thus sea salt) levels post-
lockdown.
Overall, during the pre-lockdown period, Cl−depletion was
more pronounced during the day, while absolute mass loss was
greater at night. In the post-lockdown period, both depletion
percentages and mass loss were higher during daytime. While
many past studies have reported reductions in various
pollutants in urban areas due to changes in pandemic
emissions rates, this study reports a change in sea salt
reactivity and composition, with implications for sea salt’s
optical and hygroscopic properties.
3.4. Oxalate:Sulfate Ratio. Another chemical ratio of
interest in this study is that between oxalate and sulfate
(OXL:SO4
2−), which is an indicator of aqueous-phase
processing.43,44 Hilario et al.45 indicated that mass-based ratios
between ∼0.015−0.03 are suggestive of air masses having
undergone aqueous processing, while ratios greater than 0.3
are classified as high-OXL populations (e.g., coarse aerosol
influence). In our data set, the OXL:SO4
2−ratio did not exceed
0.3 on average, and the submicrometer range values were
below 0.1 (Figure 3a). OXL:SO4
2−ratios peaked in the
supermicrometer range, indicating potential gas-particle
partitioning of oxalate or its precursors onto coarse particles,
consistent with previous observations at the same site using 48-
h MOUDI sampling.38,45 Additionally, linear correlations
between oxalate and sulfate mass concentrations in the
submicrometer range (Figure 3b) were strong across all
periods (R = 0.85−0.97), indicating similarity in their
formation pathways,46 and most likely linked to aqueous
processing.34,47

vs 0.03; post: 0.05 vs 0.04), while there are higher overall ratios
in the post-lockdown period due to systematically less sulfate
as compared to oxalate (Table S3). This is because the
predominant precursor to sulfate is anthropogenically derived
SO2, which was reduced post-lockdown, whereas oxalate is
derived from VOCs that stem from more diverse sources
including natural ones unaffected by the lockdown. The higher
daytime ratios are suggestive of more favorable production of
oxalate relative to sulfate, which warrants further investigation
as more statistics from this and other regions would be
beneficial.
For the supermicrometer range between 1.0−18.0 μm, only
slight day-night difference was observed during pre-lockdown
period (0.11 vs 0.12). In contrast, post-lockdown values show a
higher daytime ratio compared to nighttime (0.11 vs 0.07),
although nighttime value is lower than the pre-lockdown level.
Thus, the partitioning behavior of these acids to coarse aerosol
surfaces does not vary as much diurnally or between pre- and
post-lockdown in contrast to their relative formation
mechanisms in the submicrometer range.
4. CONCLUSIONS
This study provides new insights into the diurnal variability
and pandemic-driven shifts in the chemical compositions of
size-resolved aerosols in a Southeast Asian megacity. Water-
soluble species contributed 18.9−23.2% to total gravimetric
mass (<18 μm) during daytime and 11.0−23.7% during
nighttime in the pre-lockdown period, increasing post-
lockdown to 22.0−32.0 and 14.8−29.3%, respectively. A
distinct decline in total water-soluble aerosol mass was
observed post-lockdown for the submicrometer range,
especially during the day, emphasizing the sensitivity of
secondary aerosol formation to anthropogenic emissions and
photochemistry. This decline was driven in part by reductions
in sulfate and ammonium, likely due to decreased emissions of
precursor gases from anthropogenic sources. The depressed
levels of sulfate post-lockdown (especially daytime) resulted in
shifts in aerosol composition in the form of reduced chloride
depletion (on percent basis) and higher ammonium-to-sulfate
ratios and thus more available ammonia for reactions such as
neutralizing nitric acid. Also, while oxalate and sulfate were
strongly correlated in the submicrometer range pointing to
aqueous-phase formation, the oxalate-to-sulfate ratio was
higher post-lockdown and especially daytime due to reduced
sulfate precursors.
In contrast, there was an enhancement in water-soluble mass
post-lockdown in the supermicrometer range driven by
naturally higher sea salt levels (e.g., sodium and chloride).
Although the percentage of chloride depletion decreased post-
lockdown due to reduced acid levels, the absolute Cl−mass
loss increased, driven by higher sodium levels. Nitrate, calcium,
and magnesium showed minimal changes, reflecting their
stronger association with natural sources less affected by shifts
in human activity.
Collectively, the results highlight the importance of
considering both particle size and time of day when evaluating
aerosol composition and atmospheric reactivity, particularly in
regions with pronounced diurnal differences in human activity.
These findings also demonstrate how large-scale societal
disruptions, such as the COVID-19 pandemic, offer unique
opportunities to investigate the sensitivity of aerosol
composition to emission changes. Future studies with
concurrent measurements of precursor gases and higher time
resolution aerosol chemical composition are recommended to
better understand the mechanisms driving aerosol changes in
response to both emission reductions and meteorological
variability.
